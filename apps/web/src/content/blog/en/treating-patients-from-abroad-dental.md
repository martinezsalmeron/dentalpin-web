A patient walks in with implants fitted in another country three weeks ago and asks you to take over. Before anything else, understand what you are not inheriting: a health insurance card did not pay for that treatment, so there is no insurer file to request and no clinical correspondence waiting for you. Planned private treatment abroad is a private transaction, and the only record of it is whatever the patient was handed on the day. That is what you have to get hold of, and quickly, because the warranty conversation and the next restorative decision both depend on it.

This post is about the receiving end. If you are the practice treating the visiting patient, the obligations run the other way, and most of them are about the language the patient signs in.

## The card did not cover it, and that matters to you

Patients routinely believe their European Health Insurance Card or UK Global Health Insurance Card had something to do with the treatment. It did not.

The European Commission's own guidance is unambiguous: "Your EHIC does **not cover planned treatment** in another EU country", and separately, "Your EHIC does **not cover private healthcare.** You can only use it for healthcare from providers that are part of the public system". What it does cover is "any medical treatment that can't wait until you get home".

The NHS says the same thing about the UK card. A GHIC covers "medically necessary healthcare" that "cannot reasonably wait until you come back to the UK", and it does not cover "treatment in a private medical facility".

> **Why this is your problem and not just a billing curiosity.** If the patient thinks a public scheme was involved, they will also assume someone else holds their records, that a complaint has an official route, and that remedial work is covered. None of that is true, and the sooner they know it the sooner they will chase the clinic for the documents you need.

There is a reimbursement route for planned care inside the EU and EEA, under Directive 2011/24/EU, but it works the other way round: the patient pays the clinic, then claims from their own system afterwards. The Commission notes that reimbursement is "**either according to the reimbursement rates of your home country, or the rates of the country of treatment** - whichever is the **cheaper price**". Prior authorisation is "**not always necessary**", though "it may be required for interventions that include overnight stays, very serious or complex procedures, or procedures involving expensive medical equipment".

Two practical consequences for you. The claim is the patient's to make, not yours. And because the claim needs an itemised invoice, a patient who is pursuing one usually has better paperwork than one who is not: ask whether they claimed, because the answer tells you what exists.

## What to ask the patient for, in this order

Request these from the patient, not from the clinic. The patient has a right to their own record wherever they were treated, and a request from them gets answered faster than a request from a stranger in another country.

1. **The treatment report**, with dates. What was done, on which teeth, in how many visits.
2. **The dental chart** as it stood when they were discharged.
3. **Radiographs as image files**, not photographs of a screen. Ask for DICOM or at minimum full-resolution originals.
4. **Implant traceability**: the brand, reference and lot number of every fixture and abutment placed. Many systems record this as a sticker in the file.
5. **The itemised invoice**, which is often the only document that lists every procedure separately.
6. **Anything still outstanding**: the stage the plan stopped at, and when the next review was meant to be.

> **Without the implant reference you cannot restore it.** This is the single most common gap. An abutment is not generic, and a patient who loses one two years later needs the exact system. If nothing else arrives, get this, because radiographs alone will rarely identify the system reliably.

## What the clinic that treated them actually owed the patient

It helps to know what the patient was entitled to walk out with, because it tells you what to insist on.

The entitlement is set by the country where the treatment happened, and in the countries that most commonly treat visiting patients it is explicit. Spain's Real Decreto 81/2014, which transposes the Directive, gives patients who come to Spain for treatment, in article 6.3.a, "La disponibilidad de una copia, en el soporte adecuado, de los informes clínicos", a copy of the clinical reports on an appropriate medium. The same decree, in article 8.8, requires providers to keep "una lista actualizada de precios a disposición de los ciudadanos" and to ensure "información clara sobre facturas".

In Germany, the Civil Code reaches the same result from a different direction: § 630g(1) BGB gives the patient access to their complete treatment record and copies of it, and says "Die erste Abschrift wird unentgeltlich zur Verfügung gestellt", the first copy is provided free of charge.

So when a patient tells you the clinic refused to send anything, that is usually a service failure rather than a legal position, and saying so to the patient tends to unblock it.

## Documenting what you are taking on

This is where the receiving practice creates its own risk. You are about to work on something you did not place, on the basis of a report you cannot verify.

Write down, in the record, the three things a later dispute will turn on:

- **What you were given and what you were not.** "No implant reference supplied" is a clinical finding, and it belongs in the notes rather than in somebody's memory.
- **What you examined yourself** as against what you are accepting from the report. Your own radiographs and your own charting are the baseline from which any later change is measured.
- **What you told the patient about the limits.** Specifically, that you cannot warrant work you did not do, and that remedial treatment is a new plan with its own quote.

| What arrives | How to treat it | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Treatment report from the clinic | ~ Useful, not verified | It is their account, and you were not there |
| Your own radiographs and charting | ✓ Your baseline | The only record you can stand behind |
| Implant brand, reference and lot | ✓ Take as fact if documented | Traceable to a manufacturer |
| The patient's account of what was promised | ✗ Not a clinical record | Note it as reported, nothing more |

If the patient intends to complain or claim against the original clinic, the cross-border contact points are the route to explain it: every EU and EEA country runs a national contact point whose job includes telling patients what their rights are and what complaint and redress procedures exist. The patient contacts the one in their own country.

## If you are the practice treating the visiting patient

The obligations invert, and they are mostly about comprehension rather than paperwork volume. Two rules carry across every country that regulates this.

The information has to be understandable to this patient. German law puts it most plainly: § 630e(2) BGB requires that the explanation be "für den Patienten verständlich", understandable for the patient, and adds that copies of anything they signed must be handed to them. Italy's Legge 219/2017, article 1 comma 3, gives every person the right to be informed "in modo completo, aggiornato e a lei comprensibile". Spain's Ley 41/2002, article 4.2, requires that clinical information "se comunicará al paciente de forma comprensible y adecuada a sus necesidades".

And the consent, the health questionnaire, the quote and the post-operative instructions all need to exist in a language the patient reads, because post-operative instructions are the one document they will read alone, at home, with nobody to ask. Which version they signed, and in which language, has to be recoverable from the record afterwards: see [digital informed consent in dentistry](/en/blog/digital-informed-consent-dentistry/) for how to keep that auditable.

If your practice sees patients from abroad often enough that this is routine, the work is in having the templates per language and in the record keeping which one was signed. Dentalpin stores consents and quotes as per-language templates and keeps the signed version and date against each signature, and the price is published on [the pricing page](/en/pricing/).

This is not legal advice. Entitlements and retention periods are national, and a reimbursement claim is settled by the patient's own system rather than by either practice.
