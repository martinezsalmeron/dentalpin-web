A UK dental practice may advertise. What is regulated is the content: the GDC requires your advertising to be accurate and not misleading, to carry your GDC registration number, and to avoid any claim likely to create an unjustified expectation. Three rules catch most practices out. You must not imply specialist status unless you are on a GDC specialist list, you must not publish information comparing one dental professional's skills or qualifications with another's, and prescription-only medicines cannot be advertised to the public at all.

This is not legal advice. It is a reading of the official sources listed at the end, consulted on 7 October 2026, and it covers the UK. If you practise in the United States, your rules come from your state dental board and the FTC, which are not covered here.

## The standard the GDC enforces

Standard 1.3.3 of Standards for the Dental Team is one sentence: "You must make sure that any advertising, promotional material or other information that you produce is accurate and not misleading, and complies with the GDC's guidance on ethical advertising."

The guidance adds the reason, and it is worth keeping in mind when a headline is being drafted. Misleading claims "can raise expectations which cannot be fulfilled" and, in more serious cases, "can put patients at risk of harm from an inappropriate choice". Advertising that is false or misleading is unprofessional, may lead to a fitness to practise investigation, and can be a criminal offence.

## The six things you must do

Whenever anything carrying your name is produced, by you or by the practice, you are responsible for checking it. The guidance lists six duties.

1. **Ensure the information is current and accurate.**
2. **Include your GDC registration number.**
3. **Use clear language** that patients are likely to understand.
4. **Back up claims with facts.**
5. **Avoid ambiguous statements.**
6. **Avoid statements or claims** intended or likely to create an unjustified expectation about the results you can achieve.

There is a seventh that is easy to forget: you must make clear in advertisements and practice publicity whether the practice is NHS, mixed or wholly private.

> **"Back up claims with facts" is the whole job.** It is not a style note. It means the evidence has to exist before the claim is published, which is also what the ASA will ask for if the advert is challenged.

## What a practice website must display

The guidance is specific about websites, and this is the list most often incomplete.

- **The name and geographic address** at which the dental service is provided.
- **Contact details** for the service, including an email address and telephone number.
- **The GDC's address and other contact details**, or a link to the GDC website.
- **Details of the complaints procedure**, and who patients may contact if they are not satisfied with the response: the relevant NHS body for NHS treatment, and the Dental Complaints Service for private treatment.
- **The date the website was last updated**, kept current so it reflects the personnel and the services actually offered.

Where an individual is named as a dental professional providing care, two further items are required: their professional qualification with the country it derives from, and their GDC registration number.

> **One prohibition sits inside the website rules and surprises people.** You "must not display information comparing the skills or qualifications of any dental professional providing any service with the skills and qualifications of other dental professionals". That rules out the ranked team page and the "more experienced than other clinics locally" line.

## Specialist, specialising, special interest

This is the vocabulary rule, and the distinctions are exact.

| How the practice describes itself | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "Specialist in orthodontics", on a GDC specialist list | ✓ Permitted |
| "Orthodontist" or "Periodontist" without being on a list | ✗ Implies specialist status |
| "Specialising in implants" without being on a list | ✗ Not permitted |
| "Special interest in implants", "experienced in", "practice limited to" | ✓ Permitted alternatives |
| A hygienist or therapist as "Smile specialist" or "Denture specialist" | ✗ No specialist lists exist for DCPs |
| Memberships or honorary degrees in abbreviated form after a name | ✗ May mislead patients about qualifications |
| "Dr" used by a dentist | ~ Allowed, but must not imply a general medical qualification |

Two related duties round this off. If you want to offer services your primary training does not qualify you for, you must undertake appropriate additional training and must not let the primary qualification imply competence you do not have. And if you endorse a product, you must provide only factual information that can be verified by evidence, and be careful not to imply that the whole profession shares your view.

## What the ASA will ask for

Advertising also sits under the CAP Code, and the ASA's advice for the dental sector is narrower than the GDC's in some places.

- **Evidence before publication.** Objective claims about whitening, braces and product performance must be substantiated, with clinical trial evidence where the claim is a clinical one.
- **No digital manipulation** of whitening images, and no exaggeration of results.
- **Before and after photographs** must be genuine and representative, and the advertiser must hold signed and dated proof.
- **Prescription-only medicines cannot be advertised to the public**, which includes botulinum toxin products offered in a dental setting.
- **Health professionals and celebrities must not endorse medicines**, under CAP rule 12.18.
- **Offers on marketing and social platforms** need the GDC caveat: make clear that the treatment advertised may not be appropriate for every patient and is conditional on a satisfactory assessment, which still means history, examination, options and consent before any work.

## Where software helps and where it does not

No practice software stops a non-compliant advert going out. What it settles is the problem that follows: showing what the price covered on the day it was advertised, and what the patient was told it covered.
