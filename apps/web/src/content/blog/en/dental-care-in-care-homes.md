Three things need settling before the first visit to a care home, and none of them is clinical: a written agreement with the home saying what gets done and how often, a resident by resident answer to who can consent, and a way to record the visit that does not depend on the home's wifi. Equipment can be improvised on the day. Those three cannot.

This is not legal advice. It is a reading of the official sources listed at the end, consulted on 25 October 2026. The capacity law described here is the law of England and Wales.

## The agreement with the home decides everything else

A home that rings because twenty residents have not been seen wants a solution, not a proposal. The temptation is to go next Thursday and sort the paperwork afterwards, and it works the other way round: the agreement is what fixes what happens, where, with what, and who is responsible for which part.

It does not need to be long. It needs decisions written down, because each one of them becomes an argument on the day it is missing.

| Point | What should be in writing | Essential? |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Assessment and prevention only, or treatment on site too | ✓ Yes |
| Frequency | Dates or an interval, not "when needed" | ✓ Yes |
| Room and equipment | Which room, couch or chair, lighting, power, a sink | ✓ Yes |
| Escorting | Which member of home staff brings residents to the room | ✓ Yes |
| Consent | Who gathers the capacity and authority paperwork before the visit | ✓ Yes |
| Records | What stays with the home and what goes back to the practice | ✓ Yes |
| Billing | Who is invoiced for what, and when | ✓ Yes |
| Between visits | What happens to a problem on a Tuesday, and which number to ring | ~ Worth having |
| Term and exit | How long it runs, notice, and what happens to data at the end | ~ Worth having |

> **The clause that saves the most grief is the escorting one.** A three hour visit where nobody has been tasked with bringing residents down becomes a five hour visit, and the practice pays for the other two.

NICE puts the home's side of this in writing. Its guideline on oral health for adults in care homes asks managers to set out, in the home's own policy, the local general dental services, the community dental and special care teams, how residents' oral health is assessed and referred, and what happens if a resident refuses mouth care, in line with the Mental Capacity Act. A home that already has that policy is a much easier home to work with, and asking for it is a fair first question.

## The home cannot consent on a resident's behalf

This is the commonest and the most expensive mistake. The person who runs the home is not, by running it, anybody's legal representative. Physical custody of a resident and control of their day do not amount to authority to agree to an extraction.

In England and Wales the Mental Capacity Act 2005 starts from the opposite end. Section 1 requires that "a person must be assumed to have capacity unless it is established that he lacks capacity", that nobody is treated as unable to decide "unless all practicable steps to help him to do so have been taken without success", and that nobody is treated as unable to decide "merely because he makes an unwise decision".

> **Section 5 is what protects the clinician, and it is conditional.** The act is covered if, before doing it, you take reasonable steps to establish whether the person lacks capacity, and when doing it you reasonably believe both that they lack capacity and that it is in their best interests. Nothing in it excludes liability for negligence.

Read in a corridor, that means the capacity assessment and the best interests reasoning are yours, decision by decision, and they are the thing that has to appear in the record. Where somebody holds a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare, or a court appointed deputy is involved, you need to see the document rather than be told about it. What to look at and what to write down afterwards is covered in [consent for adults who lack capacity](/en/blog/consent-adults-lacking-capacity-dental/), which is a different question from this page.

NHS England's special care dentistry clinical standard is worth knowing here for a second reason: it lists residential and care home staff among the normal routes a referral reaches a general dental practice. The call from a home is a referral pathway, not an irregularity.

## A domiciliary session is not a long appointment

The scheduling mistake is treating the visit as one big slot. A visit has travel, setting up, a number of residents you do not know until you arrive, and a journey back, and all of it occupies someone who is then unavailable for anything else that day.

![Practice schedule in day view, with appointments laid out by time and by surgery](/screenshots/schedule-day.png)

*The schedule in day view, appointments laid out by hour and by surgery. A care home visit is the block that leaves no room behind it.*

What works is blocking the whole thing out and making it visible to the team:

1. **Block the travel, not just the visit.** Out, set up, see residents, pack down, back. If the block says two hours and the day takes four, reception will keep booking over the other two.
2. **Cap the residents per visit and hold the cap.** Telling the home that the first round is eight and there will be a second date beats promising twenty and doing fourteen badly.
3. **Decide what happens to the empty surgery.** A chair nobody has been moved into is pure cost.
4. **Give reception a rule for the phone that day.** Who decides whether an urgent case waits, and until when.
5. **Book the next date before you leave the building.** It is the one moment in the year when the home's manager is in front of you.

## Records get written off site

This is the part that catches people out. In the practice the recording happens by itself, because the computer is there. In a home there is a corridor, a trolley, a borrowed room and a connection that is not yours, and what usually results is paper notes typed up three days later with half of it forgotten.

> **What is not written in the building gets written badly.** Half an hour of transcription back at the practice produces a thinner record than ten minutes beside the resident, and the paper in between is health data travelling about in a folder.

Three concrete things need solving before the first visit. The device you record on has to work without a connection, because a care home's visitor wifi is not a plan; the whole question is in [working without internet](/en/blog/dental-software-offline/). It has to be encrypted and screen locked, because it leaves the building. And the list of residents you are seeing needs loading before you go, so that nothing depends on searching for a record on site.

## What stays with the home is not the clinical record

Two documents get confused constantly, and mixing them creates a data problem where there was not one.

- **The clinical record is yours and stays with the practice.** It is health records for your patient, and the home does not need access to it to look after anybody.
- **The mouth care plan belongs to the home and has to stay there.** What daily care each resident needs, with which products, how often, whether they can manage it themselves or need help, and what to report if something changes.
- **The visit report goes to the resident or whoever holds authority for them.** Findings, what was done, what is proposed, and the estimate if there is one.

That split is not a preference of ours. Germany writes it into regulation: the Gemeinsamer Bundesausschuss directive on preventing dental disease in people needing care requires the dentist to produce an individual oral health plan stating, resident by resident, whether each measure is done by the person themselves, with help from a carer, or entirely by the carer. It is the same distinction turned into a form.

NICE also gives you one small thing to get right at the lab rather than in the home: dentures made for a resident should be marked by the lab during manufacture. An unmarked denture in a building with eighty residents is a denture that goes missing.

## After the visit, the same day

1. **Close each record before you leave, or that evening.** Findings, what was done, what is proposed, and who consented to what, with the capacity reasoning.
2. **Hand the mouth care plan to the home's nursing lead**, signed and dated, and keep evidence that you did.
3. **Send reports and estimates to whoever holds authority for each resident**, not to the home as a single addressee.
4. **Invoice as the agreement says** and check the items match what you recorded.
5. **Write down what could not be done and why.** That is the next visit's worklist, and the only way the second visit is shorter than the first.

## Where software helps and where it does not

No system negotiates an agreement or finds out who holds authority for a resident: those are phone calls and documents. What it can take off you is the part that makes a domiciliary record worse than an ordinary one.

In [Dentalpin](/en/pricing/) the record and the schedule run in the browser on the laptop or tablet you took with you, so the visit is written beside the resident rather than on a sheet you type up later, and the day view shows the whole block including travel. What it will not do is decide whether the extraction is authorised: that is reading the paperwork with section 5 in mind.
