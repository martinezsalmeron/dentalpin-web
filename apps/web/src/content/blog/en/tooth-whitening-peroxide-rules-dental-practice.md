A tooth whitening product containing or releasing more than 0.1% and up to 6% hydrogen peroxide can only be sold to dental practitioners, the first use in each cycle has to be carried out by a dental practitioner or under their direct supervision, and it cannot be used on anyone under 18. Above 6% the product is no longer a lawful cosmetic at all. Those rules sit in one entry of the cosmetics regulation, and in the UK breaching them is a criminal offence prosecuted by Trading Standards rather than by the GDC.

> **This is not legal advice.** What follows is entry 12 of Annex III to Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 and the General Dental Council's own position statement on it, with the date each was consulted. This article is written for the UK; see the note on scope at the end.

## The three numbers that decide everything

Whitening regulation turns on three figures: 0.1, 6 and 18. The first separates the open shelf from professional use, the second separates a lawful cosmetic from one that is not, and the third is a flat prohibition in the middle band.

| | ≤ 0.1% | > 0.1% and ≤ 6% | > 6% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Available directly to the consumer | ✓ Yes | ✗ No, sold only to dental practitioners | ✗ No |
| First use in each cycle | No requirement in the entry | ✗ Dental practitioner, or under direct supervision | Not applicable |
| Use on a person under 18 | No restriction in the entry | ✗ Prohibited | ✗ Prohibited |
| Concentration shown on the label | Not required by the entry | ✓ Required, as a percentage | Not applicable |
| Lawful as a cosmetic | ✓ Yes | ✓ Yes | ✗ Prohibited for cosmetic purposes |

The figure that counts is hydrogen peroxide **present or released**, not the number on the front of the syringe. That is what pulls carbamide peroxide and zinc peroxide into the same entry.

## What the entry actually says

Annex III, entry 12 sets the professional band out in four short conditions. On the UK text published by legislation.gov.uk they read:

> **"To be only sold to dental practitioners."** Then: "For each cycle of use, first use by dental practitioners as defined under Directive 2005/36/EC or under their direct supervision if an equivalent level of safety is ensured. Afterwards to be provided to the consumer to complete the cycle of use. Not to be used on a person under 18 years of age."

The same entry requires the concentration of hydrogen peroxide present or released to be shown on the label as a percentage, and carries the general warning block for peroxide products: contains hydrogen peroxide, avoid contact with eyes, rinse immediately on contact.

In the UK this arrives through the Cosmetic Products Enforcement Regulations 2013, which replaced the 2012 amendment regulations that had implemented Directive 2011/84/EU. The GDC's position statement records that history and adds the part that matters to a practice: "Breach of these regulations is a criminal offence."

## The 6% ceiling is a ceiling on cosmetics, not on dentistry

This is the most commonly muddled point in the whole subject, and clearing it up changes how everything above reads.

The 6% figure does not say that no practice may ever use a stronger gel. It says a cosmetic product may not exceed it. The GDC puts the exception in writing: concentrations above 6% "remain prohibited unless wholly for the purpose of the treatment or prevention of disease".

> **The same exception applies to the age limit.** The GDC's statement is explicit that products in the 0.1% to 6% band "cannot be used on any person under 18 years of age, except where such use is intended wholly for the purpose of treating or preventing disease." Cosmetic whitening of a sixteen year old is out. Treating a discoloured tooth as disease is a different question, and it is a clinical judgement you should be able to defend from the record.

So the practical test is about purpose and about where the product goes. If it is handed to the patient to finish the cycle at home for cosmetic reasons, it is in the 6% band and carries all three obligations. If it never leaves the surgery, check what the product is actually authorised as before assuming the entry covers it.

## Carbamide peroxide: convert before you read the label

Plenty of products are marketed in carbamide peroxide, where the number looks far higher than the peroxide it actually releases. The Council of European Dentists publishes the conversion in its guidelines on the directive:

The active ingredient of carbamide peroxide is hydrogen peroxide, and 10% carbamide peroxide corresponds to 3.6% hydrogen peroxide. On that ratio the 6% hydrogen peroxide ceiling is reached at roughly 16.6% carbamide peroxide.

The same document flags something easy to miss. Sodium perborate and perboric acid count as hydrogen peroxide releasing substances, following the Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety opinion of 22 June 2010. A product advertised as peroxide free but built on perborate is still inside the entry.

## Who may carry out the first use

The regulation leaves a deliberate gap here, and it is the part each country fills in. It allows the first use to be by a dental practitioner "or under their direct supervision if an equivalent level of safety is ensured", and never defines that equivalent level.

The UK has filled it in, and more widely than the bare text suggests. The GDC's statement says these products "should not be made directly available to the consumer, other than through treatment by a registered dentist, dental hygienist, dental therapist or clinical dental technicians working to a dentist's prescription."

Two consequences worth stating plainly:

- **Registration is the line, not employment.** The GDC's position on anyone outside the register is unchanged: tooth whitening "falls within the definitions of practice of dentistry under sections 37 and 38 of the Dentists Act", and the GDC prosecutes unregistered people who do it. A beauty salon offering whitening is committing an offence, whatever the gel strength.
- **Two regulators, two regimes.** The GDC does not enforce the peroxide limits, as it says itself: that is Trading Standards and the Health and Safety Executive. But it treats a criminal offence as relevant to fitness to practise, so a registrant using above 6% for cosmetic purposes can expect both.

## The clinical examination comes first

The condition that is almost never quoted is the one justifying all the rest. The scientific opinion the rule rests on accepted the 0.1% to 6% band on two conditions, and the GDC reproduces both: "an appropriate clinical examination is to be carried out in order to ensure that there are no risk factors or any other oral pathology concerns", and exposure "should be limited to ensure that the products are only used as intended in terms of frequency and duration of application".

The European guidelines pin down the timing: the clinical examination has to happen before the first use of the whitening product, and the dentist controls the exposure through the rest of the cycle. The examination is not paperwork accompanying the sale of a kit. It is what makes the sale lawful.

## What has to end up in the record

Five facts follow from the obligations above. Either they are written down or they cannot be shown afterwards, and none of them is optional if you want to reconstruct the case two years later.

1. **The patient's age on the date of treatment**, not just a date of birth. The under-18 prohibition is breached by a birthday, and the check has to be against the day of the first application.
2. **The exact concentration of hydrogen peroxide present or released**, as a percentage, as the label declares it. If the product is sold in carbamide peroxide, record both numbers rather than the one on the box.
3. **The batch number and expiry.** This is what lets you answer a recall or a safety alert later, and it is the detail most often lost, because it lives on packaging that gets thrown away.
4. **The date of the first application and who carried it out.** That is the fact the regulation turns on, so it is the one you need to be able to evidence by name and date.
5. **The clinical examination and what it ruled out.** Active caries, periodontal disease, leaking margins, existing sensitivity: what was looked for and what was found, because it is the condition the legality of everything else rests on.

Two decisions made in the same appointment sit outside this entry. Consent for a cosmetic procedure runs on its own track, covered in the [guide to digital informed consent](/en/blog/digital-informed-consent-dentistry/), and where the patient is a minor the question is wider than the age limit, covered in the [guide to minor patients and guardians](/en/blog/minor-patients-guardians-dental/). How the treatment may be advertised is a separate and stricter regime again, in the [guide to dental practice advertising rules](/en/blog/dental-practice-advertising-rules/).

One note on scope, so this article does not claim more than its sources do. It describes the UK. The peroxide entry is identical across the European Union, because the cosmetics regulation applies directly in every member state, but who may act under direct supervision is a national decision and the GDC's wording above is the UK answer, not a European one. Outside the UK and the EU, including the United States, whitening is regulated differently and who may perform it is generally a matter for the state or national dental regulator. No US rule is stated here, because none could be sourced from an official text this run: ask your state dental board.
