A first appointment has to produce two things: a patient who understands what is going on and what you are proposing, and a record another clinician could pick up in two years without phoning you. The order that delivers both is always the same: presenting complaint in the patient's own words, medical history, extraoral exam, soft tissues, periodontal screening, charting, and radiographs only where the examination justifies them. Anything not written down that day does not exist afterwards.

The usual failure is not examining too little. It is examining properly and then recording it in three lines, with no date on the history, no negative findings and no note of what the patient was told.

## Seven blocks, in this order

The order is not a preference. Each block changes what you are looking for in the next one, and skipping one means going back with the patient already in the chair.

1. **Presenting complaint, in their words.** "It hurts when I bite on the left, since Tuesday" records more than "pain". The literal phrase drives the differential, and if there is ever a complaint it shows why you examined what you examined.
2. **Medical and drug history.** Conditions, current medication, allergies, pregnancy or breastfeeding, previous treatment. This is the block that constrains everything else, including the decision not to treat today.
3. **Extraoral examination.** Face, neck, lymph nodes, temporomandibular joint, opening, tenderness on palpation.
4. **Soft tissue examination.** Mucosa, tongue, floor of mouth, palate, oropharynx. It is the part most often skipped when someone attends with a specific emergency, and the hardest to recover later.
5. **Periodontal screening.** Systematic, using whatever system the practice uses, recorded by sextant or by tooth, not as "gums fine".
6. **Charting.** Tooth by tooth: missing teeth, existing restorations, caries, mobility, root fillings, prostheses. This is the document another clinician reads first.
7. **Radiographs, where justified by the examination.** Record the justification the same way you record the finding: why that image was taken and what it showed.

## The medical history is the part that gets copied badly

Almost every record has a medical history. Far fewer have one you can use, because the parts that make it usable are missing: the date, who took it, and the negatives.

- **Date and author.** A history with no date does not tell you whether the patient was on an anticoagulant in 2024 or is on one today. Before a surgical procedure that difference is everything.
- **Negatives, written out.** "Denies drug allergies" is not the same as an empty field. An empty field reads as "nobody asked", and that is how it will be read two years from now.
- **Medication by name.** Anticoagulants, antiplatelets, bisphosphonates and other antiresorptives, immunosuppressants, and drugs stopped recently. "Something for the heart" is not clinical data.
- **A rule for updating it.** A history is not taken once. Review it and re-date it at every new phase of treatment, and always before surgery.

> **An empty field does not mean there is nothing there.** It means nobody knows whether the question was asked. Recording negatives is the cheapest thing you can do at a first visit and the most valuable when someone reviews the record years later.

## What the record has to contain

In the UK the standard is set by the General Dental Council. Standard 4.1 of *Standards for the Dental Team* is one sentence: "You must make and keep contemporaneous, complete and accurate patient records."

The sub-points spell out what that means in practice, and they map onto a first visit almost exactly.

- **4.1.1** puts medical histories, radiographs, consent forms, photographs, models and referral letters inside the record, not alongside it.
- **4.1.2** asks for the detail of discussions with the patient and evidence that valid consent was obtained.
- **4.1.4** requires records that are clear, legible and accurate, and that can be readily understood by others, with the name or initials of the clinician who treated the patient.
- **4.1.5** requires any amendment to be clearly marked and dated, so it is visible that a change was made.

That last one is worth reading twice. A record you can silently edit later is worth less than one you cannot, which is a software question as much as a clinical one.

Requirements differ by jurisdiction: retention periods, in particular, are set nationally rather than by the GDC, and practices outside the UK should check their own regulator. This is not legal advice.

## What you record on day one and what you update later

| Block | First visit | Recall visits |
|---|---|---|
| Presenting complaint, verbatim | ✓ Always | ✓ Whenever it changes |
| Full medical history | ✓ Always | ~ Review and re-date |
| Soft tissue examination | ✓ Always | ✓ Always |
| Periodontal screening | ✓ Always | ~ According to patient risk |
| Full chart | ✓ Always | ~ Only what changed |
| Radiographs | ~ Only where justified | ~ Only where justified |
| Consent for the proposed treatment | ~ When treatment is proposed | ✓ Before each new phase |
| Written treatment plan and estimate | ~ When there is a plan | ✓ If the plan changes |

The right-hand column is usually the one set up badly. Repeating the full chart every six months adds nothing and guarantees nobody reads it. Never revisiting the medical history, on the other hand, is a real risk.

## Periodontal screening is the least recorded block

A periodontal screening with no numbers written down has to be repeated from scratch at the next visit, and it makes the only question that matters in periodontics unanswerable: is this getting better or worse?

Recording it by sextant is already enough to compare. Where the case calls for it, a full six-point chart is what turns "the gums are a bit inflamed" into a trend you can show the patient.

## What you said also goes in the record

The conversation is part of the first visit, and it is the part that disappears first. Three things deserve a line of their own:

- **What the patient was told.** Diagnosis, options discussed, and what happens if nothing is done. Two sentences is enough.
- **What they decided.** Accept, think about it, ask for a written estimate, treat the emergency only.
- **What they were given.** Estimate, consent form, post-operative instructions, a receipt. With the date.

> **A first visit either defends itself or it does not.** Two years on nobody remembers the conversation, and all that is left is what was written that day.

## How to set it up this week

1. **Pull your last ten new patient exams** and check how many have a dated medical history, a periodontal screening with numbers, and a verbatim presenting complaint.
2. **Write a first visit template** with the seven blocks in order, so the record comes from filling it in rather than from memory.
3. **Decide which fields are mandatory**, the ones that stop the visit being closed: allergies, medication, pregnancy or breastfeeding.
4. **Decide who takes the history** and at what point, because if it depends on how busy the day is it gets done halfway.
5. **Add an update prompt** before any surgical procedure.
6. **Check again in a month** against ten more records, to see whether the template is being used or worked around.
