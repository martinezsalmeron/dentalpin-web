In the UK the General Dental Council's position is short and it surprises people: "The administration of Botox for cosmetic purposes is not the practice of dentistry." That does not make it unlawful for a dentist to do it, but it does mean the act sits outside dentistry, with a prescribing requirement attached, while injectable dermal fillers follow an entirely different rule because they are medical devices rather than medicines. In England, giving either to anyone under 18 for a cosmetic purpose is a criminal offence. In the United States none of this is federal: your state dental board decides.

This is not legal advice. It is a reading of the official sources listed at the end, consulted on 23 October 2026, and for a US practice the only authoritative answer is your own state board's.

## Botox: a prescription-only medicine, and not dentistry

The GDC's direct access guidance draws the line in one sentence, and then adds the consequence.

Botox "cannot be advertised to the public as it is a prescription-only medicine (POM)", and "it needs to be prescribed by a registered doctor or dentist who has completed a full assessment of the patient".

Read those two together with the first sentence of this post and the structure becomes clear. A dentist may be the prescriber, because a registered dentist can prescribe. But the GDC does not treat the cosmetic administration as part of dentistry, which is why the usual comfort of "I am registered, therefore I am covered" does not apply here.

> **The remote prescription is the common failure.** The guidance requires a full assessment of the patient by the prescriber. A prescription signed by someone who never examined the person in the chair does not meet that, however routine the arrangement has become in parts of the market.

## Fillers follow device law, not medicines law

This is the distinction that most training courses blur, and it changes almost everything about how the two procedures are governed.

"Injectable dermal fillers are classed as medical devices and so do not require a prescription." There is no prescriber in the chain at all.

What replaces it is a competence and indemnity test. Anyone offering them "must be sure that they are trained, competent, and indemnified or insured to do so".

So fillers are easier to obtain and harder to defend. No prescription gate stands between the practitioner and the product, which means the only thing standing between a complication and an uninsured claim is whether the indemnity actually covers facial aesthetics. That is a clause to read before the first appointment, not after the first problem.

## Under-18s in England: this one is criminal law

The Botulinum Toxin and Cosmetic Fillers (Children) Act 2021 made this a criminal matter rather than a regulatory one, and it came into force on 1 October 2021.

Section 1 makes it an offence to give botulinum toxin, or a subcutaneous, submucous or intradermal filler injection for a cosmetic purpose, to someone under 18 in England. A filler injection counts as cosmetic if the filler is generally used for that purpose or the likely effect is to alter the person's appearance.

Two defences matter to a dental practice.

1. **Being a registered medical practitioner, or a regulated health professional acting on a registered medical practitioner's directions.** A dentist falls into the second category, not the first. For a patient under 18, that means acting on a doctor's directions.
2. **Having taken reasonable steps to establish the person's age** and reasonably believing them to be 18 or over.

Section 2 extends liability to the business if it lets a non-approved person carry out these procedures on under-18s or arranges for one to do so, with a defence of all reasonable precautions and all due diligence. Section 3 reaches directors and managers personally where a body corporate offends with their consent, connivance or neglect. Section 4 puts enforcement with local weights and measures authorities.

> **Age verification stops being administrative and becomes evidential.** A defence built on "reasonable steps" needs a record of what those steps were. A date of birth in the file, captured before treatment, is that record.

## The regulatory gap is acknowledged, not hypothetical

It is worth knowing that the regulator itself says this area is not fully covered.

In its response of 30 October 2023 to the Department of Health and Social Care consultation on licensing non-surgical cosmetic procedures, the GDC said it supports the intention of a clear regulatory framework, "to reduce risks to patient safety and address the current gaps in regulation which may arise when GDC registrants deliver procedures that are not technically dentistry".

That phrase, procedures that are not technically dentistry, is the whole problem in six words. The same response records that increasing numbers of dental professionals are performing non-surgical cosmetic procedures "such as botulinum toxin and dermal filler injections", and devotes a section to what it calls the lack of recognition of legal restrictions around "the practice of dentistry" in the proposed framework.

A licensing scheme being consulted on is a scheme not yet settled, so anyone building a service line here should expect the rules to tighten rather than loosen.

## What applies where

| Question | Botulinum toxin | Injectable dermal fillers |
|---|---|---|
| Is it the practice of dentistry, per the GDC? | ✗ Not for cosmetic purposes | ~ Not stated either way |
| Prescription required? | ✓ By a registered doctor or dentist | ✗ Classed as a medical device |
| Full patient assessment by the prescriber? | ✓ Required | ~ No prescriber in the chain |
| Can it be advertised to the public? | ✗ Prohibited as a POM | ~ Subject to general advertising rules |
| Training, competence and indemnity needed? | ✓ | ✓ |
| Lawful for an under-18 in England? | ✗ Offence, with limited defences | ✗ Offence, with limited defences |

The second column is not a softer version of the first. Fillers remove the prescription gate without removing the risk, and the law treats under-18s identically either way.

## In the United States, the answer is a state answer

There is no single national rule, and any article that offers one is describing a particular state without saying so.

Scope of practice for dentists is set state by state, by each state's dental practice act and its board of dentistry. Some states have addressed botulinum toxin and dermal fillers for dentists explicitly, others have not, and the conditions attached differ where they exist.

The practical consequence is simple. Before offering either procedure, get your own board's current position in writing, and get your malpractice carrier's confirmation that facial aesthetics is covered under your policy. Neither question can be answered from a continuing-education brochure, and neither can be answered from this post.

## What to have on file before the first injection

1. **Date of birth, recorded before treatment.** In England this is the evidential basis of the age defence, not a courtesy field.
2. **The prescriber and the assessment, for botulinum toxin.** Who prescribed, and the record of the full patient assessment that preceded it.
3. **Training and competence, documented.** Certificate with hours and provider, filed and findable.
4. **Written confirmation of indemnity.** That the policy expressly covers facial aesthetic procedures. Dental indemnity does not always.
5. **Procedure-specific consent.** Not the practice's general form. The workflow is covered in [digital informed consent in dentistry](/en/blog/digital-informed-consent-dentistry/).
6. **Product batch and expiry.** Same reasoning as [implant lot tracking](/en/blog/implant-lot-tracking/), tied to the patient.

If you intend to promote the service, [dental practice advertising rules](/en/blog/dental-practice-advertising-rules/) apply, and the prescription-only status of botulinum toxin forecloses advertising it to the public outright.

## Where software helps and where it does not

No practice management system tells you whether a procedure falls inside your scope of practice. That is a reading of the GDC's guidance, or a question for your state board, and in a hard case a question for your indemnifier.

What software does solve is the part that gets lost: proving two years later what the indication was, which product and batch were used, that the patient's age was captured before treatment, and when consent was given.
