A short-notice list only fills chair time when it is a real list with real fields, not a vague promise to ring around. For every patient record the treatment they are waiting for, how long it takes, which provider they need, which days and times they can come, how much notice they need and how they want to be contacted. When a slot opens, filter by length and availability, message everyone who fits at once, and give the appointment to the first person who confirms.

Two things decide whether it works: how quickly the message goes out, and whether the list knows how long each person needs. What follows is how to build it, who to offer the slot to first, and how to tell whether it is paying off.

## A cancellation is not a no-show, and only one of them is fillable

The schedule draws them the same way, but they are different problems. A no-show leaves the chair empty with no warning and no room to react. A cancellation, even two hours out, leaves a window to put somebody else in it, and that window is all a short-notice list needs.

The split between the two is worth looking at. In an analysis of 15,193 dental appointments taken from electronic dental records, published in *Cureus* in 2023, 16.70% were cancelled by the patient and 9.14% were missed with no notice.

> **The cancelled share was nearly double the share lost without warning.** That study is a dental school, not a private practice, so the absolute number is not yours. What does carry over is the shape: most lost chair time arrives announced, and announced means fillable.

Before you build anything, pull those two figures separately for your own last quarter. If cancellations outweigh no-shows in your practice, a short-notice list is the highest-return fix available to you.

## What the list has to record, beyond a name and a phone number

A list of names forces you to call one person at a time and ask the same questions on every call. A list with fields filters in ten seconds and leaves the message ready to send.

| Field | What it does | If it is missing |
|---|---|---|
| Pending treatment and its length | Filters by the size of the slot that opened | ✗ You message people who do not fit in the forty-five minutes you have |
| Provider or operatory needed | Tells you whether this slot suits this patient at all | ✗ You confirm an appointment you then have to undo |
| Days and times they can attend | Rules out people who can never come in the morning | ~ You spend the first hour on messages that were never going to land |
| Minimum notice they need | Separates who can come today from who needs two days | ✗ You treat a gap tomorrow like a gap next week |
| Preferred contact channel | Reaches people where they actually reply | ~ The message arrives and nobody reads it in time |
| Clinical urgency | Orders the list by need rather than by seniority | ✗ Someone in pain waits behind a check-up |
| Date added to the list | Surfaces the person who has been waiting for months | ~ The list ages and nobody notices |

- **Keep it where the schedule lives**, not in a notebook at the front desk or on one person's phone. A list only the person who wrote it can read stops existing on their day off.
- **Record the length in minutes**, not just the treatment name. The filter you actually reach for when a slot drops is "who fits in this gap".
- **Ask about notice when you add someone**, in those words: "would a same-day call work for you?". That answer is what separates a useful list from a long one.

## Clinical need first, fit second

The order of the list is not the order people joined it. Anyone with a clinical need that should not wait comes first, and only among the rest does fit with the slot decide.

NHS England publishes a reference framework for unscheduled dental care, last updated on 3 October 2025, that separates three levels: emergency, with clinical triage within 60 minutes; urgent care, within 24 hours or as soon as practically possible; and non-urgent, within 7 days. It is written for the English NHS rather than for private practice, but the criteria it uses to sort patients travel anywhere.

Those criteria are the extent of pain, swelling, immediate or long-term loss of function, how long the symptoms have lasted and how they respond to appropriate medication.

1. **An urgent case does not go on the waiting list**, it gets seen. The list is for what can be planned.
2. **Order everyone else by clinical need**, judged by someone qualified to judge it, not by who called first.
3. **Between similar needs, fit wins**: treatment length against slot length.
4. **All else equal, the longest wait wins.** That is why the date added is a field in the table above.

## The first hour decides whether the slot gets filled

When a cancellation lands, the clock is running. The closer the gap, the fewer people can rearrange their day around it, so the protocol has to be written down and short.

1. **Measure the real gap**, turnaround and surgery cleaning included. If a patient drops out at nine and the next appointment starts at ten, you have fifty usable minutes, not sixty.
2. **Filter the list** by length, provider and availability in that window. You should be left with a handful of names, and those are the people you message.
3. **Message everyone who fits at the same time**, not one after another. Calling in sequence spends the little time you have on three conversations that do not land.
4. **Give the slot to the first person who confirms**, and say so in the message, so nobody turns up believing it was held for them.
5. **Close the loop with the others quickly.** A "that one has gone, you are still on the list" heads off the late reply and keeps the list alive.
6. **If it is still empty after half an hour, change plan**: pull forward a patient already booked later that week, or turn the gap into internal work that was waiting.
7. **Log what happened**, even when the slot stayed empty. Without that record there is no way to know whether the list works.

> **An empty slot is not recovered later.** A chair standing idle at ten in the morning is not made up by working harder on Thursday, because Thursday already had its hours. That is why the criterion is speed rather than a tidy process.

## What the message says

A message that works can be answered with one word. A message that fails makes the patient ring you to find out what is on offer.

- **Exact date, time and length.** "Tomorrow at 10:15, forty-five minutes" can be decided on sight; "we have had a cancellation" cannot.
- **Which provider and which treatment.** The patient has to recognise their own treatment in the sentence.
- **A one-tap way to say yes**, by replying to the same message. Every extra step loses people.
- **An explicit deadline**: "if we have not heard by 6pm we will offer it to someone else". It is honest and it speeds up the answer.
- **One message per slot, and only to people who fit.** Blasting the whole list about everything trains patients to ignore you, which is the opposite of what you need.

## Have fewer gaps to fill in the first place

The list is the safety net, not the fix. It is worth asking why appointments get cancelled at all.

In a 2018 paediatric dentistry study of 294 participants, the reasons patients gave for cancelling were being unwell (27.9%), a parent's work commitments (15.0%), problems with scheduling the appointment (12.2%) and long waits at the clinic (10.5%). Small sample, specific population, but two of those four reasons are the practice's to fix, not the patient's.

- **Rebook at the moment of cancelling**, on the same call or in the same thread. That is the most willing the patient will ever be.
- **Confirm far enough ahead** that a cancellation reaches you with room to act, not two hours out. A late confirmation turns cancellations into no-shows.
- **Shorten the lead time between booking and appointment**, especially for the appointment types that fall through most. The further away the date, the more life happens in between.
- **Watch your waiting-room times.** It shows up in the reasons list and it is one of the few you can fix from the inside.
- **Ask the reason and write it down.** Four weeks of recorded reasons beats any assumption about why people drop out.

## A list nobody prunes stops being useful

Short-notice lists die by accumulation. They fill up with people who have already been treated, changed phone number or solved the problem elsewhere, and by the time a slot opens nobody trusts what is written there.

- **Take the patient off as soon as they are booked.** Otherwise the next message goes to someone who already has a date.
- **Give every entry an expiry**, three months is reasonable, and check in before you remove it.
- **Review it once a week**, five minutes, looking first at whoever has waited longest.
- **Keep the minimum you need to make the offer.** It is a list of patient data, so do not load it with more clinical detail than deciding "does this person fit this gap" requires.
- **Respect the channel each person chose.** Someone who asked not to be called at work meant this too.

## Measure it with two numbers

| Metric | How to calculate it | What it should change |
|---|---|---|
| Fill rate | Slots filled divided by slots freed, monthly | If it is low, the list is too short or missing fields |
| Minutes recovered | Total length of the slots you filled | Turns the list into chair time, the unit everybody understands |
| Time to fill | Minutes between the cancellation and the confirmation | If it rises, the message is going out late or one at a time |
| People messaged per slot | Average number contacted per gap | If it is one, you are not messaging in parallel |

With three months of fill rate and minutes recovered you can settle whether the process works without anyone having to offer an opinion.

## Where to start

1. **Pull cancellations and no-shows separately** for the last three months.
2. **Create the list with the seven fields above**, in the same place the schedule lives.
3. **Seed it by asking patients who already hold a distant appointment** whether they would take an earlier one. It is the fastest way to have a useful list on day one.
4. **Write the seven-step protocol down** and keep it visible at the front desk.
5. **Prepare the message template** with date, time, length, provider and reply deadline.
6. **Log every freed slot and what happened to it** for a month.
7. **Look at the fill rate after that month** and fix whichever part is failing: the fields, the speed of the message or its wording.
