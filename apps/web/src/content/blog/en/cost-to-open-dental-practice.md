There is no trustworthy average for what it costs to open a dental practice, and it is worth knowing that before you go looking for one. The only sector study published with a stated method and sample is German: the *InvestMonitor Zahnarztpraxis*, produced by the Institute of German Dentists with apoBank, which for 2024 put the mean investment in a newly founded single practice at EUR 853,000 and the median at EUR 690,000.

The distance between those two numbers is the real answer. There is no single figure, there is a very wide spread. What does travel between markets is the proportion between the line items and the list of things that fall outside the budget.

## Where the numbers you will find come from

In the markets we checked for this piece, Spain, France, Italy, Portugal, Poland and the English-speaking world, no professional body or public institution publishes statistics on the capital needed to open a dental practice. Germany, below, is the exception. The figures in circulation come from parties selling something in the transaction: equipment suppliers, fit-out contractors, business-plan template sellers, consultants and the lenders financing the deal.

None of those sources publishes a sample or a method, and they do not agree with one another even approximately. We checked the same question in Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese and Polish this month, and in every one of those markets the ranges on offer differ from each other by a factor of several, always with no method stated.

> **A number without a sample and a method is not data, it is a sales estimate.** Before using a figure as a reference, look at who publishes it and what they sell. No sample, no data.

France shows the distinction well, because a serious study does exist there and it is not about this. Interfimo, which finances transactions for the liberal professions, publishes dental practice **transfer** prices from around 350 transactions studied in 2021 and 2022, and finds the average sale price moving "de 43% du Chiffre d'Affaires (CA) en 2018 à 47% du CA". That is real data, but it is about buying a going concern, not about building one.

## The one study published with a method

Since 1984 the IDZ has analysed, together with apoBank, how dentists invest when they set up in practice in Germany. The basis is financings the bank actually recorded: 538 usable cases in 2024, out of the 1,122 dentists who went into self-employment that year.

For the founding of a new single practice, the 2024 edition, published on 24 October 2025, works from 24 usable financings and splits the investment like this:

| Line item | 2023 | 2024 | Share in 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fit-out and conversion of the premises | EUR 129,000 | EUR 208,000 | 24% |
| Medical and technical equipment | EUR 356,000 | EUR 348,000 | 41% |
| Furniture and IT | EUR 129,000 | EUR 112,000 | 13% |
| Other investments, including working capital | EUR 156,000 | EUR 185,000 | 22% |
| **Total investment** | **EUR 770,000** | **EUR 853,000** | **100%** |

Three things in that table apply in any market. Equipment is the largest line but it is under half the total. Fit-out rose 61% in a single year, from EUR 129,000 to EUR 208,000, and it is the line that runs away when the premises did not come ready. And a fifth of the budget is neither building work nor equipment: it is working capital and the cost of getting started.

> **The report states its own limits and they have to be respected.** It is 24 cases, and the authors write that "aufgrund der geringen Fallzahl sind die Ergebnisse mit Vorsicht zu interpretieren, die Analyse kann lediglich Tendenzen widerspiegeln": given the small number of cases, the results must be interpreted with caution and the analysis can only reflect tendencies. They are also one bank's financings, in Germany.

## The mean and the median tell different stories

The mean of EUR 853,000 describes almost nobody. The standard deviation is EUR 400,000, and a third of cases fall outside that band: below EUR 453,000 or above EUR 1,253,000.

The median, EUR 690,000, describes the typical practice better, and it moved in the opposite direction to the mean: it fell from EUR 757,000 in 2023 while the mean rose 11%. Of the cases, 62.5% sit below the mean, which means a handful of large practices are pulling the average up.

For anyone building a budget, the useful reading is this: the median is your reference point, the mean measures the tail.

## What moves the number

- **Whether you plan to employ dentists from the start.** This is the single biggest factor. Mean investment went from EUR 727,000 in practices with no employed dentists to EUR 1,002,000 in those already planning for them, 38% more, because those practices are laid out with more rooms and 116 more square metres on average (338 against 222).
- **The number of operatories.** Practices with two to five came in 10.6% below the study mean; those with six to nine, 24% above.
- **The floor area.** Between 50 and 299 square metres the investment was 23.6% below the mean; between 300 and 630, 26% above it.
- **The level of technology.** The most expensive case in the sample, EUR 1.8 million, was a practice with no employed dentists, and what explained the figure was how heavily equipped it was.
- **The floor you start from.** Without employed dentists, openings were achieved from EUR 350,000 of investment. Where employed dentists were planned, the study found none below EUR 485,000.

## Building from scratch or buying a going concern

These are two transactions with two different numbers, and the study lets you compare them directly. Against the EUR 853,000 mean for a new single practice, the acquisition of an existing single practice was financed in 2024 with EUR 450,000.

The German market votes with its feet: 67% of those setting up chose to buy an existing practice, while founding a single practice from scratch accounted for about 5% of cases. The report puts it plainly: "generell sind Neugründungen mit höheren Investitionen verbunden als Übernahmen", new foundings require more investment than takeovers.

What you buy with that difference is time. An existing practice has a schedule, patients and revenue from day one. How the price of that practice is calculated is in [what a dental practice is worth](/en/blog/dental-practice-valuation/).

## The lines budgets always miss

A first budget usually covers building work and equipment, and falls short on everything else. These are the lines that go missing, and the reason the German study puts a fifth of the total under "other investments".

- **Working capital for the first months.** Rent, payroll, consumables and loan repayments all start in month one, against a schedule that is not full yet. This is the line that decides whether the practice reaches year two.
- **Your own contributions and insurance.** They start when you register, not when the first fee arrives. The amounts and any relief for new entrants are set jurisdiction by jurisdiction and change yearly, so check them with the body that collects them in the month you register, not in last year's guide.
- **The deposit and the rent-free months.** Two or three months of deposit plus the fit-out period with no income, which is paid all the same.
- **Staff hired before you open.** Reception and a nurse usually start a few weeks early to set up workflows and learn the software. Those are full salaries against zero revenue.
- **Registration and the X-ray installation.** Not expensive in themselves, but they run on fixed timetables, and a mistimed application delays opening while the rent is already running. Both are covered in [getting ready for a practice inspection](/en/blog/dental-practice-inspection-readiness/) and [X-ray radiation protection records](/en/blog/dental-x-ray-radiation-protection-records/).
- **The recurring contracts nobody budgets.** Clinical waste, autoclave and compressor servicing, dosimetry, health and safety, data protection, accountancy. Small individually, a fixed monthly charge once added up.
- **Team training and software go-live.** Hours with the practice closed or running at half capacity, which appear on no invoice and are paid for in treatment not delivered.
- **How tax treats the investment.** Ask before you sign for the equipment, because it changes the real cost of every line, and the answer depends on the regime your practice ends up in.

## How to build your own number

The figure that helps you does not come from an average, it comes from your project. The order matters, because each decision fixes the next one.

1. **Decide how many operatories you will open and how many you will leave pre-plumbed.** On the data above this is the decision that moves the total most, and the most expensive one to change later.
2. **Find premises to match**, and get a contractor's estimate before signing anything. Fit-out is the line that overruns most, and the state of the electrics and drainage decides it.
3. **Get three fixed equipment quotes**, with installation, commissioning and warranty included, and compare them on the three-year total rather than on the price of the chair.
4. **Budget working capital as its own line**: add up six to twelve months of fixed costs and put them in the capital budget, not in a note at the side.
5. **Add your own contributions and your own salary** for that period. If the plan only works when the owner is unpaid, it does not work.
6. **Put the regulatory timetables in the calendar** before you fix an opening date, and count the rent for those months.
7. **Run the same exercise on an existing practice** of the size you want, even if you are not going to buy one. Comparing the two numbers is what tells you whether building from scratch makes sense in your case.

## What to watch in the first year

Once open, the figures that tell you whether the investment is tracking are few: new patients per month, chair utilisation, average accepted treatment plan value, and cash collected against cash billed. They are the same ones a lender will ask for if the loan has to be reviewed, and they are covered in the guide to [dental practice KPIs](/en/blog/dental-practice-kpis/).
