Sterilization traceability means being able to answer two questions from your records: which load sterilized the instruments used on this patient, and which patients were treated with instruments from this load. The minimum per cycle is six fields: date, cycle number, which sterilizer, which programme, whether the cycle completed correctly, and who released it.

The seventh field is the one most practices never write down, and it is the one that turns a logbook into traceability: that cycle number, recorded in the patient's notes at the visit where the instruments were used.

## A record that only runs one way is not traceability

Nearly every practice keeps its cycle printouts. Far fewer can say, on an ordinary Tuesday, which seventeen patients were treated with instruments from cycle 412.

That second direction is the one you need on the day something goes wrong, and it is the only one that cannot be reconstructed afterwards. If the cycle number was never written into the visit, the information does not exist anywhere.

> **The test that tells you whether your system works.** Pick a patient at random from three months ago and find out which cycle sterilized their instruments. If it takes more than a minute, you do not have traceability, you have a drawer full of printouts.

## What goes in the log for every cycle

England's Health Technical Memorandum 01-05 puts it simply: if the sterilizer has an automatic printer, the print-out should be retained or copied to a permanent record. Where there is no printer, the operator records the same thing by hand.

HTM 01-05 lists four fields for that manual process log: the date, satisfactory completion of the cycle (absence of a failure light), the temperature and pressure achieved, and the signature of the operator. In practice you want three more.

- **The cycle number.** A running number is the key that later appears on the pouch label and in the patient's notes. Without it there is nothing to link.
- **Which sterilizer.** Irrelevant with one machine, essential with two, because a fault on one does not put the other machine's loads in doubt.
- **What was in the load.** The pouches or trays that went in, in enough detail that you can work out later which instruments were processed.

The equipment itself gets its own record. HTM 01-05 says the logbook should contain all information pertaining to the lifecycle of the equipment, from purchasing through to disposal.

## The pouch label is three fields

The cycle log lives in the book or on a computer. The label is what travels to the surgery, and three fields are enough for it to do its job.

1. **The sterilization date**, which is what lets you rotate the cupboard oldest first.
2. **The cycle number**, which is the link back to the log and forward into the patient's notes.
3. **The expiry date**, which depends on the packaging, its integrity and the storage conditions, not on the sterilizer.

Leave the cycle number off the label and the chain breaks at the exact moment someone opens the pouch in front of a patient, which is precisely when it should be written down.

## What each test proves, and what it does not

The tests are not accumulated paperwork. Each answers a different question and none of them substitutes for another.

| Test | What it proves | When |
|---|---|---|
| Cycle record (printout or chart) | ✓ That time, temperature and pressure were reached | Every cycle |
| External process indicator | ✗ On its own it is not evidence the load was sterile | Every package |
| Chemical indicator inside the pack | ~ That conditions were met inside the packaging | Every package, per protocol |
| Steam penetration test (Bowie-Dick or Helix) | ✓ That air was removed from the load | Before the first load of the day |
| Vacuum leak test | ✓ That the circuit holds vacuum | As specified by the manufacturer |
| Biological indicator (spores) | ✓ The actual lethality of the process | As set by your written protocol |

HTM 01-05 groups the first three of those into the daily routine: a steam penetration test on vacuum sterilizers, an automatic control test on all benchtop sterilizers, and a record of the temperature and pressure achieved at that daily test, so you know the machine is sound before instruments go through it.

The external indicator is the most misread thing in the cupboard. It changes colour because the package went through the process, and that is all it says: it separates what has been processed from what has not.

The technical standards behind all of this are published. The reference list that accompanies the German reprocessing recommendation names EN 13060 for small steam sterilizers, EN ISO 17665-1 for the moist heat process, EN ISO 11607 for packaging, EN ISO 11140 for chemical indicators, EN ISO 11138 for biological indicators and EN ISO 15883 for washer-disinfectors.

## From the cycle to the clinical record

This is where most protocols stop half-finished. The sterilization log and the patient record are two separate files, and nobody writes the number that joins them.

![Patient record on the activity tab, showing clinical alerts, the active treatment plan and a timeline of the patient's visits](/screenshots/patient-timeline.png)

*A patient's activity, visit by visit. The cycle number belongs in the note for the visit, next to the treatment and the date.*

The note is short and it goes in the visit itself: what was done, with which instruments, from which cycle. It adds seconds to a record somebody is already writing.

> **Your system is only as good as its weakest entry.** A cupboard of immaculate printouts and a clinical record where nobody writes the cycle number produces exactly zero traceability. The work is in the visit note, not in the filing cabinet.

## When a cycle fails

The day a biological indicator comes back positive or a cycle aborts halfway, the protocol runs in this order.

1. **Quarantine the load and do not use it.** Everything from that cycle goes back through the process, however clean the pouches look.
2. **Establish how far back the doubt reaches.** Usually from the last satisfactory test to the failure, which means several loads, not just the last one.
3. **Produce the list of affected patients.** This is the moment the cycle number in the notes pays for itself, because without it the list is every patient booked on those days.
4. **Record the incident and the cause.** What failed, what was done, who decided it, and when a satisfactory cycle was next achieved.
5. **Do not resume until a test passes.** The machine goes back into service on a documented satisfactory cycle, not on the feeling that it is probably fine now.

## How long the records are kept

HTM 01-05 points at Health Service Circular 1999/053 and the *Records management: code of practice* for retention, and states that the code requires records to be maintained for not less than two years, although longer periods may be applicable subject to local policy-making. It also tells you to look at the period during which patients retain legal rights, and to keep whatever the practice would need to answer a request within them.

That is the floor, not the target. The cycle number that ends up inside a clinical record is kept as long as the record is, which in most places is considerably longer than two years.

> **HTM 01-05 is an England document.** Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland publish their own decontamination guidance, and practices outside the UK answer to their own national rules. The structure of the record is the same everywhere; the retention period and the inspection checklist are not.

This is not legal advice. It is what the sources listed below say, consulted on 24 August 2026, and the requirements that apply to your practice are the ones your own regulator publishes.

## Paper, spreadsheet or software

A single-surgery practice with one autoclave runs this in a notebook, and the notebook is compliant. The problem starts when someone has to walk the record in the hard direction, from a load back to the patients, one visit at a time.

Worth saying plainly: [Dentalpin](/en/pricing/) has no sterilization module today, so the cycle log stays on the autoclave printout or in your spreadsheet. What it does cover is the half that rarely gets closed, the note at the visit: the clinical record holds what was done at each appointment, which is where the cycle number belongs, and the whole system is driven from a public API if you ever want to automate the reverse lookup. It is open source and can be self-hosted, so those records stay on your own server.
