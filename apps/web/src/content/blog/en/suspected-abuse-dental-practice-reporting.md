In England and Wales there is exactly one thing a dental professional is required by statute to report: female genital mutilation in a girl under 18, to the police, within one month of discovery. Everything else, every bruise that does not match its explanation, every disclosure in the chair, is governed by professional standards rather than criminal statute. The General Dental Council's standard 8.5.1 is unambiguous about it anyway: you must raise your concerns, and if you decide not to, you must be able to justify that decision.

This is not legal advice. It is what the official sources cited at the end say, consulted on 12 October 2026.

> **"No statutory duty" is not the same as "your call".** The GDC is the body that can remove you from the register, and its standard does not give you discretion to stay quiet comfortably. It gives you discretion you have to be able to defend afterwards, which is a different thing.

This post is not about who consents for a child, which is in [minor patients and guardians](/en/blog/minor-patients-guardians-dental/), nor about someone outside the practice asking for the file, which is in [third party requests for dental records](/en/blog/third-party-requests-dental-records/). Here the practice is the one that has to speak first, against its own duty of confidentiality.

## The one statutory duty

Section 5B of the Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003 applies to a person who works in a regulated profession in England and Wales, which includes dental professionals. The duty bites when a girl aged under 18 either tells the professional that an act of FGM has been carried out on her, or the professional observes physical signs appearing to show it, with no reason to believe it was part of a surgical operation.

The notification goes to the chief officer of police for the area in which the girl resides, within one month of the discovery, orally or in writing. It must identify the girl and explain why it is being made. The duty does not apply if another person in the same regulated profession has already notified the same act.

That is the whole of the statutory mandatory reporting picture for a dental practice. It is narrow on purpose, and knowing how narrow it is matters, because the much broader obligation sits somewhere else.

## Where the broad obligation actually lives

Two things are easy to confuse, and confusing them is how a private practice talks itself into believing nothing applies to it.

**Section 11 of the Children Act 2004** places a duty on listed bodies to make arrangements for ensuring their functions are discharged having regard to the need to safeguard and promote the welfare of children. The list is of organisations: local authorities, NHS England, integrated care boards, NHS trusts and foundation trusts, police bodies, the National Crime Agency, youth offending teams, prison governors and others. A privately owned dental practice is not on that list.

**GDC standard 8.5** applies to you personally, as a registrant, whoever owns the practice. Its wording is short enough to put on a wall:

- 8.5.1: "You must raise any concerns you may have about the possible abuse or neglect of children or vulnerable adults." It continues: "You must know who to contact for further advice and how to refer concerns to an appropriate authority" such as "your local social services department."
- 8.5.2: "You must find out about local procedures for the protection of children and vulnerable adults." And: "You must follow these procedures if you suspect that a child or vulnerable adult might be at risk" because of abuse or neglect.

The GDC also states that if you make a professional judgement and decide not to share your concern with the appropriate authority, "you must be able to justify how you came to this decision."

## What you record, because that is what survives

A referral is made once. The clinical record is read years later by people who were not there. It is the document everything else rests on.

> **Write the finding, not the conclusion.** A record that says "suspected abuse" tells a later reader nothing they can work with, and it is the line a defence barrister will spend an afternoon on. A record that says what was on the mucosa, where, how big, and what the patient said about it, in their words, is evidence. The difference costs nothing on the day and decides everything afterwards.

1. **The date and time you observed it**, not the date the following week when somebody remembered to write it up.
2. **What you saw, described as you saw it.** Site, size, colour, number. "2 cm bruise, right buccal mucosa" is a finding; "looks like abuse" is a conclusion that is not yours to draw.
3. **What the patient said, in their own words, in quotation marks.** Not tidied, not reordered, not completed where it trails off.
4. **Who attended with the patient**, and whether they stayed in the surgery or waited outside.
5. **Any mismatch between the injury and the explanation given for it.** Describe it, do not characterise it.
6. **Photographs, if taken**, linked to the episode and carrying the same date.
7. **What you referred, to whom, when and by what route**, with a copy of what you sent.

## What you are not expected to do

Most of the reluctance to refer comes from believing the job is bigger than it is.

- **You do not interview the child.** A badly conducted conversation contaminates the properly conducted one that follows. Record what the patient volunteers, and no more.
- **You do not identify a perpetrator.** A referral describes findings. It does not accuse anybody.
- **You do not decide whether a criminal offence occurred.** That is for others, with powers you do not have.
- **You do not need proof.** Standard 8.5.1 is written around concerns, not findings of fact.
- **You do not always tell the family first.** Where telling them would leave the patient less safe, local procedures take precedence, and this is exactly what your defence organisation is there to advise on.

A clinical alert is where this outlives the memory of whoever was on that day. If a case is open, the next person to open the record needs to see it before the patient is seated, not find out in month three.

## Four things to have ready before it happens

None of this gets improvised well on the day.

- **The telephone number for your local authority's children's social care**, written where whoever is on reception can find it.
- **The adult safeguarding route for your local authority**, because standard 8.5 covers vulnerable adults too and practices almost always prepare only the child half.
- **A named safeguarding lead in the practice.** A duty that belongs to everybody belongs in practice to nobody.
- **A fixed place in the record** where these observations go, the same in every file, so it does not depend on who is writing.

Keeping that in a shared folder works until the person who maintained it leaves. In [Dentalpin](/en/pricing/) notes carry their date and author, photographs attach to the episode, and the access log records who has read a file, which is the question that gets asked when a case reaches a court. Who opened a record is covered in [audit trail for dental records](/en/blog/audit-trail-dental-records/).
