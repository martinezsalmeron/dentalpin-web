Spanish law does not require you to keep clinical records on a computer. It requires something else: that the record exists, that there is one per patient within your practice, that the professionals who took part in each episode of care are identified, and that its security, its proper conservation and the retrieval of the information are guaranteed whatever the medium. On top of that sit two deadlines and one right: five years minimum from the discharge date of each episode of care, fifteen in Catalonia for certain documents, and the copy a patient can ask you for at any time.

This is not legal advice. Every official source is listed at the end, all consulted on 15 August 2026.

## Paper is still legal, being unable to retrieve it is not

Article 14.2 of Ley 41/2002 is shorter than people expect. Each centre shall archive its patients' clinical records, whatever the medium they are held on, "papel, audiovisual, informático o de otro tipo", so that their security, their proper conservation and the retrieval of the information are guaranteed.

There is no obligation to digitise anywhere in that sentence. There are three conditions, and they are exactly the ones a filing cabinet meets badly: security against whoever should not see the records, conservation over years, and retrieval of one specific file on the day it is needed.

> **The question that decides is not "can I stay on paper?" but "can I retrieve a patient's record from six years ago in five minutes?"** If the answer depends on which cabinet and on who happens to be in that morning, the medium is not meeting article 14.2, whatever it is.

Article 14.1 adds the piece that gets forgotten. The record comprises the documents relating to each patient's episodes of care "con la identificación de los médicos y de los demás profesionales que han intervenido en ellos". An entry with no author is an incomplete entry.

## What a dental practice's record has to contain

Article 15.1 sets the general test: the record shall incorporate the information considered essential for truthful and up to date knowledge of the patient's state of health. Article 15.2 turns that into a list of fifteen documents, lettered a) to o).

That list was written with hospitals in mind, and the law says so itself. Its closing sentence states that paragraphs b), c), i), j), k), l), ñ) and o) are only required "cuando se trate de procesos de hospitalización o así se disponga".

| Item in article 15.2 | In a dental practice with no admissions | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|---|
| a) Clinical and statistical sheet | ✓ Required | The patient's identifying and administrative data |
| d) History and physical examination | ✓ Required | Medical history, allergies, medication, initial exam |
| e) Progress notes | ✓ Required | What gets written at each visit |
| f) Medical orders | ✓ Required | Prescriptions and instructions |
| g) Referral sheet | ✓ Required | Referral to a surgeon, an orthodontist, a physician |
| h) Reports of complementary examinations | ✓ Required | Radiographs, CBCT, lab results |
| m) and n) Nursing care and therapeutic application | ✓ Required | What hygienists and assistants record |
| i) Informed consent | ~ Not under 15.2, but yes under article 8.2 | In writing for surgery and invasive procedures |
| b) and c) Admission authorisation, emergency report | ✗ Hospitalisation only | |
| j), k) and l) Anaesthesia, theatre, pathology | ✗ Hospitalisation only | |
| ñ) and o) Vital signs chart, discharge report | ✗ Hospitalisation only | |

The consent row is the one that misleads people, because the exemption in 15.2 buys you nothing. Article 8.2 says consent "será verbal por regla general" but must be given in writing for surgery, for invasive diagnostic and therapeutic procedures and, generally, for procedures carrying risks of notable and foreseeable adverse effect on the patient's health. A surgical extraction or an implant lands there through article 8, not through article 15.

## One record per patient, not one per treatment

Article 15.4 asks for something that sounds obvious and that almost no multi-professional practice fully manages. The clinical record shall be kept "con criterios de unidad y de integración, en cada institución asistencial como mínimo", so that clinicians get the best and most timely knowledge of a given patient's data in each episode of care.

Unity and integration mean one record per patient in the practice, not one held by the general dentist, another by the orthodontist and a third in the hygienist's folder. In a practice with several chairs this breaks in two predictable places:

- **Radiographs that live inside the imaging software** rather than in the record, so the clinician seeing the patient on Tuesday does not know they exist.
- **The duplicated patient**, registered twice with the surname spelled two ways. That stops being an untidiness problem and becomes a failure against 15.4, because one patient's data is split in two.

## Five years is the national floor, and your region can ask for more

Article 17.1 requires clinical documentation to be kept for proper patient care "durante el tiempo adecuado a cada caso y, como mínimo, cinco años contados desde la fecha del alta de cada proceso asistencial".

Two details change the arithmetic:

1. **The clock runs from the discharge of each episode**, not from the last time the patient walked in. Orthodontic treatment finished in 2021 and a check-up this month are separate episodes with separate expiry dates.
2. **Article 17.2 extends the period for other reasons.** Documentation is also kept for judicial purposes and where there are epidemiological, research, or health service organisation grounds.

Article 17.3 settles the most common question about digitising an old archive: healthcare centres must preserve the information "aunque no necesariamente en el soporte original". Scanning paper files and destroying the originals is something the law contemplates, as long as the copy preserves the security, conservation and retrieval that article 14.2 demands.

> **Catalonia does not apply five years, it applies fifteen.** Article 12 of Ley 21/2000 sets a minimum of fifteen years from the discharge date of each episode of care for informed consent forms, discharge reports, theatre reports, anaesthesia data, reports of complementary examinations and pathology reports. If you practise in Catalonia, that is your number, not the national one.

Before writing a deletion policy, read your autonomous community's own rules. Ley 41/2002 is basic legislation setting a floor, several regions have legislated above it, and the Catalan case is the widest gap.

## Custody is yours, not your vendor's

Article 17.4 assigns record management in centres with inpatients, or those seeing a sufficient number of patients under any other care model, to the admissions and clinical documentation unit, under the responsibility of the centre's management.

Article 17.5 covers the small practice: healthcare professionals working individually "son responsables de la gestión y de la custodia de la documentación asistencial que generen".

Neither wording moves the obligation to the software vendor. Buying a cloud service is a perfectly sound decision and it shifts the duty of custody by exactly nothing: it stays with the centre or with the professional.

## Who may look, and what gets logged

Article 16 splits access by function, and the two rules that matter most in a practice are restrictive ones:

- **Administrative and management staff** may only access the record data "relacionados con sus propias funciones" (16.4). Reception needs the schedule, the phone number and the payment status, not the medical history.
- **Accredited healthcare staff performing inspection**, evaluation, accreditation and planning duties may access records in the course of those duties (16.5).
- **Everyone who accesses the record is bound by the duty of secrecy** (16.6), including those doing so for administrative reasons.

Paragraph 16.7 is why the answer depends on where you practise: the autonomous communities "regularán el procedimiento para que quede constancia del acceso a la historia clínica y de su uso". The access log is not specified in the national statute, it is delegated to regional rules, so the detail has to be looked up in your own region.

In practice this comes down to two things that either the software provides or nobody does: named users, one per person and never shared, and a record of which user opened which chart and when. A single "reception" login used by four people makes any access trail impossible.

## The patient can ask for a copy, and the deadline comes from GDPR

Article 18.1 grants the patient the right of access to the documentation in the clinical record and "a obtener copia de los datos que figuran en ella", subject to the reservations in paragraph 3. Article 18.2 allows the right to be exercised through duly accredited representation.

The reservations in 18.3 are two, and they are worth knowing before the request arrives:

- **The professionals' subjective notes.** The patient's right of access cannot be exercised to the detriment of the professionals involved in producing the record, who may withhold their "anotaciones subjetivas".
- **Third-party data.** Nor can it be exercised to the detriment of third parties' right to confidentiality over data recorded in the patient's therapeutic interest.

Article 18.4 covers access by people close to a deceased patient, which does not apply if the patient expressly forbade it, and which excludes data affecting the deceased's privacy, the professionals' subjective notes and anything harming third parties.

Ley 41/2002 sets no deadline for handing over the copy. The one that applies comes from GDPR: article 12.3 requires information on the action taken to be provided without undue delay and "in any event within one month of receipt of the request", extendable by two further months where complexity justifies it.

## Real Decreto 1093/2010 does not apply to you, and is still worth reading

This is the usual confusion behind the search "what fields must an electronic health record have". Real Decreto 1093/2010 approves the minimum data set for clinical reports, and its article 1 draws the boundary: the provisions apply "en todos los centros y dispositivos asistenciales que integran el Sistema Nacional de Salud".

A private dental practice sits outside it. What the decree standardises is nine report types inside the public system, among them the discharge report, the outpatient report, the emergency report, the primary care report, the laboratory report, the imaging results report, the nursing care report and the summarised clinical record.

It stays useful for a practical reason. When you refer a patient into the public system or receive a report back from it, that is the format you will meet, and its annexes are the most detailed published reference in Spanish for what a clinical report should carry.

## What you can reasonably demand from the software

Most of the above is protocol and judgement. These five, though, either come with the tool or fall to you by hand forever:

1. **A single record per patient** pulling together odontogram, progress notes, radiographs, consents and quotes, which is article 15.4 expressed as a product requirement.
2. **Author and timestamp on every entry**, with the professional identified, per article 14.1.
3. **Named users and role-based permissions**, so reception sees its own view and not the medical history, per article 16.4.
4. **An access log** per chart, which is what lets you answer whatever your region requires under 16.7.
5. **Full export of one patient** in a readable format, so the copy under article 18.1 does not become a screenshot exercise.

Add a sixth that is not in the statute and decides everything else: being able to take your complete data out on the day you change vendor. A clinical record that can only be read inside the program that wrote it is a five-year retention duty held in somebody else's hands.
