The only way to build Google reviews that survive is to ask every patient, at the same moment, in the same words. Buying them, running a prize draw among reviewers, or having the team post them are practices the FTC banned by rule in 2024 and the UK banned by statute in 2025, and Google's own policy removes them when it finds them.

What follows is exactly what Google allows, what the law bans on both sides of the Atlantic, how to build the asking routine, and the part that only applies to a healthcare business: how to reply to a review without confirming that the person writing it is your patient.

## What Google allows, in Google's words

The Maps user contributed content policy is short and written in the positive. It expressly permits asking: businesses may *"Solicit or encourage the posting of content that does represent a genuine experience, without offering incentives to do so"*.

Three things sit outside that. Incentives, which the policy defines as *"payment, discounts, free goods and/or services"* in exchange for posting a review, revising one, or removing a negative one. Conflicts of interest, described as *"current or former employment, a contractual or consultory relationship, or other professional or personal affiliations"*. And pressure on the premises: the policy says businesses should not require or pressure users to leave ratings while they are in the building, nor request that specific content be included.

| What happens at the front desk | Google's policy |
|---|---|
| Asking every patient, no conditions attached | ✓ Allowed |
| Texting the review link after the appointment | ✓ Allowed |
| Money off the next hygiene visit for a review | ✗ Incentive |
| Prize draw among everyone who reviews | ✗ Incentive |
| Team members or their families posting | ✗ Conflict of interest |
| Handing over a tablet chairside until they write | ✗ Pressure on the premises |
| Asking only the patients who look happy | ~ Not banned by the policy, but see below |

> **The prize draw is the most common mistake and the clearest one.** The prize does not have to be cash: the policy names payment, discounts, free goods and services, and an entry into a draw is a benefit offered in exchange for an opinion.

## In the United States, the FTC rule has teeth

The FTC's Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465, *"went into effect on October 21, 2024"*. It reaches fake reviews, reviews by people who never had the experience, insider reviews and review suppression, and it authorises civil penalties for knowing violations.

Two points in the FTC's own Q&A matter to a practice that is trying to do this properly.

- **Incentives are not banned outright.** The FTC states the rule *"does not prohibit giving incentives for reviews, as long as there isn't an express or implied requirement that the reviews have to express a particular sentiment"*. Paying for five-star reviews is a different matter and violates the rule.
- **Google bans what the FTC permits.** An incentive that survives 16 CFR 465 still breaches Google's policy and still gets the review deleted, so on Google the stricter rule is the one that governs.
- **Insider reviews need disclosure.** Staff may review the practice only if they *"clearly and conspicuously disclose their relationship"*.
- **Suppression is narrower than it sounds.** The FTC points at false accusations against reviewers, intimidation and *"unfounded or groundless legal threat[s]"*, and adds that *"Organizing reviews is not suppressing reviews under the rule"*.

## In the United Kingdom, it is a banned practice

The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 created a banned practice covering reviews, and the CMA's guidance on it, CMA208, is dated 4 April 2025. Its opening summary says the practice *"covers fake reviews, concealed incentivised reviews and requires traders not to publish consumer reviews (and information derived from them) in a misleading way"*.

The CMA spells out that commissioning is not only about money. In its words, *"It could include a trader requesting a member of staff to write a review of the trader's product"*.

> **A banned practice is automatically unfair.** The CMA describes it as behaviour that is *"automatically unfair and illegal"*, which means nobody has to prove that a patient was actually misled.

The guidance also warns publishers against removing genuine negative reviews, saying reviews should not be taken down *"merely because a trader objects to the reviewer's assessment of their experience"*. That applies to the practice's own website testimonials page as much as to a review site.

## How many reviews it takes to rank

Google publishes how local results are ordered, and it is worth reading before setting a target. Three factors: relevance, distance and prominence. On reviews it says one sentence, *"More reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking"*, and on shortcuts one just as short: *"There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google"*.

Distance is the one you cannot change, and it dominates a search made on a phone three streets away. A practice with forty honest reviews and a complete profile competes well; one with four hundred bought ones competes worse than it looks.

## The asking routine that produces a steady trickle

1. **Pick one moment.** At the desk when they settle up, or twenty four hours later by text. Two moments means some weeks it happens and some weeks it does not.
2. **Write the sentence once and keep it.** "If the appointment went well, a Google review really helps us. I'll send you the link now." Twelve seconds.
3. **Send the short review link** that Google generates from the Business Profile, so it opens straight into the writing box.
4. **Use the appointment reminder channel.** It is the one the patient already recognises as yours, so it does not land in spam.
5. **One message, no chasing.** Anyone who does not review first time will not review third time, and the third time irritates.
6. **Ask everybody.** Not the one who smiled, not the one who paid for the implant, not the regulars. Everybody.
7. **Record only whether you asked.** You do not need to know who left one, and you are better off not knowing.

Step six is the one that gets skipped and the one that costs most. Screening for the patients who look pleased is not banned by Google's policy, but it turns your average into a measure of the front desk's mood, and the day the first unscreened review arrives, nothing is holding that average up.

## Replying without breaching confidentiality

A dental practice is not a restaurant here. A public reply signed by the practice confirms that this person sat in your chair, and that is health information.

The consequences are documented rather than theoretical. In October 2019 Elite Dental Associates of Dallas paid $10,000 to settle potential HIPAA Privacy Rule violations over social media disclosures of patients' protected health information. In December 2022 New Vision Dental settled for $23,000 after impermissibly disclosing PHI while responding to patient reviews.

Replying at all requires a verified Business Profile, and Google warns that a reply usually takes up to ten minutes to appear and can take up to thirty days.

- **Never confirm they are a patient.** Not "sorry your treatment", not "according to your record", not "the day you came in".
- **Never correct the facts in public.** Even when the review is wrong about the price or the procedure, rebutting it reveals what happened in the operatory.
- **Use one format every time:** regret the experience described, give a name and a phone number, stop. Three lines.
- **Move the conversation off Google.** The reply is not there to win the argument, it is there so the next reader sees somebody is home.
- **Reply to the good ones too**, one line, naming no treatments.

Text that works as written: "Thank you for taking the time to write. We're sorry to hear about the experience described and we'd like to understand what happened. Please call us on 020 0000 0000 and ask for Sarah, our practice manager."

> **That text works because it never says whether this person has been here.** A "when you came in for your check-up on Tuesday" turns an awkward review into a confidentiality breach, and those two problems are no longer fixed the same way.

## When a review can be removed, and when it cannot

Google lets you report a review from the profile and flag the reason, and says *"Flagged reviews that violate our content policies are removed and will no longer show on Maps and Search"*. Only policy violations are eligible.

The line that saves time is on the same page: *"Do not report a review just because you disagree with it or dislike it. Google doesn't get involved in conflict between businesses and customers"*.

| A one-star review saying… | Grounds for removal? |
|---|---|
| The manner was cold and the price too high | ✗ An opinion, however much it stings |
| Something that happened at a different practice | ✓ Does not reflect the location |
| Another patient's name and diagnosis | ✓ Personal information |
| A rant about private healthcare in general | ✓ Off topic |
| Abuse or threats aimed at the team | ✓ Harassment |
| That it was written by someone who never attended | ~ Grounds, but the evidence is on you |

The personal information policy is the most useful ground and the least known in a dental setting: *"Do not distribute or post personal information without consent"*. A review written by a relative that names the patient and describes their treatment fits there.

Google says review evaluation typically takes several days, and that if it finds no violation you get a single appeal. Reporting ten reviews you merely dislike spends that appeal on the ones going nowhere.

## Three numbers, once a month

- **Appointments held, and how many were asked.** If the second number does not resemble the first, the routine is the problem, not the patients.
- **New reviews received.** Two or three a month in a two-chair practice is a healthy rate that holds up for years.
- **Reviews left unanswered.** It should be zero, and checking takes five minutes.

The star average is deliberately not on that list. It rises on its own when the other three are right, and chasing it directly is exactly what pushes a practice into screening who gets asked.

## Setting it up this week

1. **Claim and verify the Business Profile** if it is not already, because without it you cannot even reply.
2. **Copy the short review link** from the profile and store it next to the reminder template.
3. **Write the front desk sentence** and tape it to the desk, so all three people who cover reception say the same thing.
4. **Write the negative-review reply template** before the first one arrives, and have the principal sign it off.
5. **Decide who replies and on which day.** Mondays, one person, ten minutes.
6. **Go through the old unanswered reviews** and clear them in one sitting.
7. **Put the first Monday of each month in the calendar** to look at the three numbers above.

In Dentalpin, patient communications are sent from the same place as the schedule, so the message carrying the review link goes out from the appointment that justifies it and is recorded on that patient's record. What each edition includes is on the [pricing page](/en/pricing/).

None of this is legal advice. For a specific question about patient data, that is a matter for your regulator, your indemnity provider or your own adviser.
