Yes, you can offer one. A free first consultation is not prohibited in the UK. Two separate sets of rules apply to it: how you advertise it, and what you have to write down afterwards. "Free" may only be used if the patient pays nothing beyond the unavoidable cost of responding to the ad, and the examination inside that appointment produces a contemporaneous clinical record and informed consent exactly as a paid one does.

This is not legal advice. It is a reading of the official sources listed at the end, consulted on 24 October 2026, and the rules differ outside the UK even where the language does not.

## "Free" has a defined meaning in the CAP Code

It is a regulated term, not a sales adjective. Rule 3.23 of the CAP Code states that ads "must not describe a product as 'free' if the consumer has to pay anything other than the unavoidable cost". The unavoidable costs the rule allows are the ones involved in responding to the ad and in collecting or paying for delivery of the item, so for a consultation that means getting to the practice and nothing else.

The Advertising Standards Authority also makes clear that the rules apply to any word likely to mean the same thing in context: "gratis", "complimentary", "without charge" and similar phrasings are read as "free".

- **Radiographs.** If the appointment needs a periapical or a panoramic to say anything useful, and that image is charged for, the consultation was not free.
- **A records or administration fee.** Any administrative charge on the day breaks the claim.
- **A purchase condition.** "Free consultation when you book treatment" is a conditional discount on the treatment, not a free consultation.

Two further rules sit alongside it. Rule 3.24 requires ads to make clear the extent of the commitment a consumer must make to take up a free offer, and rule 3.25 prohibits using "free" to describe an element of a package whose cost is already included in the package price.

> **The threshold is the unavoidable cost of responding, and nothing else.** Getting to the practice is the patient's cost and does not affect the claim. Anything the practice invoices that day does.

## The GDC still expects your prices to be on display

A free consultation does not remove the transparency duty. The General Dental Council's guidance on dental costs states that "a simple price list is clearly displayed in the reception or waiting area of the practice", and says the list should include basic items such as a consultation, a single-surface filling, an extraction, radiographs and hygienist treatment. Items that vary can be shown as a range.

The same guidance requires clear price information in practice literature and on the website. So the consultation has a listed price even in a practice that waives it, and that is the figure a patient compares the offer against.

The GDC's advertising guidance adds the obvious constraint: advertising must be accurate and not misleading, and misleading material can lead to a fitness to practise investigation.

## The record obligations do not scale with the fee

This is the part that does not depend on price and the part most often underestimated. Standard 4.1 of Standards for the Dental Team is unconditional: "You must make and keep contemporaneous, complete and accurate patient records."

Its guidance spells out what that means in practice. Point 4.1.1 requires complete and accurate records "including an up-to-date medical history", and point 4.1.2 says you should record as much detail as possible about the discussions you have with your patients.

Consent carries the cost conversation with it. Standard 3.1 reads: "You must obtain valid consent before starting treatment, explaining all the relevant options and the possible costs", and its guidance point 3.1.3 lists "the cost of proposed treatment" among what the patient must be told.

> **A badly documented free appointment is the worst of both outcomes.** It produced no fee and it did produce a retention duty, a confidentiality duty and notes the patient can request years later. The consultation is free, the file is not.

## What the appointment is actually worth

This arithmetic is useful whether or not you ever run the offer. A first consultation occupies a chair, a nurse and reception, so its cost is the chair revenue of the time it blocks. Use your own figure: your average hourly chair revenue multiplied by the time the appointment really takes, including turnaround.

That cost is recovered only through accepted treatment plans, so the useful question is not whether to give the consultation away but how many of them end in an accepted plan.

1. **Count the free appointments over a quarter**, separated from the rest of the diary by an appointment reason of their own.
2. **Count how many produced a written treatment plan**, not how many seemed interested.
3. **Count how many of those plans were accepted** and total their value.
4. **Divide that total by the number of free appointments.** That is what one of them is worth.
5. **Compare it with the chair revenue of the time each one blocks.**

![A patient quote listing the treatments, the totals and how long the offer is valid](/screenshots/budgets.png)

*The plan that comes out of a first consultation, with its lines, its total and its validity date: the document that decides whether the appointment paid for itself.*

The value of the calculation is that it separates step 2 from step 3. If plans go out and few come back accepted, the problem is follow-up rather than the offer, and the answer is a call after ten days instead of another campaign. If few plans go out at all, the offer is attracting the wrong enquiries.

## What to check before the offer goes on the website

| What the ad or the front desk says | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "Free consultation" and nothing is invoiced that day | ✓ Meets CAP Code rule 3.23 |
| "Free consultation" and the radiograph is charged separately | ✗ The patient pays more than the cost of responding |
| "Free consultation when you book treatment" | ✗ A conditional discount, not a free service |
| "Complimentary assessment" or "no charge consultation" | ~ Equivalent wording: the same rule applies |
| The extent of the commitment is stated in the ad | ✓ Required by rule 3.24 |
| "Free" whitening described inside a priced package | ✗ Rule 3.25 prohibits this |
| A price list including a consultation is on display | ✓ GDC guidance on dental costs |
| Records and consent taken at the unpaid appointment | ✓ Standards 4.1 and 3.1, regardless of fee |

## Where software helps and where it does not

No system decides whether an advertisement is compliant. What it solves is the problem that arrives afterwards without warning: showing what was examined that day, what was discussed and which document the patient was handed.

For that it is enough to run the free appointment like any other, with its own appointment reason so it can be counted, its own record and a dated plan. In [Dentalpin](/en/pricing/) that reason is a field on the diary and quotes are stored with their versions, so the number in step 4 comes out of the data rather than out of reception's memory. What it does not do, and cannot do, is review the legality of an advertisement: that is a reading of the rules and, in case of doubt, a question to your regulator or indemnifier.
