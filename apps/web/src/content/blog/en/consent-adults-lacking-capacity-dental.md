These are two separate questions with two separate answers. Consent to the treatment belongs to the patient unless they lack capacity for that particular decision at that particular time. Signing and paying for the quote is a financial matter, and it may sit with someone else entirely.

Capacity under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 is decision-specific, not a label a patient carries. The same person can validly consent to a filling in the morning and lack capacity for a full-arch implant plan in the afternoon.

This is not legal advice, and the Mental Capacity Act 2005 applies in England and Wales only. All official sources are listed at the end, consulted on 14 October 2026.

## Start from the presumption, not the diagnosis

Section 1 of the Act sets five principles, and the first two decide most front-desk situations. "A person must be assumed to have capacity unless it is established that he lacks capacity," and "a person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision unless all practicable steps to help him to do so have been taken without success".

Section 1(4) adds the one most often forgotten: "a person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision merely because he makes an unwise decision". Refusing the extraction you recommend is not evidence of incapacity.

Section 2(3) rules out the shortcuts directly. A lack of capacity "cannot be established merely by reference to a person's age or appearance, or a condition of his, or an aspect of his behaviour, which might lead others to make unjustified assumptions about his capacity".

> **Capacity is assessed per decision and per moment.** Section 2(1) says a person lacks capacity "in relation to a matter if at the material time" they cannot make that decision. A single note reading "lacks capacity" on a patient record is not an assessment and will not stand up to anyone reading it later.

## The functional test, as four things you can actually ask

Section 3(1) says a person is unable to make a decision if they are unable to do any of the following:

- **Understand the information relevant to the decision**, which under section 3(4) includes the reasonably foreseeable consequences of deciding either way, and of not deciding at all.
- **Retain that information**, though section 3(3) is explicit that retaining it "for a short period only" does not prevent capacity.
- **Use or weigh that information** as part of making the decision.
- **Communicate the decision**, "whether by talking, using sign language or any other means".

Section 3(2) puts the work back on the clinician: the patient is not to be treated as unable to understand "if he is able to understand an explanation of it given to him in a way that is appropriate to his circumstances (using simple language, visual aids or any other means)".

## If the patient does lack capacity for this decision

Section 4 requires a best interests determination, and it is a process with steps rather than a judgement call. The decision maker must consider, so far as reasonably ascertainable, "the person's past and present wishes and feelings", the beliefs and values that would be likely to influence their decision, and the other factors they would be likely to consider.

Section 4(4) requires that the person be permitted and encouraged "to participate, or to improve his ability to participate, as fully as possible" in the decision. Section 4(7) requires consulting, where practicable and appropriate, anyone named by the person, anyone caring for them or interested in their welfare, any donee of a lasting power of attorney, and any deputy.

Section 5 is the provision that protects the practice. If you take reasonable steps to establish whether the patient lacks capacity, and you reasonably believe both that they do and that the act is in their best interests, then you do not incur liability you would not have incurred had they consented. Section 5(3) keeps negligence liability untouched.

| Who is in front of you | Who consents to treatment | What you need to see |
|---|---|---|
| Adult with capacity for this decision | ✓ The patient | Nothing beyond the usual consent record |
| Adult who lacks capacity, no attorney or deputy | ~ Nobody consents; you act in best interests under s.5 | Your capacity assessment and best interests reasoning |
| Health and welfare LPA, registered | ✓ The attorney, within the instrument | The registered instrument, not a photocopy of a letter |
| Property and affairs LPA only | ✗ Not for treatment decisions | The instrument, which shows its own limits |
| Court-appointed personal welfare deputy | ✓ The deputy, within the order | The Court of Protection order |

Section 11(7)(c) is the one worth reading twice: a personal welfare lasting power of attorney "extends to giving or refusing consent to the carrying out or continuation of a treatment by a person providing health care for P", but only where P lacks capacity, and subject to any conditions or restrictions written into the instrument.

> **A property and affairs attorney cannot consent to an extraction.** And a health and welfare attorney cannot necessarily commit the patient's money. The two authorities are granted separately, and most of the confusion at a dental front desk comes from treating one piece of paper as covering both.

## Who signs and pays for the quote

A treatment plan with a price on it is a financial commitment, so it follows the money authority rather than the health one.

Section 16 lets the court either make the decision itself or appoint a deputy, and it distinguishes "P's personal welfare" from "P's property and affairs". Section 16(4) tells the court to prefer making the decision itself over appointing a deputy, and to keep a deputy's powers "as limited in scope and duration as is reasonably practicable".

Practically, that leaves four cases:

- **The patient has capacity for the financial decision.** They sign, whatever else is in place.
- **A registered property and affairs LPA.** The attorney signs, within the instrument.
- **A property and affairs deputy.** The deputy signs, within the court order, and the order is worth reading for a limit on amount.
- **Neither exists.** There is nobody who can validly commit the patient's money, and a large elective plan may need an application to the Court of Protection before it can sensibly start.

The uncomfortable but common outcome is that the patient validly consents to the treatment and somebody else has to authorise paying for it. That is two acts, two authorities and two records.

## What to ask for and what to record

1. **Ask about attorneys and deputies at registration**, as a neutral question on the medical history form. Nobody volunteers a lasting power of attorney unprompted.
2. **Ask to see the instrument or order**, and read which of the two domains it covers. "I'm his attorney" is a starting point, not an answer.
3. **Record who holds the authority** as a field on the patient record, with the document type, its date and what you saw.
4. **Write the capacity assessment against the decision**, dated, in that day's notes. Not as a permanent flag on the patient.
5. **Record the steps you took to support the decision**: easy-read material, a second appointment, an interpreter, a longer slot.
6. **Record the best interests reasoning** where you relied on section 5, including who you consulted under section 4(7).
7. **Keep consent and the financial agreement as separate documents**, each storing its own signatory, author and timestamp.

## Three checks in ten minutes

1. **Open the record of a patient you know has an attorney** and see whether that is a field or a sentence buried in free text.
2. **Find the last large plan signed by somebody other than the patient** and check whether anything on file shows why they could sign it.
3. **Check whether your software allows a different signatory** on the consent form and on the quote. If it forces the same one, that is the gap.
