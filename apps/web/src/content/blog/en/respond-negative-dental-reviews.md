You can reply to a negative review, but the reply cannot confirm that the person was a patient, name their treatment, give dates, or correct their version of events. That leaves one legitimate reply: two or three neutral sentences about how the practice handles complaints, plus a private channel. Everything that would win the argument is exactly what you are not allowed to write.

What follows is why the rule is that strict, what fits inside a public reply, and what to do with the part that does not.

> **Here is the test: your reply has to read the same way if the reviewer had never set foot in the practice.** If a single word stops making sense under that reading, that word is disclosing that the person is a patient.

## Why the obvious reply is the one you cannot post

The temptation is to answer with facts, because the facts are usually on your side. The problem is that every fact you add is health information about an identified person, published by you, in the open, permanently.

Four leaks show up in almost every draft, and the first three do not feel like disclosure while you are typing them:

- **Confirming the relationship.** "We are sorry about your experience at our practice" already says this person was a patient. It is the smallest possible disclosure and it is still health information.
- **Naming the treatment.** "The implant was placed as planned" reveals the diagnosis, the procedure and the site.
- **Giving dates.** "You attended in March and did not return for review" reveals the visit history and publicly criticises a patient's behaviour on top of it.
- **Arguing about money.** "The treatment plan was signed and accepted" looks like accounting, but an amount attached to a treatment still describes the care someone received.

A lot of negative dental reviews are not about clinical care at all, they are about money: a quote that grew, a charge nobody expected, an insurer that did not cover it. The urge to post the invoice is even stronger, and the rule does not change.

## What the rules actually say

Two separate obligations apply at once, and that is the part the reply templates circulating online tend to skip.

The first is data protection. Article 9(1) of the GDPR prohibits processing data concerning health unless one of the exceptions in Article 9(2) applies, including the data subject's explicit consent. Publishing a reply that reveals the care an identified person received is processing that data, and it needs one of those exceptions behind it.

The second is your professional duty, and it stands on its own. Standard 4.2.3 of the General Dental Council's Standards for the Dental Team is unusually blunt about it: "You must not post any information or comments about patients on social networking or blogging sites." The GDC's Guidance on using social media, effective from 27 June 2016, adds that when using social media you must "maintain and protect patients' information by not publishing any information which could identify them on social media without their explicit consent".

> **The patient going public does not release you.** Their review is information they chose to publish about themselves. Your duty of confidentiality is still yours, and lifting it takes explicit consent, not an inference drawn from what they decided to share.

This is not legal advice. For a serious complaint, the reading that counts for your practice is your indemnifier's and your regulator's.

## What a public reply can and cannot contain

The same sentence is fine or is a breach depending on where it is written. This is the line, and it is more use in a table than in the head of whoever is replying at nine in the morning.

| | In the public reply | In a private channel with that person |
|---|---|---|
| Confirming they attended the practice | ✗ Never | ✓ Yes, they already know |
| Naming the treatment or diagnosis | ✗ Never | ✓ Yes |
| Giving appointment dates | ✗ Never | ✓ Yes |
| Discussing the fee or the treatment plan | ✗ Never | ✓ Yes |
| Correcting their version of events | ✗ Never | ✓ Yes |
| Explaining how you handle complaints | ✓ Yes | ✓ Yes |
| Offering a phone number or an email | ✓ Yes | ~ They have it already |
| Thanking them for the feedback | ✓ Yes | ✓ Yes |

The left column is short on purpose. A public reply is not where anything gets resolved, it is where you show that somewhere to resolve it exists.

## The three-sentence reply

It works because it says nothing you could not say to a stranger, and it still tells every other reader that this practice takes complaints seriously.

1. **Acknowledge without agreeing or disagreeing.** "Thank you for taking the time to write." Not "we're sorry about your experience with us", which confirms the relationship.
2. **Describe the process, in the present tense and in general terms.** "We review every complaint with the clinician involved and respond in writing." Talk about the practice, not about this person.
3. **Open a private channel.** An email address or a phone number, without asking for clinical details in the open and without asking for the review to be taken down.

Three things worth never doing, and all three happen daily:

- **Offering something in exchange for removing the review.** It turns a complaint into a negotiation, and the offer sits in writing somewhere public where anyone can quote it later.
- **Replying from a team member's personal account.** The duty of confidentiality covers everyone in the practice, on and off the clock.
- **Asking staff or family to post reviews to bury the bad one.** It is the first thing anyone spots, and it is the only item here that cannot be fixed afterwards.

## When the review is false

This is where most practices break the rule, because the unfairness is real and a public reply looks like the only outlet available. It is not, and it is the route that ends worst.

1. **Save a dated screenshot.** If this later goes to the platform or to a solicitor, the original content is the first thing they will ask for, and reviews get edited.
2. **Read the platform's rules and find its reporting route.** If the review breaks them, that route does not require you to publish anything; check what it accepts before you write a word in the open.
3. **Reply anyway, with the three sentences.** An unanswered review and a review with a calm reply read very differently, even when the reply does not engage with the substance.
4. **If it has to go further, it goes through a solicitor.** A legal claim is argued in a letter, not in the review thread, and there the clinical facts can be put forward under the right safeguards.
5. **Do not reply the same day.** It is the one rule here with no legal basis and the one that prevents the most damage.

## What to record inside the practice

The review is answered in public in thirty seconds, but the complaint lives in the practice for months. If it is not written down, the next person who picks it up starts from nothing and will say whatever seems reasonable.

- **The complaint, with its date and its wording.** In the patient record, not in a lost email thread or a staff chat group.
- **Who handled it and what they said.** This is what stops the practice giving two different accounts in two different conversations.
- **What was agreed and whether it happened.** A free review appointment promised and never booked is the second review.
- **Kept apart from the clinical notes.** A complaint is a communication, not a clinical finding, and mixing the two contaminates both records.
