Most treatment plans that never get accepted were never turned down either. They were left undecided. What recovers them is three contacts on a schedule you set in advance and an explicit close for everything that goes quiet, with an expiry date printed on the estimate and one named person responsible for each one.

Without those two things, the date and the owner, there is no follow-up to run. There is only a folder of open estimates that grows by itself and that nobody wants to empty.

## An estimate with no date and no owner cannot be chased

Fix the document before you write a single message. An estimate that does not say what is included, what each item costs and how long the price holds gives you nothing to reopen the conversation with, because every question sends the front desk back to rebuild it.

In the UK the baseline is set for you. The government's guidance on choosing and paying for dental care says you "should be provided with details of the proposed treatment, the costs and whether the treatment is NHS or private", and that your dentist "should also tell you if the treatment plan and/or estimated costs change, and give you an updated plan in writing", which it attributes to the GDC's standards for the dental team.

Read that last clause as an instruction about follow-up, not just about the first appointment. If the plan or the price moves during a chase, the paperwork moves with it.

> **An estimate with no expiry date never expires, so it never gets closed.** Setting one (thirty, sixty or ninety days, whatever the practice decides) turns an endless folder into a working list.

Two more fields belong on the record rather than on the printout: who owns this estimate, and what state it is in. Four states are enough. Pending, accepted, declined, expired.

## Why they stall, and it is rarely the price

Staffroom shorthand says patients think it is expensive. Sometimes they do, but the number is usually the reason people say out loud rather than the one that decides.

- **Timing.** A long course of treatment gets postponed for a mortgage, a holiday or a job change. That patient has not said no, they have said not now, and they need somebody to come back at the right moment.
- **Fear.** The figure is covering a question they did not want to ask, almost always about pain, the number of visits, or the result. Nobody signs what they do not understand.
- **It is a decision for two.** Larger plans get discussed at home. If the person in the chair leaves with nothing they can show, that conversation happens from memory.
- **The next step is unclear.** If the estimate does not name a concrete next action, the patient waits for a call that was never assigned to anyone.
- **They are comparing.** They are getting a second opinion elsewhere. That is reasonable, and worth knowing, because it changes what the second contact should say.

Four of those five are solved by talking and only one is about money. That is why chasing works. It is not pestering, it is finishing a conversation that stopped halfway.

## The sequence: three contacts and a close

A written schedule kills both bad versions of follow-up, which are never calling at all and calling five times in a fortnight.

1. **Within 24 to 48 hours, a short message.** Do not ask whether they are going ahead. Ask whether the plan made sense once they read it at home, and whether anything came up.
2. **Between day seven and day ten, a phone call.** This is the contact that decides most cases, and it should be made by somebody who can answer on both the clinical side and the payment side.
3. **A week before the expiry date, a notice.** This is the only reminder with a real reason attached: the prices are reviewed after that date.
4. **On expiry, an explicit close.** The estimate becomes expired with a reason recorded, and that reason is what makes the list useful again in six months.
5. **A monthly review of the closed ones.** The plans that stalled on money or on timing go back into the diary when the moment comes, not before.

> **Silence is not a maybe.** An estimate that has sat unanswered for two months without being closed distorts every acceptance rate you calculate, and nobody works it, because technically it is still live.

Step four is the uncomfortable one and the one that tidies everything else. Closing an estimate as declined is not losing the patient. It is refusing to count them as still deciding.

## Which channel for which estimate

Not every estimate deserves the same amount of time. The working rule is simple: the larger the amount and the longer the relationship, the more expensive the channel can be.

| Channel | Where it fits | Response | Leaves a record |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phone call | High value, long courses, clinical doubts | ✓ The highest | ~ Only if somebody writes it down |
| Text or WhatsApp | First contact, mid value, expiry notice | ~ Good if it is short | ✓ The message itself is the record |
| Email | Plans that get discussed at home, with the PDF attached | ✗ The lowest | ✓ Document included |
| At the next appointment | When they are already booked for something else | ✓ High, face to face | ✗ Depends on somebody flagging it |

The last row is the most wasted. If a patient with a pending estimate is booked in for a hygiene visit, somebody should know that before they walk through the door.

## What to say, and what to leave out

Wording matters less than specificity. A generic nudge throws away the one advantage you have, which is knowing exactly what was discussed.

- **Name the treatment.** "The crown on the lower right six" starts a conversation. "The estimate we gave you" makes the patient do the remembering.
- **Ask one question.** About the treatment or about paying for it. Two questions in one message get half an answer or none.
- **Offer one next step.** Reply to this message, or a phone number somebody actually answers. Every extra option lowers the response.
- **No invented urgency.** An expiry date is a real reason. An offer that ends on Friday is not, and in healthcare it reads badly.
- **Be careful with the reflex discount.** Dropping the price at the second contact teaches patients to wait for the drop, and the next estimate you write is no longer believed.

If the treatment or the amount changes during the conversation, do not fix it verbally. Issue a new estimate in writing, close the old one and start the new clock.

## The four numbers that tell you it is working

| Metric | How it is calculated | What it should trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Acceptance rate | Estimates accepted over estimates issued, per period | If it falls, look by treatment type before looking at the total |
| Time to decision | Days between issue and acceptance or close | If it grows, the sequence is skipping steps |
| Value pending | Total of estimates still pending and still in date | This is future chair time already sitting in somebody's kitchen |
| Closed with no contact | Expired with no logged contact at all | Above 20 %, the problem is the follow-up, not the price |

A single practice-wide acceptance rate misleads. A practice doing many root canals and few full rehabilitations posts a high rate that means nothing, so calculate it by treatment type, by value band and by the clinician who presented it.

The fourth number is the one nobody looks at and the fastest to fix. An estimate that expired without a single call or message is not a patient who said no.

## Where to start this week

1. **Count the pending estimates** and how old they are. The number is usually a surprise.
2. **Put an expiry date** on everything issued from tomorrow, and pick one period for the whole practice.
3. **Assign an owner** to every pending estimate still in date.
4. **Close anything over ninety days old** with no contact recorded, noting the reason where you know it.
5. **Call the ten most recent** before you write any template. Ten calls will tell you which of the five reasons above dominates in your practice.
6. **Write two templates**, one for the 48 hour message and one for the expiry notice.
7. **Measure acceptance by treatment type** this month, so next month has something to be compared against.
