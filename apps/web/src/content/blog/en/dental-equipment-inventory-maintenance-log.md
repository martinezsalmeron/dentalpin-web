For every device in the practice you want two records, and they are two different documents. An **inventory** with one line per device: what it is, make and model, serial number, year, supplier and which operatory it sits in. And a **log per device**: what was done, when, by whom, with what result, and the date the next check is due.

Almost every practice has the first in some form, usually a folder of purchase invoices. Almost none has the second. And the field that gets left out of the second is the only one that makes the maintenance happen: the next-due date.

## Two records, not a drawer of invoices

Most practices keep the invoices and the job sheets the engineer leaves behind. That is neither an inventory nor a maintenance log. It is a drawer.

The difference shows up the moment a question gets specific. How old is the autoclave? When was the compressor last checked? What exactly was repaired on chair 2 last winter? With a drawer, that takes half an hour of searching, or it does not get answered.

> **The one-minute test.** Pick the most expensive device in the practice and find its serial number, the date of its last service and the name of whoever carried it out. If that takes more than a minute, you do not have equipment records, you have paperwork.

## What belongs on the inventory line

Six fields are enough, and fewer than six leaves a gap you will feel later.

- **What the device is**, in practice language: autoclave, compressor, intraoral X-ray, curing light, chair, suction pump, ultrasonic scaler.
- **Make, model and serial number.** The serial number is the key to everything else. It is what appears on a manufacturer's field safety notice, and without it you cannot tell whether the notice applies to your device.
- **Manufacturer and supplier.** Two separate fields on purpose. One is who issues the safety notices, the other is who you call when it breaks, and they are rarely the same company.
- **Year of purchase and date first put into service.** They are not always the same date, and the second one is what the service intervals run from.
- **Location.** Which operatory, which room, which site if you have more than one. A device nobody can find does not get checked.
- **Asset number, if you use one.** A short number on a sticker saves copying fourteen-digit serial numbers every time.

That last field is also the one the MHRA's own acceptance checklist asks for: when a new device arrives, "add device details, asset number and serial number on to device management records".

## What each log entry records

The log is per device and grows over the years. Each entry answers five questions.

1. **What was done.** Installation, planned service, repair, calibration, software update, removal from service.
2. **When.** The date, not the month.
3. **Who.** Name or company, with contact details. If somebody in the practice did it, that counts and gets recorded too.
4. **The result.** Passed, passed with observations, or failed. If it failed, the date it went out of use and the date it came back.
5. **When the next one is due.** A specific date, taken from the manufacturer's instructions or from what the engineer said on the way out.

The fifth is what turns a record into a system. A history answers questions about the past. The next-due date is the only thing standing between you and finding out the compressor needed attention when it stops.

## What the MHRA guidance actually says

In the UK the clearest published statement of what good looks like is the MHRA's *Managing Medical Devices*, last updated in February 2021. It is guidance rather than a statute, and it is written for health and social care organisations. It says the management structure for medical devices should have clear lines of accountability up to board level, and that those lines should be extended, where appropriate, to general practitioners, residential and care homes, community-based services, independent hospitals providing services for NHS patients, managed care providers and other independent contractors. That is the list a dental practice reads itself into.

The core of it is a short list. A practice's policy on devices should be reviewed regularly to make sure that whenever a device is used, it is suitable for its intended purpose, used in line with the manufacturer's instructions, traceable where possible, "maintained in a safe and reliable condition, with associated records kept", and disposed of appropriately at the end of its useful life.

> **Why location is on the list.** The guidance recommends keeping "a record of the current location of medical devices to facilitate a systematic approach to medical devices management and to target safety communications". That is the real reason the field exists. When a field safety notice names a model and a serial range, the location column is what turns it into a ten-minute job.

Two more details from the guidance are worth copying outright. After any service, "input all details on individual equipment record in the maintenance database", then stick on a dated SERVICED label noting the next date due and any changes to control settings. And when choosing who services your equipment, the guidance asks whether they have access to up to date and detailed maintenance records, because without them they may not be able to do the work safely.

This is not legal advice. It is what the source cited at the end says, consulted on 16 October 2026. Outside the UK the enforcing body and the written duties differ, and the practice's own regulator or inspectorate is the place to confirm them.

## What each device needs

Treating every device the same way means over-servicing some and under-servicing others.

| Device | Periodic check | Who does it | Where it is recorded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autoclave | ✓ Manufacturer's interval, plus per-cycle records | Service engineer | Device log and cycle record |
| Compressor | ✓ Manufacturer's interval | Service engineer | Device log |
| X-ray set | ✓ Outside this log, has rules of its own | Whoever your radiation rules name | Its own installation records |
| Chair and suction | ~ No fixed legal interval, manufacturer's one applies | Service engineer | Device log |
| Curing light | ~ Output check, most often skipped | The practice, with a radiometer | Device log |
| Handpieces | ✗ No individual log, maintenance still applies | The practice | Protocol, not a log per piece |

The curing light row is the one most often left off a list like this. It is the cheapest device in the table and the only one you can check yourself in under a minute with a radiometer, which is the argument for giving it a line of its own.

Two devices sit outside what this log covers, and it is worth not assuming otherwise. The X-ray set has its own rules, which the service responsible for your installation will confirm. So, often, does the compressor, because a pressure vessel can fall under separate industrial rules that vary by jurisdiction and by machine. Check both before setting an interval for them; the maintenance log does not replace either.

## Setting it up in one afternoon

1. **Walk the practice with your phone.** Room by room, photograph the rating plate on each device. Make, model and serial number are all on it, and collecting those is the actual work.
2. **Type the walk into a spreadsheet**, one row per device, with the six fields above.
3. **Find each interval** in the manufacturer's instructions. If the manual is long gone, it is almost always on the manufacturer's website.
4. **Work out the next-due date** from the last service you can document. If a device has nothing at all, its next date is this month.
5. **Put those dates in the practice schedule**, the one somebody actually looks at, not a separate list.
6. **Name one person** to keep it current. An equipment record with no owner stays accurate for about two months.

## When a device leaves, and when the practice changes hands

End of life is where most equipment records are destroyed: the old compressor goes out and its folder goes in the bin the same afternoon.

Keep the folder. Record the date it went out of service and mark the device as retired on the inventory rather than deleting the row. The MHRA guidance makes the planning case for this too: the expected life cycle of a device should be held in the inventory record and reviewed regularly against the usage, maintenance and repair record, to see whether the end date needs adjusting. That review is impossible if retired devices vanish.

There is a commercial reason as well, and it is the sharpest one. The guidance says usage and service history should always be available to a prospective purchaser before sale and supplied with the device, listing as a minimum a record of any reconditioning work including replacement parts, a copy of all maintenance and servicing carried out with the name of the servicing organisation, a record of the type and frequency of usage over its working life, a fault log and a decontamination record. Sell a practice with a drawer of invoices and a buyer discounts the price by exactly what they cannot verify.

## Paper, spreadsheet or software

A single-chair practice runs this in a spreadsheet without difficulty, and a spreadsheet satisfies the guidance as well as any program does. Nothing in *Managing Medical Devices* specifies a medium, and anyone selling you software on the grounds that your records are otherwise non-compliant has not read it.

What changes with size is not compliance, it is whether the dates are met. With four operatories, two autoclaves and two sites, a spreadsheet nobody opens stops warning anybody.
