The patient in pain who rings at nine can only be seen today if the slot was already protected before the phone rang. Two held slots a day, one mid-morning and one late afternoon, cover the ordinary demand of a one or two surgery practice. What decides whether it works is not the slot: it is who assesses the call, and what survives in writing afterwards, whether or not the patient turns up.

That last part is the one almost nobody has solved. A call that ends in "take some ibuprofen and we will see you tomorrow" is clinical advice given over the phone, and if it is not in the notes it did not happen.

> **This is not about the medical emergency in the chair.** Collapse, anaphylaxis, hypoglycaemia and the emergency drugs kit are a separate subject with separate sources and separate training behind them. This post is about the person ringing from home in pain, not the one deteriorating while you treat them.

## The slot is held in advance, not found afterwards

A diary that is full at nine has no gaps at eleven. If urgent care is squeezed in "wherever it fits", what actually happens is that it goes on top of an existing appointment and the delay runs to the end of the day.

Holding the slot means making the decision once, calmly, instead of making it every morning under pressure. Three rules that survive contact with a real week:

- **One per surgery per session**, not one per day. A practice working mornings and afternoons needs cover in both.
- **Not first thing and not last thing.** Nobody has rung yet at nine, and by the final appointment there is no room left if the case turns out to be more than a filling.
- **Short and honest**, twenty to thirty minutes. The slot exists to diagnose and relieve pain, not to start definitive treatment.

**If the slot is still empty by mid-morning, release it.** That is the rule that makes the whole thing sustainable. A protected slot nobody is allowed to fill is an idle chair, and within a fortnight the team quietly stops respecting it. Give it an expiry time and fill the space from the cancellation list.

## What the GDC requires you to have arranged

Two of the GDC's *Standards for the Dental Team* sit directly on this. Standard 2.3.9 is the one most often missed:

> **"You must provide patients with clear information about your arrangements for emergency care including the out of hours arrangements."** GDC, *Standards for the Dental Team*, paragraph 2.3.9. Paragraph 2.3.10 adds that you should make sure patients have the details they need to contact you by their preferred method.

"Must" in the GDC standards is not advisory. It means the arrangement has to exist and the patient has to know about it before they need it: on the answerphone message, on the website, on the practice door.

The record-keeping side is standard 4.1.1, "You must make and keep complete and accurate patient records, including an up-to-date medical history, each time that you treat patients", with 4.1.5 adding that any later amendment "must" be clearly marked up and dated. A triage note written the following morning is fine. A triage note written the following morning and backdated is not.

One more that changes how out-of-hours cover is staffed: standard 6.2.2 says you should work with another appropriately trained member of the dental team at all times when treating patients, and it names "treating patients in an out of hours emergency" as one of the few circumstances in which that does not apply.

## Who may decide on the phone and who may not

Reception can and should filter: take the reason, check whether the caller is a patient of the practice, ask how long it has hurt and what they have taken. What reception cannot do is decide that something is not urgent.

The clean way to separate the two is to write down what goes straight to a dentist, so that the person answering is following a rule rather than making a judgement. For the clinical layer of that rule, use a published algorithm rather than an in-house list: SDCEP's *Management of Acute Dental Problems*, second edition March 2026, publishes decision support flowcharts explicitly written for staff in any healthcare setting, which is precisely the non-dentist-answering-the-phone case.

| What the caller describes | Who assesses it | When they are seen |
|---|---|---|
| Facial swelling with fever, difficulty swallowing or difficulty breathing | Dentist, on the call | ✗ Not an appointment: same-day referral to hospital |
| Severe pain not relieved by the patient's usual painkillers | ✓ Dentist | ✓ Today, in the held slot |
| Significant bleeding after an extraction or surgery | ✓ Dentist | ✓ Today |
| Trauma to the mouth or face with severe pain | ✓ Dentist | ✓ Today |
| Crown, bridge or temporary off, no pain | Reception, against a written rule | ~ Next available appointment |
| Fractured tooth with no pain | Reception, against a written rule | ~ Next available appointment |
| Broken denture | Reception, against a written rule | ~ Next available appointment |

The last three rows are genuinely annoying for the patient and it is reasonable to see them soon if there is room. What they are not is a reason to displace someone in pain.

## The call, step by step

1. **Find the patient in the system before discussing anything else.** If they are a patient of the practice you have their medical alerts, what was done last time and whether a course of treatment is open. If they are not, create the record now.
2. **Take the reason in the caller's own words**, not yours. "It has been throbbing since Thursday and I could not sleep" carries more than "toothache".
3. **Ask the questions that change the decision**: how long, any swelling, any fever, what they have taken and whether it helped.
4. **Pass anything in the top four rows to a dentist**, and anything the person answering cannot confidently classify. Uncertainty is a referral criterion, not a failure.
5. **Give an appointment or give an alternative.** If there is nothing today, the caller should hang up knowing where to go, not holding a "try us tomorrow".
6. **Write the note before the next call, not at the end of the day.** Two hours later nobody remembers who said what.

## What gets recorded when nobody attends

- **Date, time and who took the call**, and whether a dentist was involved.
- **The complaint in the patient's words**, plus the answers to the screening questions.
- **The decision and the reason for it**: seen today, booked Thursday, sent to the out-of-hours service.
- **Any advice given**, including self-care advice and anything said about painkillers.
- **What happened next**: attended, did not attend, cancelled, could not be reached.

> **A call that led nowhere is still a record.** The patient told to ring back tomorrow, who never rang back, is exactly the case where six months later nobody can say what they were told. That note protects the patient first and the practice second.

## Fitting an urgent patient in without wrecking the day

When the held slot is already taken and a second genuine urgency arrives, there are three ways out and it pays to have chosen between them beforehand:

- **The other session's slot.** If the call comes at ten and the morning slot is gone, the afternoon one is still there.
- **Time freed by a cancellation.** This is where held slots and [the cancellation list](/en/blog/dental-cancellation-list/) touch, and they are different things: the list fills a hole that has already opened, the held slot protects one before it is needed.
- **Moving one of your own appointments, never a colleague's.** Shifting someone else's check-up without asking creates two problems instead of one.

What does not work is "squeeze them in and see". A twenty minute urgent appointment forced into no space takes forty and leaves the last patient of the day waiting.

## What you can measure next month

- **How many held slots went unused**, and at what time. If the afternoon one is never used, it is one too many; if the morning one fills every day, it is one too few.
- **How many urgent calls found no space that day.** This is the number that tells you whether the practice is turning away people in pain.
- **How many urgent attendances became an accepted treatment plan.** A new patient who arrives in pain and leaves out of pain is the highest-intent first appointment in the whole diary.
- **How far the day overran on urgent days** compared with days without.

## What the software has to be able to do

- **Hold a slot per surgery per session** as its own block type, not as a fake appointment named "EMERGENCY".
- **Release that block automatically** at a set time.
- **Open a call record from the patient's file** without having to create an appointment first.
- **Keep that record when there is no visit**, with the decision and any advice given.
- **Show medical alerts on the same screen** the call is being taken on.
- **Count used and unused slots** without exporting anything to a spreadsheet.
