The cost of dental software is not the monthly fee. It is the fee times thirty-six, plus setup, plus migration, plus the modules billed separately, plus hosting, plus the hours your team loses in the first month. That total is the only number two vendors can be compared on, and it almost never appears on the quote they send you.

The fee is the easy part to find out and the part that decides least. What decides is the unit you are counted in, what sits outside the price, and what leaving costs.

## The billing unit matters more than the rate

Two vendors quoting the same rate can end up costing twice as much as each other, depending on what they count. Ask that before you ask the number.

| Unit | When it is cheap | When it spikes | Predictable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Per practice | Larger team, several chairs | Almost never | ✓ Yes |
| Per chair | One operatory, two clinicians | The day you open the next one | ~ If you have a growth plan |
| Per user | Two or three people in total | Hygienist, front desk, locums | ✗ Changes with every hire |
| Per clinician | A single-dentist practice | Part-time associates | ~ Depends who they count |
| Per patient volume | The first few months | Exactly when the practice is doing well | ✗ Rises with success |

> **The same rate per user and per practice is not the same rate.** With six people on the system, $30 per user is $180 a month and $6,480 over three years. Ask for the total using the headcount you expect in two years, not today's.

## The lines that are not in the fee

- **Setup and onboarding.** A one-off charge at the start, which sometimes covers configuration and sometimes only opening the account. Get in writing what it actually includes.
- **Data migration.** Almost always billed separately, and the price depends on what you are coming from and whether your current vendor hands the data over in a usable format.
- **Modules.** Billing, reminders, imaging, digital signature, patient portal and reporting are often sold on top. Ask for the closed list of what is not included.
- **Support.** Sometimes in the fee, sometimes a tier above it with different hours and different response times.
- **Hosting.** With software installed at the practice, the server is yours to pay for. In a cloud product it is usually inside the fee, but confirm it rather than assume it.
- **Backups.** That they exist does not always mean they are included, or that you can restore them yourself.
- **Hardware.** Computers, screens at the chair, sometimes a server. Real money even though the software vendor does not invoice it.
- **Training.** Rarely charged, always paid: in hours from a team that treats patients more slowly that day.
- **Getting out.** Some contracts charge for the final export. Asking before you sign costs one email.

## The three-year sum, step by step

1. **Multiply the fee by thirty-six.** Three years is the fair horizon, because switching sooner rarely pays for itself.
2. **Add setup and migration**, the two one-off charges that only show up on the first invoice.
3. **Add the modules you will genuinely use**, also multiplied by thirty-six.
4. **Add hosting and backups**, whether the vendor bills them or you contract them yourself.
5. **Count training in hours** and price them at what an hour of your team costs.
6. **Apply the annual increase** the contract allows. If it does not say, that silence is your answer.
7. **Divide the result by your chairs and your working days.** That number is the one you can actually compare.

Worked through: $130 a month is $4,680 over three years. Two modules at $25 add another $1,800. Setup at $700 and migration at $500 add $1,200 more. Total $7,680, and the conversation started at $130.

> **Compare three-year totals, never monthly fees.** It is the only way a vendor with a low fee and six paid modules ends up next to one with a higher fee and everything included, which is exactly the comparison you need to make.

## What counts as expensive, measured against your own schedule

A cost is only expensive relative to what it produces. The same amount is dear in a single-chair practice working half days and cheap in a two-chair practice running full.

![Monthly schedule report: 22 appointments, completion rate, no-show rate, new patients, hours worked per clinician and utilisation per chair](/screenshots/reports.png)

*Hours worked and utilisation for each chair over a month. These are the divisors in the sum, and without them a fee is just a loose number.*

Divide your full monthly cost by the days you open and by the chairs you run. A total of $210 a month in a two-chair practice open twenty days is $5.25 per chair per day.

That is the figure to hold against what one chair bills in a day. If the two are orders of magnitude apart, price was never the real argument.

## What "free" actually means

- **A free tier with limits** is usually capped by patients, users or features. Look at what happens the day you pass the cap, because that day is the real price.
- **Open source software** has no licence to pay for, but it still needs a server, backups and updates.
- **Free with migration charged separately** is an entry price, not a cost. Go back to the three-year sum.

> **Free and no-cost are different things.** Open source saves you the licence, not the server and not the hours. The honest move is to put those lines in the same table as anyone else's fee, and then compare.

## What makes cheap software expensive

The fee is easy to see and usually the small part. The money that actually leaves never appears on a vendor invoice.

- **Minutes per task.** Two extra minutes on every payment, twenty payments a day, is more than a hundred hours a year.
- **Downtime during surgery hours.** A morning with no schedule and no clinical records costs you that morning's billing.
- **What goes uncollected.** Software that does not show you what is outstanding is software that lets it stay outstanding.
- **The exit.** If the data only comes out as PDFs, the cost of switching in three years is already agreed today.
