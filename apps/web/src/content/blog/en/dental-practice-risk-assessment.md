If your practice employs anyone, a risk assessment has to exist, and in two of the three common cases it has to exist on paper. In Britain the duty comes from regulation 3 of the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999, and the written record is required once you employ five or more people. In the United States the document a dental office is actually inspected on is the written Exposure Control Plan, which has no headcount threshold at all and must be reviewed every year. The two regimes agree on the duty and disagree on almost everything about the paperwork.

> **The most expensive mistake is assuming the review is annual.** It is annual in the United States and it is not annual in Britain: regulation 3 sets no interval, only triggers. A practice that reviews once a year and nothing else has a gap every time it buys an autoclave, hires a trainee or reconfigures a surgery, and that gap is exactly what an inspector asks about.

## What the British regulation actually says

Regulation 3(1) of the 1999 Regulations is the whole duty, and it is worth reading in full rather than in summary:

> **"Every employer shall make a suitable and sufficient assessment of (a) the risks to the health and safety of his employees to which they are exposed whilst they are at work; and (b) the risks to the health and safety of persons not in his employment arising out of or in connection with the conduct by him of his undertaking, for the purpose of identifying the measures he needs to take to comply with the requirements and prohibitions imposed upon him by or under the relevant statutory provisions."**

Three things follow from that wording, and each one matters in a practice:

- **It covers patients and visitors, not just staff.** Paragraph (b) is about "persons not in his employment", which in a dental practice means everyone who walks through reception.
- **It is not an assessment for its own sake.** The purpose clause ties it to "identifying the measures he needs to take", so an assessment that identifies nothing to do is either a very unusual practice or an assessment that was not done.
- **"Suitable and sufficient" is the standard.** The regulation does not prescribe a form, a template or a length, which is why no two practices' assessments look alike and why buying a template is not the same as doing one.

## When the written record becomes compulsory

Regulation 3(6) is the threshold that decides whether yours has to be on paper:

> **"Where the employer employs five or more employees, he shall record (a) the significant findings of the assessment; and (b) any group of his employees identified by it as being especially at risk."**

Two readings of that are wrong and both are common. The first is that a four-person practice has no duty: it has the full assessment duty under paragraph (1), it simply is not required to write it down. The second is that the record is everything you considered. It is the **significant findings**, plus the groups especially at risk, which in a dental setting is usually where the pregnant member of staff, the trainee and the person who handles the sharps end up named.

## When you have to review it

Regulation 3(3) sets the triggers, and there are only two:

1. **There is reason to suspect the assessment is no longer valid.** An incident, a near miss, a complaint from the team, a change in guidance.
2. **There has been a significant change in the matters to which it relates.** New equipment, a new procedure, building work, a new role.

The paragraph then adds the step people skip: "where as a result of any such review changes to an assessment are required, the employer or relevant self-employed person concerned shall make them". Reviewing and concluding that something needs fixing does not discharge the duty. Changing the assessment does.

Regulation 3(4) and (5) add a separate trigger that applies to a great many practices: you may not employ a young person until you have made or reviewed an assessment taking particular account of their inexperience, the layout of the workplace and workstation, the nature and duration of exposure to physical, biological and chemical agents, the work equipment and how it is handled, and the health and safety training provided. Taking on a trainee dental nurse is a review event by law.

## The American document is a different document

In the United States there is no single general risk-assessment regulation of the British kind. The paperwork a dental office is held to is the Exposure Control Plan required by the OSHA bloodborne pathogens standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030(c)(1). It is written, it binds "each employer having an employee(s) with occupational exposure" with no headcount qualifier attached, and its rules are tighter in three specific ways:

- **It must be reviewed and updated at least annually and whenever necessary**, including when tasks, procedures or job classifications with occupational exposure change.
- **It must document, every year, the consideration and implementation of safer medical devices.** Looking at safer sharps is not enough, writing down that you looked at them is the requirement.
- **It must record input from the people doing the work.** The employer has to solicit input from non-managerial employees responsible for direct patient care in identifying, evaluating and selecting engineering and work practice controls, and that solicitation has to be documented in the plan.

A copy also has to be accessible to employees, and available to the Assistant Secretary and the Director on request.

| | UK, regulation 3 MHSWR 1999 | US, 29 CFR 1910.1030(c)(1) |
|---|---|---|
| Written document required | ~ Only at five or more employees | ✓ Always |
| Fixed review interval | ✗ None in the regulation | ✓ At least annually |
| Review on significant change | ✓ Yes | ✓ Yes |
| Staff input documented | ✗ Not required by regulation 3 | ✓ Required |
| Accessible to employees | ✗ Not stated in regulation 3 | ✓ Required |
| Scope | ✓ All risks, staff and visitors | ✗ Bloodborne pathogens only |

> **Neither document covers the other one's ground.** A British practice with a good assessment still has nothing written about annual sharps review, and an American office with a model Exposure Control Plan has nothing written about the receptionist's back, the wet floor or the autoclave room. If you work across both, you need both, and they are not merged by stapling them together.

## Getting it in order this week

1. **Find the document and read its date.** If nobody knows where it is, that is the finding, and it is the one an inspector reaches first.
2. **Check it is written per role, not per practice.** Dentist, hygienist, nurse, reception and decontamination are different exposures doing different work.
3. **Count your employees.** Five is the line in Britain for the written record, and crossing it quietly is how practices end up with a duty nobody noticed.
4. **Name the groups especially at risk.** Pregnancy, trainees, anyone with a relevant health condition, anyone handling sharps.
5. **Write down the trigger list and give it an owner.** New equipment, new role, building work, any incident. A trigger nobody is watching for is not a system.
6. **If you are in the United States, diarise the annual review with the sharps evaluation attached**, because the documentation of that evaluation is the part most often missing.
