A survey people answer has two questions: one closed question about how the visit went, and one open box where they write whatever they want. It goes out a day or two after the appointment, through the same channel that reminded them to come, and it takes under a minute on a phone. The ten questions on a one-to-five scale, the PDF attachment and the "this will only take five minutes" are precisely what stops anyone answering.

What follows is what to ask word for word, when to send it, what to do with the answers, and why chasing a high response rate is the most expensive mistake available here.

## The annual questionnaire arrives too late to fix anything

NHS England collects patient feedback from a single question, the Friends and Family Test, rolled out to services between 2013 and 2015, and publishes the results monthly. Its own guidance separates that mechanic from the traditional survey, and the distinction is what decides whether your survey is worth running.

In its words, the FFT is "a continuous feedback stream, it is not a one-off feedback opportunity or a traditional survey". The big questionnaire gives you a snapshot. The continuous question gives you a time series.

| | Long annual questionnaire | One question after every visit |
|---|---|---|
| What it produces | A snapshot of one moment | ✓ A series you can track month to month |
| When the information lands | ✗ Months after the visit | ✓ The same day |
| Who answers | A representative sample | ✓ Anyone who wants to |
| Benchmarking against others | ✓ Yes, with controlled method | ✗ Not what it is for |
| Fixing something this week | ✗ No | ✓ Yes |

> **The two do different jobs rather than competing.** If you need a figure that stands up to an outside body, you need a methodologically controlled survey. If you need to find out that the waiting room has been at thirty degrees for three weeks, you need the continuous question.

For a two or three chair practice, the second one is what survives contact with a normal week, because it does not depend on anyone clearing a fortnight to run it.

## The two questions that do all the work

The English guidance sets one mandatory question for every setting, dental included, preceded by "Thinking about your dental practice…": *Overall, how was your experience of our service?* The response scale is fixed at six options: very good, good, neither good nor poor, poor, very poor, and don't know.

The familiar alternative is NPS, created by Bain & Company, which asks how likely you are to recommend the business to a friend or colleague and scores it from zero to ten.

| | Experience scale (NHS) | NPS (Bain & Company) |
|---|---|---|
| The question | Overall, how was your experience of our service? | How likely are you to recommend us to a friend or colleague? |
| The answers | Six options, very good to very poor, plus don't know | 0 to 10 |
| How it is summarised | Distribution across options, no average | Promoters (9 and 10) minus detractors (0 to 6) |
| What it leaves out | Nothing, every answer counts | ~ The 7s and 8s, which Bain calls passives |
| What it is good for | ✓ Tracking your own practice over time | ✓ A single number anyone understands |

Bain defines the bands without ambiguity: promoters score 9 or 10, passives 7 and 8, detractors 0 through 6, and the score is the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors. Passives are excluded from the calculation.

- **Pick one and leave it alone.** The value of either is the comparison with your own last month, and changing the scale erases the history.
- **The six-option scale is easier to answer.** Nobody has to decide whether their visit was a 7 or an 8.
- **NPS travels better outside the practice.** It is a single number, a partner or a lender understands it, and it comes with vocabulary that already exists.
- **Neither of them tells you what to fix.** The free-text box does that.

The guidance requires at least one free-text question alongside the fixed one, and recommends this pair: "Please can you tell us why you gave your answer?" and "Please tell us about anything that we could have done better." It warns on the same page that the format is designed to be quick and simple, and that adding too many questions may put people off.

> **The free-text answers are the most important part.** The guidance says so in those words, and it matches what happens in a practice: an average score has never changed a protocol, and "I was told the price when I was already in the chair" has.

## When to send it, and why the same minute is not best

The guidance frames the trade-off precisely: balance giving people time to recover and reflect against the fact that if there is too long a lag, they will not respond or their recollection will fade.

In dentistry that has a concrete meaning. An extraction does not get rated fairly ten minutes afterwards, with a numb mouth and the adrenaline still up. The next morning it does.

- **Twenty-four to forty-eight hours** after the visit for routine treatment.
- **Through the appointment reminder channel**, the one the patient already recognises as yours. The guidance itself allows sending the question by text message after an appointment or treatment, provided it is anonymous and available to all.
- **One message, no chaser.** People who ignore the first request ignore the third one too, and the third one irritates.
- **No account, no password.** Every screen between the link and the answer costs you a share of the responses.
- **Never after a painful emergency** or bad clinical news. That conversation is a phone call, not a form.

## Response rate is the wrong target

This is the part that surprises everyone, and it comes from the organisation that has run this at national scale for over a decade.

NHS England stopped calculating and publishing an FFT response rate. Its guidance is explicit: there is no target or requirement around the number of responses to collect, and it recommends focusing reporting on what feedback has been collected and what has been done with it, rather than on response rates and scores.

> **Chasing a high rate works against you.** The guidance says it plainly: collecting too many responses can be overwhelming, managing them is resource intensive, and chasing very high response rates can mean having too much feedback to analyse effectively.

Forty answers a month that somebody genuinely reads beat four hundred sitting in a dashboard. If nobody in the practice has half an hour a month to read them, the response rate is not your problem.

## Asking for reviews is fine, choosing who you ask is not

Two things get mixed up constantly, so separate them: the survey is for you, the review is for the person who is not a patient yet.

Google's content policy is clear in both directions. It prohibits incentivized reviews, which it defines as reviews influenced by a payment, discount, free goods or services or any other benefit offered to the reviewer, and reviews biased by a personal or professional conflict of interest. It also expressly allows a business to solicit or encourage content that represents a genuine experience, without offering incentives.

- **A prize draw for reviewers is covered.** The policy names any other benefit offered to the reviewer, and an entry in a draw is one.
- **Reviews from staff or family are too.** They fall under the conflict of interest the policy names.
- **Asking everyone, with no filter, is the permitted version.** It is also the only version that leaves your own average meaning something.
- **Screening for who looks happy breaks your measurement.** Ask only the patients who smile on the way out and your monthly series measures the mood at the front desk, not the practice.

A clean sequence: the survey goes to everyone, and the review link appears after they answer, the same for everyone, never conditional on the score.

## What to do with the answers

1. **Triage on the day.** Anything mentioning pain, a safety issue or a clinical error leaves the list and becomes a phone call.
2. **Get the rest to whoever treated them.** The English guidance notes that giving feedback directly and quickly to the staff providing the service makes acting on it more effective.
3. **Read the free text end to end once a month.** It takes twenty minutes and it is where the patterns are.
4. **Group it into three or four themes**, not twenty tags. Waiting times, price explained too late, phone nobody answers, and not much else.
5. **Pick one theme a month and change it.** Two changes at once can be attributed to nothing.
6. **Say what you did.** The format the guidance recommends is "you said, we did". It is the thing that gets the next survey answered.

## What should never end up in the survey

- **Clinical data.** The survey asks about the experience, not the diagnosis. If the tool belongs to a third party, every health detail you send it is an added problem with no upside.
- **Identity, where you can avoid it.** The FFT premise is that anyone can give anonymous feedback quickly and easily, whenever they want. Anonymous gets answered more, and answered more honestly.
- **Comments published without permission.** The guidance is blunt: patients must be able to opt out of having their comments published even though they are anonymous, and any comment that identifies an individual must be anonymised before publication.
- **Photos of handwritten slips.** The same guidance advises against publishing scanned copies of handwritten comments because of the risk of identifying the author.
- **A mass send nobody checked.** Its information governance checklist asks exactly that: if you contact patients proactively to invite feedback, have you checked that your data security arrangements meet the legal requirements and that people can opt out?

## Setting it up in a week

1. **Choose the scale**, six options or NPS, and write it down once so it never gets rewritten.
2. **Write the two open questions** and keep them short. The pair recommended by the English guidance works as it stands.
3. **Decide the send moment**, one or two days after, and which visits are excluded.
4. **Use the reminder channel** and time yourself answering it on your own phone. Over a minute means something has to go.
5. **Name who reads the answers and when.** With a person and a day of the month, or it does not happen.
6. **Write the rule for urgent answers** before the first one arrives.
7. **Block twenty minutes on the first Monday of the month** to read, pick one theme and publish the "you said, we did".
