An orthodontic case gets lost for four reasons, and none of them is clinical: nobody knows which phase it is in today, nobody compares the planned end date against today's date, the patient leaves the chair without the next appointment booked, and retention goes unmonitored because the case has been marked complete. Track those four things and the rest of the follow-up looks after itself.

What makes orthodontics different from everything else in the practice is the length of it. A root canal opens and closes in two visits, and anything forgotten shows up immediately. An orthodontic case outlasts the average tenure of a front desk coordinator.

## A case is ten to thirteen visits, not one

It helps to have the numbers in front of you before designing any tracking, because they explain why this is a systems problem rather than a memory problem.

The NHS describes fixed braces as a treatment that stays in place for "1 to 2 years" and puts adjustment visits at "every 6 to 8 weeks". The American Association of Orthodontists gives a similar range, "12-24 months", with adjustments "every 4-12 weeks, depending on where you are in your treatment".

Do the arithmetic on an eighteen-month case at six to eight week intervals and you get ten to thirteen adjustment visits, plus the initial records, plus debond, plus retention. Every one of those is a chance for the case to fall off the schedule.

> **No orthodontic appointment is the last one.** While a case is active, a visit is only properly closed when the next one is already booked, before the patient leaves. An active case with no next appointment is not a stable case, it is a lost case that does not look lost yet.

## What to record when the case opens

Diagnosis day decides most of the follow-up that comes after it. These are the fields people wish they had two years later:

- **The initial records**: intraoral and extraoral photographs, a panoramic radiograph, a lateral cephalogram where one is taken, and the study model or scan. They are the only reference you can measure the finished result against.
- **The diagnosis and the treatment objective**, written in a sentence that still makes sense on rereading. "Class II division 1" does not say what you were trying to achieve.
- **The planned phases and how long each one runs.** A case with two phases separated by a year of waiting needs that wait to live in the system, not in somebody's head.
- **The planned completion date.** It is the field almost nobody fills in, and the only one that lets you detect later that a case has overrun.
- **The appliance**: type, brand and product reference. If a component needs replacing, or there is a problem with a batch, that field saves opening the whole record.
- **The financial agreement**: total, number of instalments, how often, and what happens if treatment runs long or stops early.
- **The signed consent**, dated, plus a note that the expected duration and the retention phase were both explained.

## What to record at every adjustment

The temptation here is to write almost nothing, because the visit takes ten minutes and there are three patients waiting. The useful minimum fits in four lines:

1. **What was done**: archwire placed, ligature changes, rebonds, elastics issued and in what configuration.
2. **What the patient was given** and what they were asked to do, particularly anything that depends on their compliance.
3. **Problems**: debonded brackets, breakages, oral hygiene, early decalcification. A case with three debonds in two months is a case that is going to run long, and it is worth seeing that written down before it happens.
4. **The interval to the next visit**, not just the date. It is a small difference with a large consequence: if the system knows this case runs at six weeks, it can flag the patient who has gone nine without appearing. If it only knows a date, it can flag nothing.

## Where the tracking lives

The three usual ways of running a long case behave very differently after eighteen months and a change of front desk staff.

| | Paper ortho card | Separate spreadsheet | Treatment plan in the system |
|---|---|---|---|
| See the whole case on one screen | ✓ If it is all in the folder | ✗ It sits outside the record | ✓ Next to the rest of the record |
| List active cases with no next appointment | ✗ Folder by folder | ~ If someone keeps it current | ✓ It is a query |
| Spot cases past their planned end | ✗ | ✓ Comparing two columns | ✓ If the planned date was saved |
| Survives a change of reception staff | ~ If the handwriting holds up | ✗ Usually lives on one desktop | ✓ It is in the system |
| Monitor retention after discharge | ✗ The case gets filed away | ~ If you add another tab | ✓ If retention is another phase |
| Cost to set up | ✓ None | ✓ An afternoon | ~ Configure the plan once |

The spreadsheet is not a ridiculous answer. It solves the two hard questions, which are who has overrun and who has no next appointment, and plenty of practices have run that way for years. Its problem is that it lives outside the clinical record and depends on somebody remembering to write in it.

## The three ways a case gets lost

None of these is a clinical error. All three are scheduling.

1. **The patient leaves without the next appointment.** It is busy, they say they will ring, and they do not. Four months later they turn up with an active appliance that nobody has checked in all that time.
2. **The case runs long and nobody compares.** Each visit on its own looks normal. Nobody sits down to ask which cases planned to finish in March are still open in October, because answering that means opening records one at a time.
3. **Retention is orphaned.** Debond day is a celebration, the final photographs get taken, the patient is discharged, and the case drops off every list at exactly the point where teeth can start to move.

The third is the most expensive of the three, because the patient has already paid and the result is lost anyway.

## Retention is a phase, not a goodbye

The NHS puts it about as briefly as it can be put: once the braces come off you need a retainer, "which is a device that stops your teeth moving back to their original position", and it adds that "the orthodontist will tell you how long you need to wear the retainer for".

That last sentence is what breaks tracking systems. If the duration is decided case by case, it has to be stored case by case, and somebody has to look at it.

> **A case in retention is still an open case.** If discharge removes the patient from every list, the practice has thrown away the only tool it had for checking that the result holds. Retention needs its own booked appointment, exactly like a check-up recall.

In practice that means three concrete things: retention carries a review date like any other appointment, the retainer type and what was fitted is recorded, and what the patient was told about how long to wear it is written down.

## The money runs for two years too

A twenty-four month payment plan is a longer commitment than many of the practice's own contracts, and most software treats it like an ordinary invoice.

- **The instalment has to attach to the case**, not just to the patient. When a sibling starts treatment, two payment plans against one family are easy to confuse.
- **You need to know at any moment how much is still outstanding on each active case**, because that figure is what decides whether extending a treatment is worth it.
- **Write down what happens if treatment stops early**, before it stops early. A patient who moves away mid-case is a far easier conversation when the agreement already covered it.
- **A case that runs six months long usually does not bill six months more.** Watching for overruns is not only clinical governance, it is margin.

## A monthly checklist

All of the above reduces to four queries somebody has to look at once a month, on paper or on screen:

1. **Active cases with no next appointment booked.**
2. **Cases past their planned completion date.**
3. **Cases that have gone longer than usual without attending**, measured against their own interval.
4. **Patients in retention with no review scheduled.**

If those four lists come back empty, the tracking works. If they have to be assembled by hand every month, they will stop being looked at by March.
