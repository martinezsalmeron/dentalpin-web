A dental practice produces three waste streams and only one of them can go out with the general rubbish. The rest needs a registered carrier, a container that holds, and a written record you keep for two years if the waste is non-hazardous and three years if it is hazardous. The bin is the easy part. The part practices lose is the paper: the note for each collection is the first thing an inspector asks for and the thing almost nobody has in full.

This is not legal advice. It is what the official sources cited at the end say, consulted on 11 October 2026.

> **This post is anchored in UK law**, because "clinical waste" is a UK term and the duty of care sits in section 34 of the Environmental Protection Act 1990. If you practise elsewhere, the shape of the problem is the same and the retention periods are not: check your own regulator before you throw anything away.

This is not the amalgam article. Mercury has its own stream, its own separator and its own ban, and it lives in [the amalgam ban and its waste records](/en/blog/dental-amalgam-ban-waste-records/). Everything else is here.

## The three streams, and which is which

The classification that matters day to day is not the catalogue code, it is the operational one: which bin, who takes it, what paper is left behind.

- **General waste.** Paper, packaging, and anything that never touched a patient or is not contaminated with blood or body fluids. This goes out with the ordinary commercial collection.
- **Clinical waste.** Sharps (needles, blades, burs, files) and material soaked with blood or fluids. Rigid closed container, registered carrier.
- **Chemical and hazardous waste.** Developer and fixer if any analogue radiography remains, disinfectant concentrates, and anything with a safety data sheet. Not down the drain.

The trap is the first one. Most of what leaves a surgery is not clinical waste, and paying to incinerate it as though it were is the expensive mistake in the opposite direction to the one everybody warns about.

## What the duty of care actually requires

The duty sits in section 34(1) of the Environmental Protection Act 1990, and the statutory Code of Practice is issued under section 34(7). It asks you to "take all reasonable steps" to do five things: prevent unauthorised deposit, treatment or disposal; prevent a permit breach; prevent the waste escaping; transfer it only to an authorised person; and describe it accurately.

Two of those five are where practices come unstuck, and both are checkable in an afternoon.

> **"Authorised person" is a thing you verify, not a thing you assume.** The Code says you should check whether a business is authorised before you transfer waste to them. In England that is the Environment Agency public register or 03708 506 506; in Wales, Natural Resources Wales or 0300 065 3000. A quote on headed paper is not a registration.

The second is the description. The waste has to be classified with the right code from the List of Wastes, and the written description has to be agreed and signed by you and the next holder. It also carries the quantity and nature of the waste, the container type, the time and place of transfer, your SIC code, both parties' names, addresses and authorisation numbers, and a statement about the waste hierarchy.

## Passing the waste on does not pass on the duty

The Code is explicit that the obligation follows the waste rather than stopping at your door. You take reasonable steps to see it managed correctly "throughout its complete journey to disposal or recovery", and where dealers, brokers or preliminary treatment are involved, "each holder in the waste chain shares the duty of care obligations".

That single sentence decides how you choose a contractor. Signing with the cheapest and never looking again does not move the problem, because you stay in the chain. What protects you is a complete, dated record of every handover.

## What to ask a contractor before you sign

1. **The registration, with its number.** A registered carrier, broker or dealer appears on the public register. The number gets checked, not assumed.
2. **Which streams the contract covers.** A clinical waste contract does not cover chemical waste, and finding that out on the day a drum of fixer needs to go is late.
3. **The collection frequency, in writing.** It has to fit how fast you fill containers, not how the round happens to run.
4. **Who supplies the containers, and in what size.** If the contractor supplies them it is in the price; if you do, they still have to meet the standard.
5. **Which document you get at each collection.** That is what you file for two or three years. If the answer is "we send a summary annually", that is not a note per transfer.
6. **What happens when a collection is missed.** Waste sitting past its interval because the lorry did not arrive is still your problem.

## Storage, in practice

The place where containers wait between the surgery and the collection is the most improvised part of the whole process, and the part an inspection looks at.

- **Away from patient routes and clean stock.** Not the cupboard that also holds consumables.
- **Containers closed, not "nearly full".** A sharps bin left open because one more thing fits is an injury waiting to happen.
- **Never straight on the floor.** It is the detail that takes thirty seconds to fix and goes unfixed for years.
- **With the date visible.** If you cannot say when a container was closed, you cannot show you met any interval at all.

A collection is a recurring appointment, and treating it as one is what prevents the month nobody remembered. It belongs in the team's diary with a named owner, the same as an equipment service.

## What you have to be able to produce

An inspection does not ask for opinions, it asks for documents. These are the ones that need to exist and be filed together:

- **The contract** with a registered carrier, current, covering the streams you actually produce.
- **The transfer notes**, for the last two years.
- **The consignment notes** for hazardous waste, for the last three.
- **The waste descriptions and codes** that go with them.

The rest of the folder an inspection works through is in [getting ready for a practice inspection](/en/blog/dental-practice-inspection-readiness/), where waste is one line on a longer list.

Keeping this outside your practice software is not impossible, just fragile: a shared folder survives until the person maintaining it leaves. In [Dentalpin](/en/pricing/) the collection notes are filed with their dates and export filtered by period, which is the form an inspector asks for them in.
