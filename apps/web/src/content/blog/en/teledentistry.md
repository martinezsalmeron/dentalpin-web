Remotely you can triage an emergency, follow up a post-operative patient, walk someone through a treatment plan they never understood, and check on an orthodontic case between appointments. You cannot diagnose caries or periodontal disease, because that needs a probe, good light, a dry field and radiographs. Teledentistry is a filter and a follow-up channel, not a cheaper version of the appointment.

Framed that way, the question stops being whether it is allowed and becomes what decision comes out of each contact. A remote contact that does not end in a recorded decision is not a clinical act, it is a conversation.

## What it genuinely settles

These are the situations where the missing information is not inside the mouth. It is in the patient's head or already in their record.

- **Emergency triage.** Telling a spreading swelling that needs a chair today from a sensitivity that will keep until Tuesday. A three minute call organises the day better than an emergency slot booked blind.
- **Post-operative follow-up.** The day after an extraction or a surgical case, the only real question is whether healing is going as expected. Looking and listening answers it, and saves a sore patient a journey.
- **Explaining a treatment plan.** A patient who has not accepted a plan has often not refused it, they have not understood it. Going through the document on a video call converts better than a call from reception.
- **Orthodontics and aligners between reviews.** Checking wear compliance, dealing with a debonded attachment, deciding whether the next appointment needs pulling forward.
- **A second opinion between clinicians.** The case travels better than the patient. Sharing radiographs and photographs with a surgeon or an endodontist before referring can save a whole visit.
- **Pre-appointment history taking.** Medication, allergies, medical history and the reason for attending, collected before the visit so chair time goes on examining.

## What it does not

This is where teledentistry gets oversold and then disappoints. A phone camera, bathroom lighting and no retractor do not see what a mirror sees at ten centimetres.

- **Caries diagnosis.** Interproximal lesions are invisible in even a perfect photograph. They need bitewings.
- **Periodontal diagnosis.** No probing means no periodontal chart, and without one there is no diagnosis, only an impression of red gingivae.
- **Pain of uncertain origin.** Separating a pulpitis from a cracked cusp or referred pain needs vitality testing and percussion.
- **Anything that ends in a prescription without an examination.** An antibiotic prescribed on the strength of a photograph is a clinical decision made with less information than was available.

> **Photographs a patient sends you are health data.** If they land in a nurse's personal WhatsApp, they live on a phone nobody controls, outside the clinical record and outside any retention policy. That is the most common failure in all of this, and it has nothing to do with clinical judgement.

## A table reception can actually use

Whoever answers the phone needs a written rule, not an improvised clinical judgement.

| Situation | Remote? | What decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Pain with facial swelling or fever | ✗ Same day appointment | Risk of spread |
| Cold sensitivity, no swelling | ✓ Triage by phone | Can wait for a routine slot |
| Fractured tooth, no pain | ~ Photo, then decide | Whether pulp is exposed |
| 24 hour post-operative check | ✓ Video call | Healing as expected or not |
| Suspected caries at a check-up | ✗ In person | Needs radiographs |
| Debonded bracket or attachment | ~ Photo, then decide | Weeks left until the next visit |
| Questions about a plan already given | ✓ Video call | Nothing left to examine |
| Gums bleeding on brushing | ✗ In person | Needs probing |

## What the regulators expect

The General Dental Council publishes, alongside other UK healthcare regulators, a shared set of ten high level principles for remote consultations and prescribing. The document states that safeguards are necessary "whether the consultation happens in the context of a continuing treating relationship or is a one-off interaction", and four of the ten do most of the work in a dental practice.

1. **Principle 3.** Tell patients your name, role and, if online, your registration details, "establish a dialogue and make sure the patient understands how the remote consultation is going to work".
2. **Principle 4.** Explain that you can only prescribe if it is safe, and that "it's not safe if they don't have sufficient information about the patient's health or if remote care is unsuitable to meet their needs".
3. **Principle 6.** "Undertake an adequate clinical assessment and access medical records or verify important information by examination or testing where necessary."
4. **Principle 9.** "Keep notes that fully explain and justify the decisions they make."

Principle 6 is the one that settles most arguments about teledentistry. If the assessment you can make remotely is not adequate for the decision in front of you, the answer is an appointment, not a more careful photograph.

> **This is not legal advice, and it is UK-specific.** The principles above were published by UK regulators and apply to UK registrants. If you practise elsewhere, your own regulator and your indemnity provider set the rules, and they are worth reading before you offer remote appointments rather than after.

## Setting it up without buying anything

You do not need a telemedicine platform to do this properly. You need a seven step procedure the whole team follows the same way.

1. **Identify the patient** before discussing anything clinical. Full name plus a second check, such as date of birth.
2. **Explain how the contact works** and what the alternative is: that it is advice, that it may end in an appointment, and whether it is chargeable.
3. **Take and record consent** for being seen this way, and separately for recording or keeping images if you intend to.
4. **Use a practice channel, not a personal one.** A practice number and account, with images downloaded into the record and deleted from the device.
5. **Write the same notes you would write chairside**: the complaint, what you observed, what you decided and why.
6. **Close with an explicit decision.** An appointment and when, instructions until then, or discharge of the episode. Without that the contact stays open.
7. **Tell the patient what to do if it worsens**, with a concrete threshold: if swelling appears, if the pain does not settle, which number to call.

## What it changes about the schedule

Triage that works does not fill the day, it orders it. The practical consequence is that the practice stops holding emergency slots blind and starts holding them against what that morning's triage actually found.

Two numbers are worth checking a month in: how many remote contacts ended in an appointment, and how many of those that did not ended up attending for the same problem within a fortnight. The first tells you whether the filter is calibrated; the second tells you whether it is filtering too hard.

## Where this lives

The important part of all of the above is not the video tool, it is where the conversation ends up. If the record of the contact lives in a phone, an inbox or a shared folder, the clinical record is incomplete, and whoever sees that patient in six months will not know anything happened.

Worth testing in whatever you already run: can you record a non-attending contact as part of the record, attach the images the patient sent, and see all of it on the same timeline as the visits.
