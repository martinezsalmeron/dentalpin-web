Report it to the MHRA through the Yellow Card scheme, and do not throw the device away. The MHRA's position is that healthcare professionals "should report adverse incidents involving medical devices to the Yellow Card scheme", in accordance with their organisation's own medical device policies and procedures. You do not need to have established what went wrong first: the MHRA's guidance is explicit that "you do not have to prove causality to report a suspected ADR, only a suspicion is needed". A fractured implant, an instrument that breaks mid-surgery, an unexpected serious infection after fitting a prosthesis: all of them belong in a report.

This is not legal advice. It is what the official sources cited at the end say, consulted on 13 October 2026.

> **The MHRA says "should", and points at your own policy.** Its instruction to healthcare professionals is to report "in accordance with your organisations medical device policies and procedures". That makes the written policy the thing that decides whether anything gets reported at all, which is why a practice that has never written one effectively has no reporting process.

This post is the outward direction: what the practice has to tell someone. The inward direction, which lot and UDI you keep so an alert can find the patient, is in [implant lot tracking](/en/blog/implant-lot-tracking/), and what the practice signs when the device is made for one named patient is in [the declaration of conformity for a custom-made dental device](/en/blog/custom-made-dental-device-declaration/).

## What Yellow Card covers, and what counts

Yellow Card takes reports on "all medical devices available on the UK market". For devices, the MHRA defines an adverse incident as an event that caused, or almost caused, an injury to someone.

The "almost" is the part most practices skip. A near miss is reportable: the bur that fractures and comes out whole, the torque wrench found out of calibration before the abutment went on, the batch of cement that never set. Nobody was hurt in any of those, and each one is the kind of event the scheme exists to collect.

| | What it is | Report it |
|---|---|---|
| Harm occurred | Patient, user or anyone else injured | ✓ Yes |
| Harm almost occurred | Failure caught by luck or by the team | ✓ Yes |
| Cause established | You know why the device failed | Not required before reporting |
| Software and apps | Used for a medical purpose, likely a device | ✓ Yes, via the digital form |

Software is on that list for a reason worth knowing: software, apps and AI used for a medical purpose are likely to be medical devices in their own right, and their failures go to Yellow Card the same way a fractured implant does.

## What to do with the device, which is where reports get spoiled

The MHRA's instructions here are short and unusually blunt.

- **"If a device has been involved in an incident, do not throw it away or repair it."** Repairing it destroys the evidence of why it failed.
- **"If the police or coroner is involved in the investigation, keep the device until the MHRA contacts you."** It is not yours to hand on in that situation.
- **"Otherwise you can make the device available to the manufacturer for analysis."** The manufacturer is the one with the equipment to analyse it.
- **"Do not send medical devices to the MHRA unless we specifically ask you."** The report goes to the MHRA; the object does not.

Handing the fractured implant to the rep without a signed record of what left the building and when is the common version of this going wrong. Write down who took it, on what date, and for what.

## Where the report goes, which depends on where you are

The route is not the same across the four nations, and this is the detail that sends people to the wrong form.

1. **England and Wales.** Report to the Yellow Card scheme, following your organisation's medical device policies and procedures.
2. **Scotland.** Incidents go to the Health Facilities Scotland online incident reporting system. A private facility providing care to private clients reports instead to the MHRA and to the Care Inspectorate.
3. **Northern Ireland.** The MHRA's guidance, updated on 24 July 2025, states that the Northern Ireland Adverse Incident Centre's incident and reporting function will be closing, and that healthcare professionals in Northern Ireland will report adverse incidents involving medical devices under their organisation's device policies. The same page still lists NIAIC under how to report, so check the current route before you file.
4. **Any nation, manufacturers and suppliers.** They use MORE, a separate system, not Yellow Card.

> **A private practice in Scotland has a second addressee.** Care provided to private clients is reported to the MHRA and the Care Inspectorate rather than through the NHS board's system. It is the one place where getting the nation right changes who you write to.

## What goes in the record

The report is filed once. The patient record is read afterwards, by people who were not there, and it is what the manufacturer's investigation has to work from.

1. **The date and time you found it**, not the date someone remembered to write it up.
2. **The device, identified.** Manufacturer, reference, batch or lot number, and the UDI where it carries one. Without this nothing can be investigated.
3. **What happened, described as you saw it.** "Implant fractured at the first thread" is a finding.
4. **The consequences for the patient**, and the treatment their consequences required.
5. **If harm was avoided, what avoided it**: luck, or something the team did. That is what makes a near miss legible as a near miss.
6. **Photographs**, attached to the episode and carrying the same date.
7. **What you reported, when, and the reference you got back**, plus who took the device away and on what date.

A single failure is an accident. Three of the same lot in eight months is something a regulator can act on, and it only looks like a pattern if the dates and the lot numbers are in one place. Spread across three paper notes it looks like bad luck three times.

## What the practice does not have to do

- **You do not have to work out the cause.** Analysing the device is the manufacturer's job, and assessing the risk is the MHRA's.
- **You do not have to be certain.** A suspicion is the stated threshold.
- **You do not have to send anything physical** unless the MHRA asks for it.
- **You do not have to wait for the patient to complain.** Most of what the scheme wants to hear about never reaches a complaint.

## What to have ready before it happens

- **The lot number and UDI of every implanted device**, in the patient's record rather than on a delivery note in a drawer.
- **A written medical device policy**, because the MHRA's instruction points at it. Two pages naming the route, the person and the record is enough.
- **One named person** who files the report and chases the reference. A duty that belongs to everyone belongs to nobody.
- **A fixed place in the record** where these episodes go, the same in every file.

Keeping that in a spreadsheet works until you need every patient who received one lot. In [Dentalpin](/en/pricing/) the lot and UDI sit on the patient's treatment, notes carry their date and author, and photographs attach to the episode, which is what you need to assemble when a manufacturer asks what you saw.
