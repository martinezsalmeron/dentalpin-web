Seven numbers are enough to run a dental practice: chair utilisation, no-show rate, new patients, treatment plan acceptance, production per chair hour, collection rate and receivables over sixty days old. Pull them on the same date each month, compare them against your own last three months, and the whole review fits in half an hour.

What does not work is the twenty metric dashboard that gets built in January and never opened again. A dashboard that changes nothing is a report, not a tool.

## A number is a KPI only if it changes a decision

The test is short. If that number moved twenty per cent in either direction, would you do anything differently next week? If the answer is no, it is an interesting fact and not an indicator.

Half of what usually sits on a dashboard fails that test. Total monthly revenue fails it almost every time, because it rises and falls with the number of working days and with a single large case, and it never says what to do.

> **A KPI nobody looks at on the fifth of the month is not a KPI.** A mediocre number reviewed every month at the same hour beats a perfect dashboard consulted only once things have already gone wrong.

## The seven numbers

| Number | How it is calculated | What decision it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Chair utilisation | Hours with a patient in the chair ÷ hours that chair is staffed | Opening hours, hiring, moving a clinician between rooms |
| No-show rate | (No-shows + cancellations inside 24 h) ÷ scheduled appointments | Reminders, day before confirmation, how gaps are filled |
| New patients | First visits in the month | How much to spend on acquisition, and when to stop |
| Treatment plan acceptance | Value accepted ÷ value presented | How plans are presented, whether financing is needed, what follow up exists |
| Production per chair hour | Production in the period ÷ staffed chair hours | Which treatments get scheduled in which slots |
| Collection rate | Cash collected in the month ÷ invoiced in the month | Payment methods accepted, and the moment payment is asked for |
| Receivables over 60 days | Outstanding balances due more than sixty days ago | Who gets a call this week |

None of the seven needs a special tool. The first four come out of the schedule and the last three out of billing.

## The schedule: utilisation and no-shows

Chair utilisation is the number most practices get wrong, and it is always the denominator. Staffed hours are not the hours the building is open, they are the hours that specific chair has a clinician assigned to it.

Count each room separately. An average of seventy per cent across one room at ninety and another at fifty hides exactly the problem you are trying to find.

- **A no-show is not the same as an empty slot.** A patient who fails to attend leaves a gap, but gaps also appear because nobody filled that slot in the first place. They are fixed differently, so measure them separately.
- **Late cancellations count as no-shows.** Two hours notice leaves the same chair empty as no notice at all. If your system separates them, add them together for this indicator.
- **Break no-shows down by weekday and by time of day.** They are almost always concentrated, and that concentration is the action: that slot needs a confirmation call the day before.

## The money: production, collection and debt

Production, billing and collection are three different things, and treating them as one is what makes a month look good when it was not. Production is the work done, billing is what was issued, collection is money that actually arrived.

Production per chair hour is the best of the three because it cancels out the calendar. A short February and a March with two bank holidays stop looking like a decline.

A collection rate below ninety per cent for three months running is not a chasing problem, it is a timing problem. It is usually fixed by taking payment at the front desk before the patient leaves, not by calling afterwards.

> **Debt is measured by age, not by total.** Ten thousand in outstanding invoices issued last week is a practice that bills. The same ten thousand at ninety days is something else entirely, and the total on its own cannot tell the two apart.

## The patients: new arrivals and acceptance

New patients is the easiest number to produce and the easiest to misread. It tells you how much to spend on acquisition, but on its own it does not say whether the practice is growing, because a practice can fill the front door while emptying out of the back one.

Always pair it with return for recall. If thirty new patients arrive every month and none of them come back at six months, the problem is not acquisition.

Treatment plan acceptance is measured **by value, not by count**. Accepting nine plans of one hundred and turning down one of six thousand is ninety per cent acceptance by count and thirteen per cent by value, and only the second describes what happened.

- **Fix a window.** A plan presented in August may be accepted in October. Measure acceptance at ninety days from presentation, which means each month closes with the figure from three months ago.
- **Separate declined from never answered.** They are two different conversations, and the second is almost always the bigger number.
- **Do not mix orthodontics with routine work.** The values and the decision times differ so much that a combined average describes neither.

## The numbers that mislead

| Number | Useful for deciding? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Total monthly revenue | ~ Only against the same month last year | Moves with working days and with a single large case |
| Total appointment count | ✗ No | A ten minute check and a ninety minute root canal count the same |
| Production per chair hour | ✓ Yes | Cancels out both the calendar and the treatment mix |
| Average review score | ✗ Not as a monthly KPI | Moves very slowly and changes nothing this month |
| Hours worked per clinician | ~ For planning, not for scoring | Useful to balance a rota, toxic as a performance measure |
| Collection rate | ✓ Yes | Points at when payment is asked for, which is the part you control |

**Be careful about turning these into a staff league table.** The moment someone knows they are measured on production per hour, the schedule fills up with whatever scores well. Schedule and money indicators describe the practice; individual performance is a different conversation.

## Definitions are set once and left alone

The usual argument in a monthly meeting is not about what to do, it is about whether the number was calculated correctly. Settle that once.

1. **Write down the formula for each indicator** on one page, numerator and denominator, and keep it where the team can see it.
2. **Decide what counts as a no-show** and at what precise hour an appointment becomes one.
3. **Decide whether production is recorded on the treatment date or the invoice date.** Either works, changing it mid year does not.
4. **Fix the day of the month** the figures are pulled, and have the same person pull them.
5. **Keep the history in one place**, even a spreadsheet. An indicator without twelve months behind it cannot be read.

## The thirty minute monthly review

1. **The numbers are ready before the meeting**, not during it. Hunting for data in the room is what turns thirty minutes into two hours.
2. **Each indicator sits next to the previous three months**, not next to an annual budget.
3. **Flag only the ones that moved more than ten per cent.** The rest get read and passed over.
4. **Every flagged indicator leaves with an action, a name and a date.** Without that, the meeting was a reading aloud.
5. **The next review opens with last month's actions.** That is the only thing that makes the previous one worth having held.

## The only benchmark that helps you

Plenty of figures circulate about what a dental practice should be hitting, and almost none of them say which country, which practice size, which treatment mix or which year. An orthodontic practice in a capital city and a general practice in a small town do not share a single comparable indicator.

> **The benchmark that matters is your own from a year ago.** Your June utilisation against last June answers a question that means something. The same number against the average of an undefined sector answers nothing.

If you want an external reference, use one published by your professional association or your national statistics body, with the methodology visible. Any figure with no source and no year is an anecdote with decimal places.

## Where to start this month

1. **Pick three indicators, not seven.** Utilisation, no-shows and receivables over sixty days is a good first trio for almost any practice.
2. **Write their formulas today** and pull the last six months, by hand if necessary.
3. **Put a date in the calendar**, the same day each month, half an hour, the same person every time.
4. **Add a fourth indicator once the first three have produced actions for three months.** Not before.
