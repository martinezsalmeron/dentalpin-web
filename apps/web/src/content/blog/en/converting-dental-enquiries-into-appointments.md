An enquiry turns into an appointment when three things happen on the same call: somebody answers the phone, a specific slot is offered before the call ends, and the conversation is written down somewhere other than the memory of whoever took it. What loses enquiries is almost never the price, it is "we will call you back".

The rest of this page is how to build those three things with the team you already have. No CRM and no campaign required.

> **You will read a lot of figures about this and almost none has a study behind it.** "30% of first visits are lost on the phone", "14% of calls go unanswered": they travel from blog to blog with no source. There are none here, because the procedure works without them and one month of your own call log gives you the real number for your practice.

## Who answers, and what happens when they cannot

The first decision is not what to say, it is who picks up and what happens when that person is busy. A single-handed reception that also takes payments, chairsides and runs the steriliser has stretches of the day when nobody can answer.

1. **Name someone per time block, not per person.** If reception goes into surgery at 11:00, somebody holds the phone from 11:00 to 11:30 and it is written down.
2. **Decide what a missed call does.** A voicemail nobody listens to is the same as having no phone. A divert to the practice mobile, or an automated message promising a callback within a stated window, both work as long as the window is kept.
3. **Set the callback window in hours, not days.** Someone calling in pain, or holding a quote from elsewhere, is usually calling more than one practice that morning.
4. **Log the missed call the same way as the answered one.** If it is not written down, the callback depends on somebody remembering.

## The first ninety seconds: what to ask and what to write down

The point of the opening of the call is not to sell anything, it is to have enough to offer a slot that fits. It is a short list and it fits on one card.

- **A name and a phone number that works.** Read back out loud to confirm it, because one wrong digit turns every follow-up into wasted time.
- **What is wrong, or what they want, in their words.** "I broke a tooth eating" and "I want a quote for aligners" lead to different diaries.
- **Whether there is pain, and since when.** It is the only thing that decides whether the appointment is today or next week.
- **How they heard of you.** One word: Google, a relative, the sign outside. It is the only marketing data you need and the only way to get it is to ask.
- **Which times suit them.** Asking before you look at the diary avoids the round of rejected offers.

That is enough to offer. The rule that changes the outcome most is offering **two specific slots** rather than asking "when suits you?". An open question at the end of the call sends the conversation back to the start.

If they cannot commit there and then, the call does not end without one of two things: a provisional appointment with a date, or an agreed day and time to call back. "We will be in touch" is neither.

## Where an enquiry with no appointment lives

This is where the work of the previous section gets lost. An enquiry with no appointment has no natural home in the diary, so it ends up on paper, and the paper ends up in a drawer.

| Where it is written | Survives a shift change | Remembers the follow-up | Shows who spoke to them |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paper pad at reception | ✗ No | ✗ No | ✗ No |
| Shared spreadsheet | ~ If somebody updates it | ~ Only if somebody opens it | ~ Depends how it is filled |
| Provisional slot in the diary | ✓ Yes | ✓ The diary shows it by itself | ✓ Yes |
| Contact record in the software | ✓ Yes | ✓ With a task and a date | ✓ Yes |

The last two rows are not alternatives, and the combination works best: a contact record for the details and the history of attempts, a provisional slot so the date is visible without opening anything.

> **A provisional slot has to expire on its own.** Held without confirmation and never reviewed, three weeks later the diary is full of blocks held for people who were treated elsewhere. Give it a short life, 48 or 72 hours, and review the expiring ones daily.

## How many attempts, on which channel, and when to stop

Chasing without a rule annoys people, and never closing anything is worse. A plain written sequence fixes both.

1. **Same day, on the channel they used.** Someone who sends a WhatsApp expects a WhatsApp back. Someone who rang expects a call.
2. **Second attempt at 48 to 72 hours**, on a different channel. If the call went unanswered, a short message with two specific slots and nothing else.
3. **Third attempt a week later**, and it is the last of the sequence. A message that says plainly it is the last one and leaves the door open closes better than one hinting at a fourth.
4. **Stop there.** Three attempts, each with its date and channel recorded, then close the enquiry as not converted with the reason in one line.

That last line is what pays off six months later. "Thought it was expensive", "waiting on their insurer", "a relative did it" are three different decisions, and only one of the three is ever worth writing to again.

## What you may keep on someone who is not a patient yet

Someone who has only enquired is not a patient, but their details are personal data from the first call, and if they described what hurts they are health data too.

The lawful basis is the contract basis in Article 6(1)(b) UK GDPR. The ICO describes it as covering the case where "you haven't yet got a contract with the individual, but they have asked you to do something as a first step (eg provide a quote)", which is exactly what a treatment enquiry is. The same guidance sets the limit: it "does not apply if you take pre-contractual steps on your own initiative, to meet other obligations, or at the request of a third party".

When the enquiry describes a problem, it is special category data, and the ICO is explicit that this needs a second look: "If processing of special category data is necessary for the contract, you also need to identify a separate condition for processing this data." For a dental practice that condition is the health care one in Article 9(2)(h), with the professional secrecy conditions that go with it.

- **Keep the minimum that lets you ring back.** A presumed diagnosis taken from a two minute call is not data minimisation, it is a note that will be wrong on the record.
- **Put a period on it.** Storage limitation in Article 5(1)(e) means the data cannot sit there indefinitely. A written period of twelve or twenty four months from last contact is defensible; "until we delete it" is not.
- **Give the privacy information at collection.** If the enquiry arrives through a web form, the information about how you will use it belongs on the form, not in the reply.

> **Answering the enquiry and marketing to them are different things.** Under PECR the ICO's rule is that you "must not send electronic mail marketing to individuals, unless: they have specifically consented". The soft opt-in covers people who "bought (or negotiated to buy) a similar product or service from you in the past", with an opt-out offered when the details were collected and in every message since. A discussed treatment plan can fall inside that; a one line price enquiry that went nowhere is a much weaker case for it.

This is not legal advice. Before you write the wording on the web form or the script read out on the phone, have whoever handles data protection at the practice check it.

## Mistakes that lose the enquiry before it reaches the diary

- **Quoting a firm price on the phone without seeing the mouth.** It creates an expectation the first visit contradicts, and the appointment falls through there instead of on the call.
- **Answering only the price.** Someone asking "how much is an implant" is almost never asking only that. One short question back about what is going on changes the whole call.
- **Leaving the enquiry with no owner.** If there is no name next to the follow-up, the follow-up does not exist.
- **Mixing enquiries and patients in one list.** A contact record with no clinical record is handled differently and deleted differently.
- **Never closing the ones that do not convert.** A list of open enquiries from eight months ago is a list nobody opens.
