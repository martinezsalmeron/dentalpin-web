The bottleneck in a multi-chair practice is almost never the number of chairs, it is how time is shared between them. Measure the real utilisation of each operatory, size every appointment to what it actually takes, group similar procedures into blocks, and arrange the day so the dentist moves from one chair to the next while the team preps the following patient. Two well-dovetailed operatories out-produce three that are poorly split.

What follows is how to measure that utilisation, how to set appointment lengths, how to build the blocks, and how to dovetail the chairs so nobody stands around waiting.

## First separate two things: chairs and providers

A single-operatory schedule only has to line up one thing: that the dentist is free. The moment there are two or three chairs, every appointment needs two scarce resources at once, a free chair and a free person, and the jam appears when one of the two is missing even though the other is idle.

That produces the two classic mistakes. An empty chair while the dentist works in another is capacity thrown away. Two patients booked at the same minute for the same provider, one in each chair, is an appointment that starts late and drags the delay through the whole morning.

- **Decide which column runs your schedule.** If the scarcest resource is the dentist, organise by provider and watch for chair clashes. If you have hygienists working independently, each is her own column and the chair is what has to line up.
- **Make the chair visible, not just the person.** A schedule that only shows "Dr. Garcia" hides the fact that her second operatory has sat empty for two hours.
- **Count capacity in chair-hours**, not in number of appointments. Three chairs open for eight hours are twenty-four chair-hours a day, and that is the real ceiling to measure against.

## Measure utilisation per chair, not for the whole practice

The number that matters is a division per operatory: the time a patient sat in that chair divided by the time that chair was open, over a period longer than a single stray week.

> **The practice average hides the problem.** If one operatory runs at 90% and another at 55%, the average says 72% and nobody looks at the second. Measure them separately or you are not measuring anything useful.

| Situation | How to count it | What it lets you do |
|---|---|---|
| Patient seated, treatment under way | ✓ Chair occupied | This is what you want to maximise |
| Chair free between two appointments | ~ Turnover | Reducible by cleaning in parallel |
| Chair open with no appointment booked | ✗ Gap | Fillable from a cancellation list |
| Chair closed (no staff that session) | Outside the calculation | Does not count as capacity |

The range commonly cited as healthy across the industry sits between 75% and 85% utilisation, but do not set that number as a target on its own: a new practice and a mature one are not playing the same game. What is universal is how you improve it, and it is the three things that come next.

## Size every appointment to what it actually takes

The most expensive and most invisible waste is booking round blocks for work that does not last a round block. If a filling takes you thirty-five minutes on average and you book it into an hour, you give away twenty-five minutes of chair time on each one, and across the week that is a whole appointment lost per operatory.

The fix is not to rush, it is to measure. Pull from your own software how long each appointment type really takes and size the templates to that reality, with an honest margin, not an optimistic one.

| Appointment type | Common error | How to set it |
|---|---|---|
| Exam and hygiene | ✗ All at 30 min out of habit | ~ Measure: a new patient's first visit runs longer |
| Simple filling | ✗ A 60-minute block | ✓ The real measured length, plus a margin |
| Root canal | ~ The same for all | ✓ Varies by tooth and by number of canals |
| New-patient visit | ✗ Booked like a check-up | ✓ Longer: exam, history and treatment plan |

- **Standardise appointment types**, not individual bookings. Five or six templates with their durations cover almost everything and take the decision off the front desk.
- **Split "dentist time" from "chair time".** A root canal ties up the chair for an hour, but the dentist is only hands-on for part of it, and that gap is where the other chair fits.
- **Review the templates every quarter.** Real times shift with the team, the technique and the mix of treatments.

## Block similar procedures together

Alternating a crown, a hygiene visit and an emergency forces the tray to be set and stripped every time and breaks the team's rhythm. Reserving slots for one kind of work, what is called block scheduling, keeps the instrument setup stable and lets the morning build momentum.

1. **Reserve the sharpest hours for the long work.** The early slots, with the team fresh, are for root canals, surgery or full-mouth rehab, not for check-ups that fit any gap.
2. **Group exams and hygiene into their own blocks**, ideally in the chair and with the person who usually does them.
3. **Leave the short appointments for the end of the session**, when a delay no longer drags anything behind it.
4. **Block, do not just leave blank.** Marking a slot as reserved for a certain appointment type is different from leaving it empty: the front desk knows what may go there and what may not.
5. **Review the blocks against the data, not against intuition.** If Tuesday's surgery block sits half empty every week, it is not a block, it is a gap with a name.

## Dovetail the chairs: the dentist moves, the patient stays seated

Here is the real advantage of having several operatories, and most practices waste it. The idea, called four-handed dentistry and dovetailing between chairs in the management literature, is that the clinician is never waiting: while finishing in one chair, the other already holds a patient seated, anaesthetised or prepped by the support team.

1. **Book the patient who needs preparation into chair two.** Anaesthetic that has to take effect, a radiograph, an impression: the assistant gets all of that ready without the dentist standing there.
2. **The dentist finishes the hands-on part in chair one** and moves to chair two, which is already set, with no dead time in between.
3. **The assistant turns over chair one**, clears, cleans and preps for the next patient, while the dentist works in chair two.
4. **Line up the durations so the handovers fit.** Dovetailing only works if the time the dentist spends away from one chair is close to what the team needs to prep the other.
5. **Do not attempt it with treatments that demand the dentist throughout.** A long surgery ties the clinician up start to finish and leaves no room to dovetail: that goes in its own block, not in a chain.

> **Dovetailing needs support staff, not just a second chair.** A second operatory with no extra hands moves nothing forward. The sum that decides whether it pays is how many assistant hours you add against how much dentist time you free, and it almost always comes out ahead once there is volume.

## Leave room for what does not fit the plan

A schedule packed to the minute is fragile: the first emergency or the first overrun breaks the whole thing. Leaving air on purpose is not lost capacity, it is protected capacity.

- **Reserve one emergency slot a day per active chair.** If it is not used by mid-morning, release it to the cancellation list and it is not lost.
- **Put a margin after treatments that tend to overrun.** Ten minutes behind a root canal cost less than a delay that drags five patients with it.
- **Keep the internal plan B ready for the slot that frees up.** Outstanding team exams, treatment-plan follow-ups, radiographs to review: a slot filled with something useful does not hurt like an empty one.

## One person owns the schedule and the rules are written down

Chaos in a multi-chair practice is almost never a software problem, it is that three people book with three different criteria.

- **One person owning the schedule per session.** It does not mean only she books, it means she decides when there is doubt and the judgement is hers.
- **The rules written down and on the wall.** What fits each block, how long each appointment type runs, which chair does what. A rule that only lives in someone's head falls apart the day that person is off.
- **One colour per provider or per type, and only one.** Colour is information, not decoration: if everyone paints to taste, it stops saying anything.
- **Confirm before treating a slot as secure**, especially with patients who have missed before, so the chain is not built on an appointment that will not arrive.

## Where to start

1. **Pull utilisation per chair** for the last three months, per operatory and not as an average.
2. **Measure the real length** of your five or six most frequent appointment types and size the templates to that number.
3. **Draw the blocks of a typical week**: long work early, exams grouped, short appointments at the end.
4. **Try dovetailing two chairs** in one specific slot, with an assistant assigned, and measure whether it works.
5. **Reserve the emergency slot** and define its release rule.
6. **Name the schedule owner** and put the rules in writing where the whole team can see them.
7. **Measure utilisation again at three months.** Before that, the noise is bigger than the effect.
