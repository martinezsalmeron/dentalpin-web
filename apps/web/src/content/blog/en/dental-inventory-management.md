Running inventory in a dental practice means setting a reorder point for the twenty or thirty items that actually matter, counting those every week, and recording the lot number and expiry of anything that ends up inside a patient. The rest of the cupboard needs no spreadsheet, just tidy shelves and someone who speaks up when the last box comes out.

The expensive failure is not running out. It is holding thousands of dollars of stock and binning part of it every month because nobody checks the dates.

## What is worth tracking

Not every supply justifies the time it takes to count it. The question that separates the two is what happens if it runs out on a Tuesday morning.

If the answer is "we cancel a surgical case", it belongs on the short list. If it is "we open the spare box", it does not.

| Category | Level of control | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Implants and biomaterials | ✓ Unit, lot and expiry | They go into the patient and they are the most expensive thing you hold |
| Anesthetic cartridges | ✓ Lot and expiry | They go into the patient and they expire |
| Composites and bonding agents | ✓ Expiry and minimum | In a small practice they expire before they run out |
| Endodontic files and points | ~ Minimum per item | Many references, irregular use |
| Sterilization indicators | ✓ Lot and expiry | They are the documentary evidence for the cycle |
| Gloves, gauze, saliva ejectors | ~ Minimum per box | High and steady use, restock by level |
| Burs and rotary | ~ Minimum per type | Replaced by wear, not by date |
| Office and cleaning supplies | ✗ No unit tracking | Counting it costs more than the item |

A two-chair practice rarely has more than thirty items on the short list. If yours runs past a hundred, you are not controlling stock, you are cataloguing a cupboard.

## Demand comes from the schedule, not from history

Usage history tells you what you spent. Next fortnight's schedule tells you what you are about to spend, and only one of those arrives in time to do anything about it.

Every booked implant is an implant that has to be on the shelf, with its abutment and healing cap. Every booked root canal is a specific file sequence and a specific gutta-percha taper.

![Weekly schedule view showing each clinician's appointments laid out by time slot](/screenshots/schedule-week.png)

*Next week, chair by chair. Every booked treatment is material that should already be bought.*

Checking next week's schedule before you place the order removes most last-minute panics. It is a ten-minute job on a Thursday.

## The reorder point is the whole formula

You do not need an inventory model. You need one number per item, and it is built like this:

1. **Measure average weekly use** for that item over the last three months. Three, because a single month is skewed by one unusual case.
2. **Ask the supplier for their real lead time** in days, not the one on the website. The lead time that counts is the one they hit in August and in the week before Christmas.
3. **Add safety stock** worth one week of use for critical items, and none at all for anything you can buy from a local dental dealer the same day.
4. **Add it up.** Reorder point = weekly use × lead time in weeks + safety stock.

A worked example with round numbers: if you get through 8 boxes of gloves a week, the supplier takes two weeks, and you want a week of cover, you order when 24 boxes are left. Not when one is.

> **The maximum matters as much as the minimum.** Without a ceiling per item, a reorder point only guarantees that nothing ever runs out, and the store cupboard grows until somebody panics. Set the ceiling at four to six weeks of use for anything that expires.

Those figures are an example, not a benchmark. Built from your own usage, they turn restocking into an automatic decision anyone on the team can make.

## Expiry is managed FEFO, not FIFO

FIFO means first in, first out. FEFO means first expired, first out, which is not always the same thing, because a box delivered yesterday can carry a shorter date than one that has sat on the shelf for a month.

The physical rule is simple: the shortest date goes at the front, on every shelf, every time. Putting the new delivery in front is where most wasted material starts.

- **Check dates monthly**, not annually. A monthly pass over the short list takes fifteen minutes and finds what expires in ninety days, while there is still time to use it.
- **Flag anything within three months** with a coloured dot. It is low technology and it works better than an alert nobody opens.
- **Log what you throw away and why.** It is the only figure that tells you whether you are buying too much or buying the wrong thing.
- **Ask for long dates on expensive lines.** On implants and biomaterials that is a reasonable request and many dealers will meet it.

## Lot and expiry: what the rules expect you to keep

This is where stock control stops being about efficiency and becomes traceability. What goes into a patient has to be traceable back to them.

In the United States, the FDA's unique device identification system puts a UDI on device labels. The FDA describes it as a device identifier, "a mandatory, fixed portion of a UDI that identifies the labeler and the specific version or model of a device", plus a production identifier that carries the lot or batch number, serial number, expiration date or manufacturing date when those appear on the label.

That obligation sits with the labeler, normally the manufacturer, not with your practice. What it gives you is a machine-readable code on the box that already contains the two fields you need to record.

![A treatment plan split into stages, showing the treatments in each stage and their status](/screenshots/treatment-plan.png)

*A treatment plan with its stages. The lot number of an implant placed in one of them belongs in the patient's record, not only on the delivery note.*

> **The delivery note is not the record.** Keeping the box or the supplier's paperwork proves what you bought, not what you placed in whom. The lot number has to sit in the patient's clinical record, next to the treatment and the date.

For practices in the EU, the obligation is stronger and it lands on you. The European Commission's factsheet for healthcare professionals states that "for Class III implantable devices, health institutions shall store and keep, preferably by electronic means, the UDIs of the devices they have supplied, or with which they have been supplied (MDR Article 27(9))", and that Member States may extend that duty to other device categories and to individual healthcare professionals.

This is not legal advice. It is what the sources listed at the end say, consulted on 23 August 2026, and your own regulator or dental association is the place to confirm how it applies to you.

## How often to count

Counting the whole store four times a year costs a day each time and prevents no stockouts at all. Cycle counting works better because it spreads the load.

1. **Weekly, the short list.** The critical items, the expensive ones, the ones that expire. Ten minutes, same day, same person.
2. **Monthly, one block.** Split the rest of the catalogue into four blocks and count one each month. In a quarter you have covered everything without losing an afternoon.
3. **Annually, a full count.** To close the year, and to find whatever has been at the back of a drawer for two of them.
4. **Always, on delivery.** Checking the note against the box before signing it is the one moment a supplier error gets corrected without an argument.

## The four numbers that say whether it works

- **Stockouts per month.** How many times a treatment plan had to change or somebody drove to the dealer. If it has been zero for months, you are probably holding too much.
- **Inventory value.** What is sitting on the shelves at cost. Compare it with your monthly spend on supplies: more than two months of use in the cupboard is money doing nothing.
- **Expired material.** In money and by category. It falls fastest once FEFO is in place, and it is the figure that justifies the time spent.
- **Material cost per treatment.** What the consumables for one root canal or one implant actually cost. It is what turns a quoted price into a real margin.

## The mistakes that cost most

- **Buying on offer without checking the date.** Three boxes for the price of two is not a saving if the date is six months out and you use one box a quarter.
- **One person in charge, with no backup.** When stock control lives in one head, their holiday is a guaranteed stockout.
- **Recording the lot only on the delivery note.** Reconstructing afterwards which lot went to which patient, under review, is work that can take days.
- **Mixing lots in the same container.** Topping up a container without emptying it makes the lot unknowable, and it voids the record you were keeping.
- **Counting only the store room.** Opened stock in each operatory is inventory too, and it is usually where half of what you are missing has gone.
