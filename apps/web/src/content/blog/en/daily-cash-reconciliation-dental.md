A cash reconciliation is the comparison between the notes and coins physically in the drawer and what your system says was collected in cash that day, done at the same time, by a named person, with the difference written down even when it is zero. In a practice with a tidy front desk it takes ten minutes at close.

What ruins the habit is not doing it badly. It is doing it whenever somebody remembers, which in practice means doing it on the day money is already missing.

## Counting the drawer is not a reconciliation

Counting is the easy half and it proves nothing on its own. The reconciliation is the comparison of two figures that arrived by different routes: the physical count and the payment record.

That is the whole point. If both numbers come from the same place, for example if the day's cash total is written down from whatever is in the drawer, the reconciliation cannot detect anything.

- **The physical count.** What is in the drawer, note by note, minus the fixed float the day opened with.
- **The system figure.** What the software says was taken in cash today, with card, transfers and direct debits excluded.
- **The variance.** It falls out of the other two and it is the only number that needs interpreting.

> **A zero variance every single day is the most ignored warning sign at a front desk.** Real cash goes out by a few pence now and then. A perfect zero for months usually means somebody is fitting the count to the system instead of counting.

## The close, step by step

Doing it in the same order every day is what gets it down to ten minutes, because nobody has to make a decision while they are doing it.

1. **Stop taking payments.** No collecting while counting. If a patient comes to pay, take it and restart the count rather than adding it in your head.
2. **Pull the day's cash payment list from the system before you count.** That order matters: looking at the expected figure after counting invites you to make it match.
3. **Count the drawer**, keeping the float separate. The float is not takings.
4. **Write down both figures and the difference**, including when the difference is zero.
5. **Investigate only above the threshold** you have agreed. Below it, record and move on.
6. **Remove the surplus** and leave the float at exactly the same amount for tomorrow.
7. **Sign the sheet**, with a name and a time. Paper or screen, but with the person identified.

Step two is the one most often skipped and the one that makes the rest meaningful. Getting the expected figure before counting turns the close into a real check rather than a formality.

## What gets written down every day

The cash sheet does not need to be pretty. It needs to be identical every day and findable a year later. Six fields are enough.

| Field | Why it is there |
|---|---|
| Date and time of close | A reconciliation with no time cannot be cross-checked against anything |
| Opening float | Without it the count means nothing |
| Cash taken according to the system | The expected figure, pulled before counting |
| Cash counted | The physical count, float excluded |
| Variance and its explanation | Zero gets written down too |
| Who closed | A name, not "reception" |

Keeping the sheets matters as much as filling them in. When a variance surfaces in the accounts three months later, the only way to know what happened is to still have the sheets from those days.

## Normal variances and the ones that mean something else

Not every variance is the same, and treating them alike teaches the team to stop reporting the small ones, which are exactly the ones that help you find the big ones.

- **Pennies and mis-given change.** They happen, they get recorded, they do not get investigated. Set a threshold, say five pounds or five dollars, and hold to it.
- **A round number appearing or missing.** Fifty or a hundred is usually an unrecorded payment or a supplier paid out of the drawer. The day's list normally settles it.
- **Small variances always in the same direction.** Being short a little every day is a different problem from being short a lot once. The pattern is the signal, not the amount.
- **A one-off nobody can explain.** Record it exactly as it is, without hunting for a culprit in the moment and without quietly closing the subject.

> **The most common cause is not theft, it is a supplier paid in cash with no receipt.** Someone settles the courier or the lab out of the drawer, nothing is written down, and the count comes up short. One rule fixes it: nothing leaves the drawer unless a piece of paper goes in.

## What the cash rules actually require

Cash limits are national, and the practice is always the business side of the transaction, which is what brings the rules into play. Two reference points are worth knowing before you check your own jurisdiction.

**In the United States**, any person in a trade or business who receives more than **10,000 dollars in cash** in one transaction, or in two or more related transactions, must file Form 8300 with the IRS and FinCEN. The form is due by the 15th day after the cash is received, later payments within one year of the first are added together to test the threshold, and a copy is kept for five years.

**Across the European Union**, Regulation (EU) 2024/1624 sets a **10,000 euro** ceiling on cash payments for goods and services, applying from 10 July 2027. Several member states already run lower national limits, some of them far lower, so the local figure is the one that binds.

> **Neither figure tells you what your practice must do.** The UK, Ireland, Australia, Canada and each EU country handle this differently, and a treatment plan paid in instalments raises a further question about what counts as one transaction. Ask your accountant. This post is not legal advice.

## The float and banking the surplus

The float is a fixed amount that stays in the drawer to give change and never changes. A hundred or two hundred is plenty in a practice where most patients pay by card.

Everything above it gets banked. How often depends on volume, but a useful rule is that accumulated cash should never exceed what you would be relaxed about losing to a theft or a mislaid bag.

Record the banking as what it is, a transfer from cash to bank, not as a reduction in the day's takings. Netting it off is the fastest way to make the accounts and the drawer stop agreeing.

## When the variance keeps coming back

If the pattern holds, the problem is no longer counting and counting harder will not fix it. Three changes do more than any conversation about trust.

1. **One person per shift.** If three people open the same drawer, the reconciliation cannot attribute anything and stops being a tool.
2. **Every payment recorded as it is taken**, not reconstructed from memory at close. Late recording is behind almost every unexplainable variance.
3. **Someone else reviews the sheets once a week.** Five minutes. The existence of the review changes behaviour on its own.

## Where to start this week

1. **Fix the time of the close** and keep it the same every day.
2. **Count the float today** and set it to a round, fixed amount.
3. **Pull the cash list before counting**, starting tomorrow.
4. **Write down the variance threshold** you will not investigate and tell the team.
5. **Put a receipt tray inside the drawer** for anything paid out in cash.
6. **File the sheets in one place**, on paper or in the system, and review them on Friday.
