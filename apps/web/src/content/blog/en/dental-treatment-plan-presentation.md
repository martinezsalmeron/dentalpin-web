The clinician who will carry out the treatment presents the clinical part, it gets an appointment of its own rather than the last five minutes of an examination, and the patient leaves with the plan and the costs in writing, including the options that were considered and set aside. Those three decisions are most of what separates a presentation that ends in an informed yes from one that ends in an estimate nobody looks at again.

In the UK the General Dental Council sets this out in its Standards for the Dental Team, and the wording is more specific than most practices assume.

## What the standards actually require

Principle Three is titled "Obtain valid consent", and its first requirement is the one that frames the appointment: "You must obtain valid consent before starting treatment, explaining all the relevant options and the possible costs".

Options and costs sit in the same sentence as consent. A conversation that covers the price but not the alternatives, or the alternatives but not the price, does not meet it.

Clause 3.1.1 adds the part that decides who does the talking: "You must make sure you have valid consent before starting any treatment or investigation", and the clause goes on to say you should not assume that another member of the team has already obtained it.

> **Consent is not a baton that gets passed down the corridor.** The GDC puts the duty on the person about to treat, which means a plan presented entirely by reception, however well, leaves the clinical part of the conversation undone.

Clause 3.3.1 settles the second-appointment argument in one line: "Giving and obtaining consent is a process, not a one-off event." The standard describes it as part of ongoing communication with everyone in the team involved in the patient's care.

## Time to think is part of the requirement

Clause 3.2.1 requires you to give patients sufficient information and a reasonable amount of time to consider it. That is a standard about pacing, not paperwork, and it is the one most often broken by a well-meaning practice trying to be helpful.

> **Consent does not survive a change of price.** Clause 3.3.5 requires fresh consent if the agreed treatment or the estimated cost changes, and a record that you obtained it. A plan whose cost moves mid-course needs the conversation again, not a revised invoice.

Clause 3.2.2 requires you to tailor how you obtain consent to each patient, presenting information in a format they can readily understand. Clause 3.2.3 suggests encouraging patients with communication difficulties to bring a friend, relative or carer who can help with questions.

- **A reasonable amount of time is not the same as a reasonable amount of information.** Handing over more pages faster does not discharge 3.2.1.
- **The format is yours to choose and theirs to understand.** A printed plan, a drawing on the chart, a photograph of their own mouth: whichever one lands.
- **A companion in the room changes the yes.** The person who has to be convinced at home is often the one who did not come.
- **Documentation is the proof, under 3.1.2**, which asks you to document the discussions you have in the process of gaining consent.

## Who presents which part

| | Who does it | Can it be delegated |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosis, prognosis and options | The treating clinician | ✗ No |
| Risks and benefits of each option | The clinician doing the work | ✗ No |
| Taking the consent itself | The clinician, before starting | ✗ No |
| Costs, payment plans and scheduling | Reception or a coordinator | ✓ Yes |
| Handing over copies and filing them | Anyone in the team | ✓ Yes |
| Following up an unaccepted estimate | Reception or a coordinator | ✓ Yes |

A treatment coordinator is a real and useful role, and nothing above argues against it. What it cannot absorb is the clinical half of the conversation: why this option and not that one, and what the risk of each actually is.

## Why a separate appointment earns its slot

1. **Examine, then decide, and not in the same breath.** Somebody who has just learned they need six teeth treated is not in a position to choose between ways of doing it.
2. **Prepare the case before they arrive.** Radiographs up, costs firm, the consent form written for the procedure actually proposed rather than a generic one.
3. **Lead with the reason they came.** If it was pain or one visible tooth, that is point one of the plan even when it is not clinically first.
4. **Show the finding before the plan.** A plan without the evidence that justifies it sounds like a quote.
5. **Give the options, including doing nothing.** Declining treatment is an option with consequences, and those consequences are part of the information.
6. **Say the number out loud.** Silence about cost gets filled by the patient with the worst figure they can imagine.
7. **Ask what did not land.** This is the 3.2.2 check, and it is the step that gets dropped when the next patient is waiting.
8. **Book the next date before they stand up.** A specific date, even one to confirm. "We will call you" is a lost plan with extra steps.

## What the patient should leave with

- **The written plan, with the stages separated**, because at home it gets retold from memory and memory keeps only the total.
- **The cost of the stage they accepted**, and the rest marked clearly as an estimate.
- **The options that were considered**, with a line on why each was set aside.
- **A copy of anything they signed.** Clause 3.3.5 requires fresh consent if the agreed treatment or the estimated cost changes, so both sides need to know what the original said.
- **A name and a number for questions.** The question answered in two days becomes a treatment; the one that waits becomes a cold estimate.

## What the software has to give you

- **Staged plans with a cost per stage**, so the total is not the only figure on screen.
- **Alternatives held inside the same plan**, rather than three loose estimates where nobody can later tell which was presented.
- **Consent tied to the procedure**, with wording for that procedure, not one document covering a whole record.
- **An automatic record of which version was presented and when**, which is the documentation 3.1.2 asks for.
- **Copies out in one step**, printed or signed on screen, with nothing retyped.
- **An alert when an estimate expires**, which turns follow-up into a task instead of a memory.
