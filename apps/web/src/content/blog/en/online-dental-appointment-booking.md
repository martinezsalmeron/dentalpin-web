Open online booking only for the appointment types whose length you can know without asking anything: check-ups, hygiene visits and the sessions of a treatment plan the patient has already accepted. Keep new patients, emergencies and anything that needs preparation on the phone. Publish a subset of your slots, never the whole schedule. And let the form ask what booking the appointment needs, not what is wrong with the patient.

What follows is what the research says about no-shows, which is not what the sales page says, the rules a published schedule needs, and where the limit sits on what a booking form may ask.

## Online booking does not cut no-shows on its own

It is the argument the feature is sold on, and it is the one that holds up worst.

A 2025 study in *Frontiers in Digital Health* compared online-booked appointments with those booked by phone or at the desk, in two settings at once: a private ophthalmology practice in Frankfurt with 16,894 appointments, and the ophthalmology department of a university hospital in Freiburg with 81,173.

In the private practice, online bookings were missed less often: a median of 1.8% against 5.9% for the offline ones. In the hospital it went the other way, a mean of 14.3% online against 11.2% offline. Both differences were statistically significant.

> **The same intervention reversed its result depending on where it was applied.** A small private practice and a hospital department do not see the same patient or hold the same relationship with them, and that matters more than the booking channel. If someone quotes you a no-show reduction for opening your schedule, they are quoting another clinic's median.

The second figure comes from a Mayo Clinic Health System study published in *JMIR Medical Informatics* in 2021, covering well-child visits. Appointments patients booked themselves through the portal had a 3.07% no-show rate against 4.12% for staff-booked ones, a difference the authors describe as nonsignificant.

What did change sharply was the number of steps. Of the self-scheduled appointments, 93.07% were finalised in a single action, against 71.68% of the staff-scheduled ones.

That is the honest case for opening the schedule. Not more patients through the door, but more appointments that close without anyone picking up a phone, at eleven at night.

## What to open and what to keep on the phone

The decision is made per appointment type, not per patient, and it turns on one thing: whether you can know the length without talking to anyone.

| Appointment type | Online? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Check-up or review | ✓ Yes | Fixed length, nothing to assess beforehand |
| Hygiene | ✓ Yes | Just as predictable, and the one most often requested out of hours |
| A session of an accepted treatment plan | ✓ Restricted | The plan already states the length, but only for patients who have one open |
| New patient | ~ It depends | Length varies and you want their details before they are in the chair |
| Emergency or pain | ✗ No | Somebody has to decide whether that waits until tomorrow |
| Surgery, implants or orthodontics | ✗ No | Needs preparation, materials and sometimes two people |

- **Open by appointment type, not by empty slot.** A forty-minute gap is not "forty minutes of anything", and publishing it as such is how a root canal lands in a check-up slot.
- **Start with two types and one clinician.** A month of check-ups and hygiene on one schedule teaches you more about your own practice than any rollout plan.
- **Restricted booking pays best.** A patient with an accepted plan that runs to six sessions will call six times, and that is the phone traffic actually worth removing.

## The schedule you publish is not the schedule you keep

Opening the schedule does not mean showing all of it. What goes out is a subset with rules, and these are the rules.

1. **Publish only some of the gaps.** Show every one and you lose the room to fit tomorrow's emergency in, and to move an appointment when you need to.
2. **Set a minimum notice**, somewhere between twenty-four and forty-eight hours. Without it, someone books at 8:05 for 8:15 and nobody sees it.
3. **Set a ceiling at the other end.** An appointment booked online six months out gets forgotten; open four to six weeks at most.
4. **Leave a buffer around online slots.** Whoever is booking does not know your turnaround and cleaning time.
5. **Block the last slot of the day.** It is the hardest to refill if it falls through, and the one you want free for the seven o'clock emergency.
6. **Cap how many appointments a stranger can book in a row.** An open form with no limit collects bookings that never show.
7. **Decide what happens with cancellations** before you open, not after. If people can book online but must phone to cancel, you moved the work rather than removing it.

## What the booking form may ask, and what it may not

An appointment at a dental practice is personal data, and anything that reveals why the person is coming is health data under Article 9 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, with reinforced protection. Readers outside the EU should check the equivalent in their own jurisdiction, because the detail is national even where the principle is not.

The most detailed public guidance in existence today was adopted by the German data protection authorities on 16 June 2025, as a position paper on appointment management in healthcare practices. It reads the same Regulation everyone else does, and it goes down to the level of detail nobody else has published.

- **Only the data the appointment needs.** That paper lists them: name, date of birth, the clinician who will see them, the type of appointment (its own examples are a check-up or an X-ray) and a means of contact so the appointment can be cancelled at short notice.
- **The appointment type yes, the symptoms no.** "Check-up" or "hygiene" is what you need to reserve the right amount of time. A free-text box where the patient describes what hurts turns your form into a collection of health data you did not need.
- **Do not hand your patient list to the provider up front.** Those authorities say plainly that there is normally no need to supply the master data of every patient the practice has ever treated: booking one appointment needs the data of that appointment.
- **An external provider is a processor**, on an Article 28 contract, barred from using the data for its own purposes, named in your privacy notice, and worth checking for support or maintenance carried out outside the EU.
- **Always keep a route that is not the website.** That is the same authorities' express position, and in practice it is what stops you losing the seventy-eight-year-old who has been coming for fifteen years.

> **The appointment reminder is not part of the booking.** The German authorities separate the two: sending a reminder is not necessary in order to hold the appointment, it is an added service, and it needs the patient's express agreement, which the practice must be able to evidence. If your form books the appointment and switches on reminders in the same click, those are two different things.

> **The schedule is not the clinical record.** The same paper points out that calendar entries are not part of the treatment documentation and are to be deleted shortly after the appointment; anything that does belong in the record gets moved there and kept there. Worth asking a provider before signing, because almost none of them delete anything on their own.

## What breaks in the first month

None of this is a reason not to open. It is the list to have settled the day before you do.

- **Duplicate patients.** Someone already in your database books with a different email address and a second record appears. It is the number one side effect of online booking, and it is contained by matching on phone number and date of birth before creating anything.
- **The wrong length.** If the published appointment type says thirty minutes and in your practice that visit takes forty-five, the schedule drifts out of true within a fortnight. Measure your real durations before you publish them.
- **The ghost appointment.** Two people book the same slot because the sync lags. Ask the provider how often it syncs and what happens when it fails.
- **The phone does not go quiet.** It goes quiet for what you opened. Everything else still rings, and for a few weeks people also ring to ask whether the appointment they booked online is real.
- **The bad slots stay bad.** Online booking fills the attractive gaps faster. Nine in the morning on a Tuesday does not fill because it has been published.

## Four numbers that tell you whether it works

| Metric | How it is calculated | What decision it triggers |
|---|---|---|
| Share of appointments booked online | Online bookings over total appointments, monthly | If it does not pass 10% in three months, the button is hidden or the form asks too much |
| No-show rate by channel | The rate for online bookings and for the rest, kept apart | The only way to know whether your practice behaves like Frankfurt or like Freiburg |
| Slots published against slots filled | Filled over published | A low figure means you are publishing the gaps nobody wants |
| Inbound calls | A one-week count, before and three months after | Turns online booking into front-desk time, which is what you were paying for |

No-show rate by channel is the one that matters. Split it from day one, because it cannot be reconstructed afterwards.

## Where to start

1. **Measure the real length** of your three most common appointment types over two weeks.
2. **Pick two types** that need no prior assessment, and one clinician.
3. **Write the rules** for minimum notice, maximum window, buffer and the last slot of the day.
4. **Cut the form back** to name, date of birth, clinician, appointment type and contact.
5. **Separate reminder consent** from the booking itself, with its own tick box and its own record.
6. **Sign the processor contract** and ask where the data is processed and when past appointments are deleted.
7. **Name the provider** in your privacy information, and keep the phone number visible.
8. **Review the four numbers at three months** and only then decide whether to open a third appointment type.
