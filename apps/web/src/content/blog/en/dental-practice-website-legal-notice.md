A dental practice website carries two sets of identification, and the second one is usually missing. The first is the set every service provider owes: name, geographic address, email, registration details and, where it applies, the VAT number. The second belongs to regulated professions only: the professional body you are registered with, your professional title, and the country that granted it.

On top of that, the General Dental Council expects named individuals to show their qualification and their GDC registration number, and the practice site to show how a patient complains.

None of this needs a template. Two short pieces of law and one page of GDC guidance between them list every item.

This post is about the rules that apply in the United Kingdom, and it is not legal advice. If you practise elsewhere, your own regulator sets the equivalent list. All official sources are at the end, consulted on 15 October 2026.

## What the e-commerce regulations require

Regulation 6 of the Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002 says a service provider must make the following available "in a form and manner which is easily, directly and permanently accessible":

- **The name of the service provider.**
- **The geographic address** at which the service provider is established.
- **Contact details**, including the email address, that make it possible "to contact him rapidly and communicate with him in a direct and effective manner".
- **Trade register details and registration number**, where the provider is entered in a public register.
- **The relevant supervisory authority**, where the service is subject to an authorisation scheme.
- **Where the provider exercises a regulated profession**: the professional body it is registered with, its professional title "and whether that title has been granted in the United Kingdom, or if not, the member State where that title has been granted", and "a reference to the professional rules applicable" plus how to access them.
- **The VAT number**, where the activity is subject to VAT.

> **"A reference to the professional rules" is not an invitation to paste them.** It asks you to name the rules you work under and give the reader a way to reach them. A line naming the GDC's Standards for the Dental Team with a link does the whole job.

The regulated-profession paragraph is the one that turns a generic contact page into a dental one. Dentistry is a regulated profession, the professional body is the GDC, and the title is the one on the register.

## What the GDC adds

The GDC's guidance on advertising goes further than the regulations, because it is aimed at patients choosing a clinician rather than at consumers buying a service.

If you are responsible for a practice website, the guidance says it must display:

- **The name and geographic address** at which the dental service is provided.
- **Contact details of the dental service**, including email address and telephone number.
- **The GDC's address and other contact details, or a link to the GDC website.**
- **Details of the practice's complaints procedure.**
- **Information about who patients may contact** if they are not satisfied with the response.
- **The date the website was last updated.**

And for any dental professional named on the site as providing care, it requires "your professional qualification and the country from which that qualification is derived" and "your GDC registration number".

The guidance also tells registrants to make sure the GDC registration number is included in information containing their name, to keep the site updated, and not to display information comparing the skills or qualifications of one dental professional with another's.

## If the practice is a limited company

Incorporation adds a short list of its own. Regulation 25 of the Company, Limited Liability Partnership and Business (Names and Trading Disclosures) Regulations 2015 requires every company to disclose on its websites:

- **The part of the United Kingdom** in which the company is registered.
- **The company's registered number.**
- **The address of the company's registered office.**

The same regulation adds that where a website discloses the amount of share capital, that disclosure "must be as to paid up share capital". A practice that trades as a partnership or as a sole trader does not owe this block, which is exactly why copying another practice's legal notice produces something either short or wrong.

## What does not belong on this page

- **The privacy notice is a separate document.** It answers what you do with patient data, which is a different question with a different legal basis, and burying it inside the legal notice means neither gets read.
- **A link to the EU online dispute resolution platform is dead.** Regulation (EU) 2024/3228 repealed the regulation that created it, and the platform stopped operating on 20 July 2025. Older templates still link it.
- **Claims about treatments** belong elsewhere. This page answers who you are, not what you sell.

> **The "date the website was last updated" is the item most likely to be quietly false.** It is also the easiest for anyone to check, and a 2019 date on a site advertising a 2026 technique undermines every other line on the page.

## Rebuilding it in an afternoon

1. **List every clinician named on the site** and get each one's GDC number, qualification and the country that granted it. Not the practice's, each person's.
2. **Check those numbers against the GDC register** rather than against the last version of the website.
3. **Write the provider block**: practice name, the address where care is actually provided, email and telephone.
4. **Name the professional rules and link them** instead of transcribing them.
5. **Add the complaints route**, including who the patient contacts if the practice's answer does not satisfy them.
6. **Add the company particulars** if the practice is incorporated, and skip them if it is not.
7. **Put the update date on the page and a reminder in your own calendar.** The page goes stale on the day an associate leaves, and nobody looks at it that day.

Step 1 is the one that takes the time, and it is also the one that catches the real problem: the associate who left eighteen months ago whose registration number is still published next to a photograph.
