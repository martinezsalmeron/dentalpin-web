The two biggest English-speaking markets publish different numbers for the same water, and a practice that mixes them up will either over-treat or fail an inspection. CDC says dental unit waterlines should be treated to meet the EPA drinking water standard, at or below 500 CFU/mL of heterotrophic water bacteria. England's HTM 01-05 says that where monitoring is undertaken, the total viable count should be expected to lie in the range 100 to 200 cfu/ml, generally incubated at 22 °C. Neither figure is a misprint: they are different measurements against different benchmarks.

This is not legal advice. It is a reading of the official sources listed at the end, consulted on 25 October 2026, with the paragraph reference for each point so it can be checked.

## Why the numbers differ

CDC's figure is a drinking water benchmark. Its position is that waterlines should be treated so the output water meets the Environmental Protection Agency's regulatory standards for drinking water, which is where the 500 CFU/mL of heterotrophic water bacteria comes from.

HTM 01-05's figure is an expectation about what a well-managed system actually delivers, not a limit borrowed from a water regulation. It also notes that some commercial water-purification services offer microbiological control such that total viable count values may be maintained below 10 cfu/ml, which tells you the 100 to 200 range is a working expectation rather than a ceiling to aim for.

| | CDC (United States) | HTM 01-05 (England) |
|---|---|---|
| Figure for output water | ≤ 500 CFU/mL | 100 to 200 cfu/ml |
| What it is measured against | ✓ EPA drinking water standard | ~ Expected range where monitoring is done |
| Organism counted | Heterotrophic water bacteria | Total viable count, generally at 22 °C |
| Testing frequency | Routinely, as the equipment manufacturer recommends | A typical regime, with an external specialist engaged |
| In-house test kits | Not addressed | ✗ Not recommended |
| Surgical procedures | ✓ Sterile saline or sterile water, appropriate delivery device | Covered by the wider decontamination guidance |

> **Both documents say the same thing about the device itself, and it is the sentence worth remembering.** CDC: "conventional dental units cannot reliably deliver sterile water even when equipped with independent water reservoirs." HTM 01-05, paragraph 6.80: "no currently available single method or device will completely eliminate biocontamination of DUWLs or exclude the risk of cross-infection." A bottle is not a solution, and neither is any single product.

## What the waterline actually does

Biofilm is a thin, slimy film of bacteria that sticks to moist surfaces, and CDC's point is that dental unit waterlines have unique characteristics that make them prone to forming it: narrow bore, long stagnant periods, low flow at the walls of the tubing.

That is why starting with clean water does not settle the question. A self-contained system filled with water that already meets the standard will still become contaminated if the biofilm inside the lines is not controlled, which is why CDC describes self-contained systems as something to combine with periodic or continuous chemical germicide treatment protocols rather than as the treatment itself.

## The daily routine, as HTM 01-05 sets it out

The English guidance is the more prescriptive of the two about what happens each day, and the sequence is worth having written down:

1. **Flush the waterlines for at least two minutes at the beginning and end of the day**, and after any significant period when they have not been used, for example after lunch breaks (paragraph 6.85).
2. **Drain down at-risk systems at least at the end of each working day**, which is the advice HTM 01-05 takes from the HSE's approved code of practice L8, and apply the manufacturer's daily cleaning protocol where one exists (6.82).
3. **Remove self-contained water bottles, flush them with distilled or reverse osmosis water, leave them open to the air to dry overnight and store them inverted** (6.83).
4. **Where visual contamination is present, flush with a suitable disinfectant and then wash thoroughly.** The manufacturer's instructions specify the disinfectant and may require the continuous presence of an antimicrobial agent to prevent biofilm building up (6.84).
5. **Disinfect the lines periodically, consulting the manufacturer's instructions in every case.** Sodium hypochlorite, isopropanol and a number of other agents have been shown to remove biofilm as well as reduce contamination, but only where the manufacturer recommends them, and the lines must be thoroughly flushed after disinfection before returning to clinical use (6.86).

Two structural points sit alongside that routine. Self-contained supplies should use distilled or reverse osmosis water, and where self-contained bottles are not used, a Type A air gap should separate the waterlines from the mains supply. HTM 01-05 also tells practices, through the Registered Manager, to engage an external specialist, which may be a locally-based engineering consultant with specialist knowledge of Legionella and other water-borne organisms.

> **HTM 01-05 does not recommend in-house test kits.** It describes microbiological monitoring as a complex procedure and points to commercial microbiological services instead. A practice that tests its own water with a dip slide has a number, but not one that helps it in an inspection.

## Surgery is a separate answer

CDC is explicit and short on this: for surgical procedures, sterile saline or sterile water should be used as a coolant or irrigant, delivered through an appropriate delivery device. The waterline, however well treated, is not the route for a surgical irrigant.

That is the one place where the two chains, building plumbing and dental unit, both drop out of the picture entirely, and it is worth separating in a written protocol so that nobody has to decide it chairside.

## What has to be recorded

Both documents put the same weight on the manufacturer's instructions for use, and both leave the practice to prove what it did. In practice that means four things, per chair and per date, that have to be retrievable years later: the flush and when it was done, the chemical treatment with the product used, the sample with its point, time and laboratory, and the result with whatever followed from it.

How to build that history per piece of equipment is covered in [the equipment inventory and maintenance log](/en/blog/dental-equipment-inventory-maintenance-log/), which is a different question from this one and not worth repeating here.

## Where software helps and where it does not

No software picks a treatment chemistry, fits an anti-retraction valve or replaces a laboratory. What it removes is the reason well-written protocols fail, which is reconstructing two years later what was done on which chair and when, and by whom.

In [Dentalpin](/en/pricing/) each chair is managed as a piece of equipment with its own record, so the flush, the treatment, the sampling date and the result stay attached to the operatory and to the person who entered them, with the date they were entered. What it will not do is set your testing frequency: CDC sends you to the equipment manufacturer for that, and HTM 01-05 sends you to a specialist.
