A lab case goes out with nine things on it: the patient identified, the teeth written in one notation, what is being made, the material, the shade and the guide it was taken with, the occlusal record, what is in the box, the date of the fitting appointment, and who prescribed it. With those nine, the technician can start without phoning you.

The finished work comes back with a document of its own, the statement for a custom-made device, and that statement has required contents. It is worth knowing what they are, because it is the document a patient can ask you for.

## What goes on the lab prescription

The prescription is not a reminder for you. It is an instruction for someone who has never seen the patient, and it should be written that way.

- **The patient, identified.** A name, or a code if you would rather not send the name. What does not work is "the ten-thirty patient", because the lab does not have your schedule.
- **The teeth, in a stated notation.** A bare 16 means different teeth in different systems, and the next section is entirely about that.
- **What is being made, in detail.** Crown, three-unit bridge with the pontic at UR5, occlusal splint, partial denture. Abutments and pontics go tooth by tooth, not as "a bridge".
- **The material.** Zirconia, lithium disilicate, metal-ceramic, acrylic. The technician may suggest something better, but they need to know where you started.
- **The shade and the guide.** A shade is only interpretable if the lab knows which guide it came from. Photographs with the tab held next to the tooth settle most of the questions before they are asked.
- **The occlusion.** Bite registration, opposing arch, facebow if there is one, and whether the case is mounted on an articulator.
- **What is in the box.** Impressions, models, intraoral scan, photographs, a diagnostic wax-up. Written on the prescription, so the lab knows what should have arrived.
- **Two dates, not one.** The date the work leaves and the date of the appointment where it is fitted. The second one is the one that matters.
- **Who prescribed it.** The clinician's name. This is not paperwork for its own sake: the lab needs it to be able to issue its statement.

> **The date that matters is the fitting, not the return.** A lab that knows the day and time of the appointment can sequence its bench. A lab told "some time next week" has to ring five practices to work out whose case is urgent.

## A tooth number needs a system attached

The notations in common use give different numbers to the same tooth, so a number on its own carries no meaning.

ISO 3950:2016, *Dentistry, Designation system for teeth and areas of the oral cavity*, is the standard behind the two-digit system, the one usually called FDI notation. The first digit is the quadrant and the second is the position from the midline.

Which one you use matters far less than that both ends use the same one and the prescription says so. If you send work abroad, that single line prevents the one mistake nobody catches until the case has been made.

## The workflow, step by step

A case passes through seven moments, and five of them need something written down.

1. **The work is decided at the appointment.** Tooth, restoration type and material go into the notes before any prescription exists.
2. **The impression or scan is taken.** If it is digital, name the file after the patient and the date, not with the serial number the scanner assigns.
3. **The prescription is written and the case goes out.** With the nine items above, and a line in the patient's notes saying the case has left and which lab has it.
4. **The lab confirms receipt.** What arrived, and the return date it is committing to. Without that confirmation you do not have a case, you have a parcel in transit.
5. **The try-in comes back, if the case has one.** Coping, bisque bake, wax try-in. Every trip back is a fresh prescription saying what to change, not a sticky note.
6. **The finished work arrives.** With the statement described below. Check it against the original prescription before the appointment, not with the patient in the chair.
7. **It is fitted and recorded.** What was fitted, which tooth, which materials, on what date, and where the statement is filed.

Step four is the one almost nobody systematises, and it is the only one that warns you in time. A case not confirmed within forty-eight hours is a case worth a phone call.

## What has to come back with the finished device

A dental appliance made for one named patient is a custom-made medical device, and that puts requirements on the lab.

The MHRA's guidance on custom-made devices in Great Britain describes one as "manufactured specifically in accordance with a written prescription of a registered medical practitioner" and "intended for the sole use of a particular patient". It notes the prescription may take the form of "a letter from a qualified person or a moulded impression of the shape of the required device together with the order".

The statement that accompanies the device has to carry, per the same guidance:

- **Data allowing identification of the device**, which the guidance spells out as "description, serial number, order number, generic name".
- **A statement that the device is intended for exclusive use by a particular patient**, together with the name.
- **The name of the qualified prescriber** and their place of work.
- **The particular features of the device** as specified in the relevant prescription.
- **A statement of conformity** with the essential requirements.
- **The manufacturer's name and address.**

The guidance is equally clear about who the statement is for. It "should be available to the named patient for whom the device has been manufactured", and patients should be "made aware that they can request a statement".

> **The statement is the lab's to sign, not yours.** The manufacturer is whoever makes the device. Your side of it is the written prescription, receiving the statement, and being able to hand it over when a patient asks.

This is not legal advice. It is what the sources listed at the end say, consulted on 28 August 2026. Practices in Ireland and the rest of the EU work to Regulation (EU) 2017/745 instead, whose Annex XIII sets out a similar statement with eight required contents.

## Who the manufacturer is when you mill in-house

The answer is uncomfortable for some practices: if you design and mill the restoration on your own unit, you are the manufacturer, and the manufacturer's obligations are yours.

The MHRA guidance says a manufacturer of custom-made devices or their UK responsible person must register with the MHRA if they place the devices on the market.

It gives one explicit retention figure, and it covers a category most dental work does not fall into: documentation for all active implantable medical devices "shall be kept for at least 15 years from the date of manufacture". For ordinary crown and bridge work, agree with your lab how long each side keeps what, and write that down rather than assuming.

| Document | Who issues it | What it has to carry |
|---|---|---|
| The lab prescription | The practice | Patient, teeth, work, material, shade, occlusion, dates and prescriber |
| The custom-made device statement | The manufacturer (the lab, or you if you mill) | Device identification, named patient, prescriber, features, conformity, manufacturer address |
| The fitting entry | The practice | What was fitted, which tooth, when, which materials, and where the statement is filed |

## What goes in the clinical record

The prescription lives inside the case. What has to survive for years is what ends up in the patient's notes.

1. **The date it went out and which lab has it**, so you can reconstruct who made what.
2. **The work and the tooth**, in the same notation the prescription used.
3. **The final material and shade**, including anything changed by phone halfway through.
4. **A reference to the statement received**, or the file itself if you work without paper.
5. **The fitting date and any problems.**

That last one pays for itself when the patient comes back three years later with a fracture and somebody has to decide whether it is a remake.

## When a case comes back wrong

A badly handled remake costs twice: the work, and the goodwill of a patient who sat in the chair for nothing.

1. **Check the work against the prescription before the appointment.** Tooth, material, shade, occlusion. Ten minutes the day before saves an hour the day after.
2. **Write the fault in measurable terms.** "Shade is off" is not information. "One shade lighter in the incisal third, compared with the tab in the attached photograph" is.
3. **Agree who bears the remake before it goes back**, not when the invoice arrives.
4. **Record the remake in the notes.** A second fitting date with no explanation is a hole in the case history.
5. **Look at the pattern each quarter.** If remakes cluster around one type of work or one material, the problem is in the prescription, not in the lab.
