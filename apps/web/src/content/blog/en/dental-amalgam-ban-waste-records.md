If you practise in the European Union, dental amalgam has been off the table since 1 January 2025 except where the dental practitioner deems it strictly necessary for a patient's specific medical needs. If you practise in the United States or Great Britain, no such ban applies to you, and the rule that does bind you is about the drain and the paperwork rather than the material. Northern Ireland is a third case again. The one thing every jurisdiction has in common is that the waste obligations do not end when the fillings stop, because they are triggered by **removing** old amalgam, not only by placing new.

This is not legal, clinical or waste-compliance advice. It is a reading of the official sources listed at the end, consulted on 7 October 2026.

## Three jurisdictions, three different answers

| | Use of amalgam | What governs the waste |
|---|---|---|
| European Union | ✗ Banned since 1 Jan 2025, specific medical need only | Art. 10, Regulation (EU) 2017/852 |
| United States | ✓ No federal ban on placing it | 40 CFR Part 441 (EPA dental rule) |
| Great Britain | ✓ Not bound by Regulation (EU) 2024/1849 | Retained Art. 10 plus domestic waste law |
| Northern Ireland | ~ Permitted for UK-resident patients to 31 Dec 2034 | Art. 10 as applied under the Windsor Framework |

> **Do not read the EU ban into a US practice.** There is no federal prohibition on placing dental amalgam in the United States. What there is, and what carries penalties, is a pretreatment standard about what reaches the sewer and what you can show an inspector.

## What the EU actually did

Regulation (EU) 2024/1849 amended the 2017 mercury regulation and inserted a new paragraph into Article 10. From 1 January 2025 dental amalgam shall not be used for dental treatment in the Union, except when deemed strictly necessary by the dental practitioner based on the specific medical needs of the patient.

Two dates sit either side of that one. Export of dental amalgam has been prohibited since 1 January 2025, and import and manufacture have been prohibited since 1 July 2026, with the same medical-need exception. A temporary derogation let some member states carry on until 30 June 2026, and it has now expired.

- **The derogation was narrow and is gone.** It applied only where amalgam was the only publicly reimbursed filling material at a rate of at least 90 % under national law. It ended on 30 June 2026.
- **The Commission revisits the exemptions by 31 December 2029**, taking the availability of mercury-free alternatives into account.
- **Great Britain is outside all of this.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1849 is an EU act, and the UK is not an EU member state. Article 10 of the 2017 regulation survives in domestic law as retained EU law, which is why the separator duty still reads the same there, but the 2024 amendment does not follow it across.

Northern Ireland is the genuine exception rather than a rounding error. The Northern Ireland Assembly's Windsor Framework Democratic Scrutiny Committee reports that under Commission Notice C/2024/4675 "dental amalgam may be used in Northern Ireland for the purpose of dental treatment for patients residing in the United Kingdom, and dental amalgam can be imported from outside the Union for that purpose until 31 December 2034".

## The US rule is about records, and that is the part people miss

![Patient record with the odontogram, clinical alerts, active plan and next appointment](/screenshots/dental-chart.png)

*A tooth-by-tooth chart: which restoration is which material, recorded where it can be found again.*

The EPA's Dental Office Point Source Category rule has applied to existing dental dischargers since 14 July 2020. It asks for three things, and only the first is equipment.

1. **An amalgam separator or equivalent device** meeting ANSI/ADA Specification 108 or ISO 11143, which is to say at least 95 % removal efficiency, sized for the maximum discharge rate and inspected per the manufacturer's manual.
2. **Two banned practices.** Waste amalgam from chair-side traps, screens, vacuum pump filters, tools and cuspidors must not be discharged to a treatment works, and the lines must not be cleaned with oxidising or acidic cleaners below pH 6 or above pH 8.
3. **A One-Time Compliance Report** to the local Control Authority, kept available for inspection for as long as the practice operates, and resubmitted within 90 days of a change of ownership.

> **A broken separator has a ten-day clock.** The rule says a malfunctioning unit must be repaired or replaced as soon as possible and no later than 10 business days after the discharger discovers the malfunction. That is a deadline that only exists if someone noticed and wrote the date down.

## The three-year file

This is the half of the rule that nobody writes about, and it is the half that gets audited. Part 441 requires four kinds of record to be kept and made available for inspection, physically or electronically, for a minimum of three years.

- **Each inspection of the separator**, with the date, who conducted it, the result, and a summary of any follow-up action.
- **Every replacement of the amalgam retaining container**, with the date.
- **Every pickup or shipment of collected amalgam**, with the date and the name of the permitted or licensed facility that received it.
- **Every repair or replacement**, with the date, who did it, and what was done, including make and model.

Practices that neither place amalgam nor remove it except in unplanned emergencies can certify that to the Control Authority and are exempt from the rest. Practising exclusively in oral pathology, oral and maxillofacial radiology or surgery, orthodontics, periodontics or prosthodontics also falls outside the rule.

## What the EU text says about removal, in every jurisdiction that kept it

![A treatment plan with its stages](/screenshots/treatment-plan.png)

*A staged treatment plan: every line carries its own date, which is where the reason for a material choice belongs.*

Article 10(4) covers operators of dental facilities "in which dental amalgam is used or dental amalgam fillings or teeth containing such fillings are removed". That wording is why the separator did not become optional in 2025, and why it will not become optional for decades: somebody is still taking out the fillings that were placed before 2019.

Article 10(6) is the other half. Amalgam waste "including amalgam residues, particles and fillings, and teeth, or parts thereof, contaminated by dental amalgam" must be handled and collected by an authorised waste management establishment, and practitioners must not release it into the environment directly or indirectly under any circumstances. Across the EU it travels under List of Waste code **18 01 10\***, dental amalgam waste, where the asterisk means hazardous.

## Where software helps and where it does not

In the EU the medical-need exception is now something a practice has to be able to prove for a single treatment, years later. That belongs on the treatment line in the clinical record, dated, rather than in a policy on a wall. In the US the equivalent is narrower but the same shape: the inspection dates, the pickup dates and the receiving facility's name are documents that need somewhere to live.

In Dentalpin the justification is written on the treatment line and keeps its author and its date in the clinical record, which is the form it needs to be in if anyone asks two years later. The code is open, so that can be audited rather than taken on trust, and the [pricing is published](/en/pricing/). The separator file is not something any practice management system solves: that is a contract with a licensed hauler and a folder somebody has to keep current.
