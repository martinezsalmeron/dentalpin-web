Consent to treat does not authorise publication, and neither does consent to take a clinical photograph for the record. Publishing on your website or social media needs a separate written permission, tied to a stated purpose, naming the channels, limited in time and revocable. In the UK the General Dental Council requires that consent to be explicit and specific; in the United States it is a marketing authorization under HIPAA.

This is not legal advice. It is a reading of the official sources listed at the end, consulted on 6 October 2026.

## Three different permissions, not one with three uses

Most practices hold one signature and assume it covers everything. It does not, because each permission answers a different purpose, and purpose is what limits a permission.

- **Consent to treatment.** Authorises the clinical act. It says nothing about communication.
- **Consent to clinical photography.** Authorises taking the image and keeping it in the record, and at most using it for teaching.
- **Consent to publish.** Authorises putting it outside the practice. It is the only one that makes an Instagram post lawful, and it is the one that is usually missing.

> **The third does not follow from the first two.** A signature covering "photography for medical, scientific or educational purposes" is exactly the signature that fails when the image ends up on a commercial profile.

## What the GDC actually requires

Standard 4.2.3 of the *Standards for the Dental Team* is short and absolute: *"You must not post any information or comments about patients on social networking or blogging sites. If you use professional social media to discuss anonymised cases for the purpose of discussing best practice you must be careful that the patient or patients cannot be identified."*

The GDC's *Guidance on using social media* then sets out what consent has to look like. You must *"maintain and protect patients' information by not publishing any information which could identify them on social media without their explicit consent"*, and, on obtaining it: *"When obtaining consent you should specify, to the patient how exactly the information you propose to share will be used, for what purpose and where it will be available."*

Three words in that sentence do the work. **How**, **for what purpose**, and **where**. A blanket media-release form says none of them.

| What you hold | Covers the clinical record | Covers teaching | Covers your website and social media |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treatment consent form | ✓ Yes | ✗ No | ✗ No |
| Clinical photography consent | ✓ Yes | ~ Only if it says so | ✗ No |
| Teaching or case-presentation consent | ✓ Yes | ✓ Yes | ✗ No |
| Explicit publication consent naming the channels | ✓ Yes | ~ Only if it says so | ✓ Yes, within its purpose and term |

## The advertising layer, which is separate from consent

Even with a valid consent, the image is an advertising claim. The ASA treats before and after photographs the same way it treats testimonials, so CAP Code rules 3.47 to 3.50 apply, and its advice is explicit about the evidence you have to be holding before you publish: *"hold signed and dated proof that the photos are genuine and have not been manipulated"*, and *"retain appropriate material to be able to demonstrate whether any re-touching has been carried out, in the event of an investigation"*.

That changes what you keep, not just whether you post. The "after" image has to be representative of what patients can generally expect, because a visual claim misleads in exactly the same way a written one does.

> **The rule is about exaggeration, not only about editing.** A different light or a different angle between the two shots overstates the result as effectively as retouching does, and the practice has to be able to show what it did either way. Shooting both images under the same conditions is the version of this that survives a complaint.

## In the United States it is an authorization, not a consent

Under HIPAA the general rule in 45 CFR § 164.508(a)(1) is that *"a covered entity may not use or disclose protected health information without an authorization that is valid under this section"*, and paragraph (a)(3) removes any doubt about marketing: *"a covered entity must obtain an authorization for any use or disclosure of protected health information for marketing"*, with narrow exceptions for face-to-face communication and promotional gifts of nominal value.

A valid authorization has required core elements under § 164.508(c)(1), and they map almost exactly onto the list below: a specific description of the information, who may use it, to whom it may be disclosed, each purpose, an expiration date or event, and the patient's signature and date. State dental boards add their own advertising rules on top.

## What a publication permission has to say

1. **What it is for.** Promotion of the practice, in those words, not "communication purposes".
2. **Where.** The channels, one by one: website, Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, printed material, paid ads.
3. **Which images.** The specific ones, identified by date or reference, not "photographs of my treatment".
4. **For how long.** A term with an end date, and what happens when it expires.
5. **Who else gets them.** An agency running your accounts is a processor and needs its [data processing agreement](/en/blog/data-processing-agreement-dental-software/).
6. **How it is withdrawn.** A named route and a response time, because consent is revocable at any moment.
7. **What does not change if it is withdrawn.** Care does not depend on that signature, and the document should say so.

Point 6 is the one that fails in practice. Deleting the post from your own account is the easy half; the image has already been reshared, saved and indexed, and a withdrawal you cannot execute is a permission you never really had.

## What the software has to do

*A patient timeline: a permission and its withdrawal are two dated events, and this is where they read in order.*

Four concrete requirements fall out of the rules above:

- **Hold the publication consent as its own versioned document**, separate from the treatment consent. A yes/no checkbox cannot tell you what the patient signed or when.
- **Record purpose and channels on it**, because that is what bounds the permission and what you will have to produce.
- **Attach it to the specific image**, not to the patient. A 2024 permission does not cover a 2026 photograph.
- **Make withdrawal propagate.** That means the record has to know where each image was published in the first place, or nobody can take it down everywhere.

The ASA's evidence requirement adds a fifth: keep the original, unedited file alongside the published one, with its date. Logging every access to clinical images is worth the same as [logging access to the record](/en/blog/audit-trail-dental-records/), and for the same reason.
