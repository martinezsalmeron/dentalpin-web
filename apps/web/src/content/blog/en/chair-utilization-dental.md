Chair utilization is the hours with a patient in the chair divided by the hours that chair has a clinician assigned to it, worked out chair by chair and week by week. The mistake is almost always in the denominator: available hours are not the hours the practice has the lights on, they are the hours that particular operatory is staffed. And the room to grow usually sits in the gaps the schedule creates for itself, not in no-shows.

One number for the whole practice decides nothing. Two operatories, one at ninety percent and one at fifty, average out to seventy and describe a practice that does not exist.

## The formula, and the denominator most people get wrong

The numerator is the easy half: total booked hours with the patient actually present in that chair over the period.

Available hours are the hours somebody is assigned to work that chair. An operatory with no hygienist on Tuesdays does not have gaps on Tuesdays, it is simply closed, and putting those hours in the denominator sinks the number in a way nobody can act on.

> **Measure every chair separately and never publish the average.** The average is the one number here that changes no decision, because it hides the exact operatory that needs fixing.

Fix the period too, then leave it alone. Weeks work better than months for acting on, because a month blurs the difference between a full Thursday and an empty Monday, and that difference is the action.

## What counts as an occupied chair

| Time slot | Does it count as occupied? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Appointment with the patient in the chair | ✓ Yes | That is the definition of the numerator |
| Patient arrives thirty minutes late | ~ Only the real chair time | The rest is a gap, and it is worth seeing as one |
| Turnaround and setup between patients | ✗ Not in the numerator | Necessary time, but not time with a patient |
| Gap left by a no-show | ✗ No | It stays in the denominator, which is what makes it visible |
| Emergency slot that got used | ✓ Yes | If it filled, it was a productive hour |
| Emergency slot that went unused | ✗ No | It is still a gap, one you reserved yourselves |
| Hour with nobody assigned | Outside the calculation | Neither numerator nor denominator |

The turnaround row is the one that starts arguments. If setup time is excluded from the numerator, a well run practice never reaches one hundred percent, and that is correct. That margin is not a failure, it is what makes the next appointment possible.

## Where the gaps actually come from

There are four sources, they are fixed in different ways, and measuring them together guarantees working on the wrong one.

- **No-shows and late cancellations.** The first thing everyone looks at and rarely the biggest. They leave a gap announced a few hours ahead, when it is already hard to fill.
- **Structural gaps.** Slots nobody ever filled: first thing in the morning, straight after lunch, Friday afternoon. No patient creates these, the shape of the schedule does.
- **Defensive blocks.** Time held back for emergencies, for hygiene, or for a long case that might come in. When they go unused, they are gaps the practice created for itself.
- **Turnaround time.** Fifteen minutes between patients, times ten patients, times five days, is twelve and a half hours a month per operatory. It never shows up as a gap on the schedule and it is the most expensive one.

Count each source for two weeks before changing anything. The split is usually a surprise, and it tends to move the work from reminders towards the schedule template itself.

## What an empty chair hour costs

This figure does not come from an industry report, it comes from your own books, which is exactly why it is the reliable one.

1. **Add up the fixed monthly costs** of that operatory: rent, assigned staff, equipment, insurance, software, supplies.
2. **Count the open hours in the month** for that chair, using the denominator above.
3. **Divide.** With $12,000 of fixed cost and 320 open hours, that is $37.50 an hour, occupied or not.
4. **Compare it with production per chair hour** for that operatory. The gap between the two figures is what an empty hour genuinely costs.

Those are round numbers for illustration, not a benchmark. Run with your own, the calculation turns an argument about percentages into a conversation about dollars a month, which is the one that moves decisions.

> **Unused capacity is the normal state, not the exception.** In the quarterly survey by the American Dental Association's Health Policy Institute, "around one-quarter of dentists reported that they were not busy enough" in the second quarter of 2026, while the average wait for a new patient appointment rose to 13.9 days. It is US data and it is self-reported busyness rather than measured chair time, so it is not a target to hit, but it does put an operatory with gaps in it firmly in the majority.

## Raising it without opening longer

1. **Start with the structural gaps**, not the no-shows. Find the slots that have been empty for three months and actively offer them for the treatments that fit them, instead of waiting for someone to ask.
2. **Keep a real short-notice list** with name, treatment and availability. Without one, filling tomorrow's cancellation means calling people at random.
3. **Group appointments by length.** Holding the long afternoon slot for long procedures stops you from creating the forty minute gap that fits nothing.
4. **Review the blocks monthly.** An emergency block used twice in ten is reserving eight hours for nothing; shrinking it to the times emergencies actually arrive gives those hours back.
5. **Time the turnaround for a week.** It usually shortens by preparing the next operatory while the current appointment finishes, which is organisation rather than investment.
6. **Confirm the day before only where no-shows happen**, not across the whole schedule. The no-show rate by slot already tells you where.

## When high utilization is a bad sign

| Situation | Good news? | What is actually happening |
|---|---|---|
| Sustained 95 percent | ✗ No | No room for an emergency, and waiting times start to stretch |
| High utilization, low production per hour | ~ Depends | The chair is full of short, low value appointments |
| One hundred percent on a single day | ✓ Yes | A day that happened to fit together, nothing more to read into it |
| High utilization with a high no-show rate | ✗ No | You are overbooking to compensate, and the day everyone turns up nobody fits |
| Mid utilization with a long waiting list | ✗ No | The problem is the schedule template, not demand |

> **One hundred percent is not the target.** A chair booked to the edge cannot absorb an emergency, cannot take an appointment that overruns, and turns any surprise into an afternoon of running late. Slack has a price too, and so does the lack of it.

Reference figures circulate for this metric, usually somewhere between seventy five and eighty five percent. Almost none of them say which country, what size of practice, what treatment mix or which definition of available hours they used, and several are published by companies selling scheduling software. Your own figure for the same month last year answers a question that actually means something.

## Measuring it this month

1. **Write the definition down on one page**, what goes in the numerator and what goes in the denominator, and keep it where the team can see it.
2. **Pull the last four weeks per chair**, by hand off a printed schedule if that is what it takes.
3. **Sort one week of gaps** into the four sources above and note how many hours each accounts for.
4. **Pick the biggest source** and apply exactly one action from the list, with a name and a date against it.
5. **Measure again after four weeks** using the same definition. Changing the formula halfway is the fastest way to never know whether it worked.
