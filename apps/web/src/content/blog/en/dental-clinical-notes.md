Every visit needs seven things in the record: the date and who provided the care, why the patient attended, what you found when you examined, the diagnosis, what you did (teeth, materials, batch numbers, anaesthetic), what you discussed and what the patient agreed to, and what happens next. Write it during the appointment or immediately after, never at the end of the day.

Everything else is refinement. Notes that carry those seven things treat the patient, answer a complaint years later, and let a colleague pick the case up without phoning you.

## The minimum entry

- **Date and clinician.** Who treated, who assisted. In a practice with several dentists, an unsigned entry belongs to nobody.
- **Reason for attending**, in the patient's own words where they add something. "Sore when I bite down since Saturday" beats "check-up".
- **Medical history checked, not copied forward.** Allergies, medication, pregnancy, anticoagulants, cardiac history. What matters is evidencing that you asked again and whether anything changed.
- **Examination findings, including relevant negatives.** "Soft tissues clear" is information. Silence does not prove you looked.
- **Diagnosis**, tied to a tooth or a sextant. Without it, the treatment that follows has no written justification.
- **What you did**, in reproducible detail: teeth treated, anaesthetic and dose, material and batch, radiographs taken and why they were justified.
- **What you explained and what the patient agreed**, including the options declined. A recorded refusal protects both sides.
- **Plan and next appointment.** What is outstanding and when. A plan that lives only in the treating clinician's head disappears with the first sick day.

> **You are writing for whoever reads this in five years.** That may be a colleague, an expert witness or the patient. If the entry only makes sense with your memory next to it, it is a personal note, not a clinical record.

## What the GDC actually asks for

Standard 4.1 of the GDC's *Standards for the Dental Team* is one sentence: "You must make and keep contemporaneous, complete and accurate patient records". The guidance underneath it is where the detail sits.

It expects the record to include an up-to-date medical history, radiographs, consent forms, photographs and models, any audio or visual recordings of consultations, laboratory prescriptions and statements of conformity, referral letters, the details of discussions that show consent was valid, and the name or initials of the treating clinician.

It also sets two conditions that decide whether the rest is worth anything. Records must be "clear, legible, accurate, and can be readily understood by others", and any amendment must be clearly marked and dated.

## What changes with the type of appointment

NHS England published *Dental Record Keeping Standards: a consensus approach* on 17 October 2019, built with the profession through a Delphi process. Its useful output is three templates rather than one: a new patient examination, a recall examination and an urgent examination, with each item sorted as essential, aspirational, conditional on presentation, or not required.

The items it covers are the ones an argument usually turns on: personal information, medical history, reason for attendance, social history including smoking, alcohol and diet, dental anxiety, the effect of the dentition on quality of life, extra oral and soft tissue examination, BPE, charting and its update, caries, defective and existing restorations, previous endodontic treatment, mobility, prostheses, occlusion and occlusal abnormality, tooth wear, the recall interval, and for radiographs a separate line each for recording and justifying them, evaluating them clinically, and grading their quality.

The table below is a practical summary of what usually changes between those three settings. The classification of each individual item as essential, aspirational or conditional is in the NHS document itself, and it is worth reading against your own templates.

| | New patient | Recall | Urgent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full medical history | ✓ Always | ~ Reviewed and updated | ✓ Always, even if brief |
| Full charting | ✓ Baseline | ~ Update what changed | ~ The area involved |
| BPE | ✓ Baseline | ~ Per risk and last date | ✗ Rarely the priority |
| Radiographs justified in writing | ✓ Yes | ~ Per interval | ✓ Usually decide the diagnosis |
| Recall interval | ✓ Set it | ✓ Reset it | ~ State the follow-up instead |
| Definitive or temporary | ~ N/A | ~ N/A | ✓ Say which, in words |

That last row is the one urgent entries miss. Writing "temporary dressing, patient told to return for definitive restoration" removes most of the argument when the patient comes back two months later.

> **A declined option is worth as much as a completed one.** The patient who refused the extraction, or postponed the root canal, needs the same care in the record as the treatment you carried out. It is the only evidence the alternative was ever offered.

## Writing it in two minutes

1. **Open the record before the patient sits down** and read the last entry. Thirty seconds that stops repeated questions and unwelcome surprises.
2. **Update the medical history out loud** as they settle. If nothing has changed, record that, dated.
3. **Chart findings as you examine**, not afterwards. Notes reconstructed at the end of the day lose detail and gain adjectives.
4. **Write the procedure while the nurse clears down.** Tooth, material, batch, anaesthetic, anything unexpected.
5. **Close with the plan and the next appointment**, then check the entry against one question: if I am not here tomorrow, can somebody continue this?

## Corrections: never delete, always add

A mistake in the record is corrected by adding to it. In an electronic record the system should keep what the entry said before, who changed it and when. On paper, strike it through so the original still reads, then date and sign the correction.

This is not defensive bureaucracy. It is what makes the record evidence of what happened, rather than a document somebody could have rewritten afterwards, and it is equally what lets you show that today's note was written today.

## What should not be in the notes

- **Judgements about the person.** "Difficult patient" is not a finding. Describe the behaviour and its clinical consequence, which is more useful anyway.
- **Abbreviations only your practice uses.** They work until another clinician, or a court, has to read them.
- **Copied-forward entries.** Two visits with identical wording undermine both.
- **Third party information.** What the accompanying adult volunteers about themselves is not the patient's clinical record.
- **Decisions made over text message.** If a message changes the treatment, it belongs in the record. Left on the front desk phone, it never happened.
