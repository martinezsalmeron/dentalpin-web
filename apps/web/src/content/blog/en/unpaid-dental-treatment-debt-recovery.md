In England and Wales an unpaid dental bill ends up as a county court money claim, the defendant has 14 days from service of the particulars of claim to file a defence, and the claim has to be brought within six years of the cause of action accruing. In the US the court route is state law, but the question practices get wrong is a federal one, and the answer runs the opposite way to Europe: HIPAA already permits disclosure for collection.

Either way, the thing that decides the outcome is the paperwork behind the amount, not the number of reminders sent.

> **The two jurisdictions give opposite answers on sending the file to a collector.** In the US, "collection activities" sit inside the HIPAA definition of payment, so the disclosure is permitted without authorisation. In the UK, the General Dental Council's standard 4.3 is that you must only release a patient's information without their permission "in exceptional circumstances", and chasing money is not one.

This is not the post about the ledger, and not the one about financing. Knowing who owes what and chasing it inside the practice is [patient debt and payment plans](/en/blog/patient-payment-plans-tracking/), and a third party lending the patient the money up front is [patient financing](/en/blog/patient-financing-dental-treatment/).

Here the patient is not going to pay and the next step leaves the building. That is a different decision, made by a different person: the one choosing between writing it off, sending it out and going to court.

## England and Wales: the money claim

You apply to a county court for money you are owed, online or by post, and the fee scales with the amount claimed. GOV.UK lists the bands.

| Amount claimed | Court fee |
|---|---|
| Up to £300 | £35 |
| £300.01 to £500 | £50 |
| £500.01 to £1,000 | £70 |
| £1,000.01 to £1,500 | £80 |
| £1,500.01 to £3,000 | £115 |
| £3,000.01 to £5,000 | £205 |
| £5,000.01 to £10,000 | £455 |
| £10,000.01 to £200,000 | 5% of the claim |

Two things about that table. You can claim the fee back if you win, and if you do not know the exact amount you cannot claim online at all: you have to use the paper form, estimate, and pay the band for your estimate.

The steps, with the deadlines that actually bind:

1. **Check the claim is still in time.** Section 5 of the Limitation Act 1980: "An action founded on simple contract shall not be brought after the expiration of six years from the date on which the cause of action accrued."
2. **Put the figure on the treatment, not on the plan.** The amount you claim is the treatment carried out and invoiced, with dates. A quote that was accepted and then partly delivered is not a claim for the quote.
3. **Consider mediation first.** GOV.UK puts it plainly: "A mediation service could be quicker and cheaper than going to court."
4. **Issue the claim and pay the fee.** Online if you know the amount, on the paper form if you do not.
5. **Wait for the response.** Under rule 15.4 of the Civil Procedure Rules the period for filing a defence is 14 days after service of the particulars of claim, or 28 days if the defendant files an acknowledgment of service. The parties can agree to extend that by up to 28 more days under rule 15.5, and the defendant then has to tell the court in writing.
6. **No response, ask for judgment.** Online if you claimed online, or form N225 for a specified amount on a paper claim.
7. **If it is disputed, expect a hearing.** The court may send a questionnaire, there is a further fee, and you may be offered or required to attend mediation.

## The US: HIPAA permits it, the minimum necessary rule limits it

The federal rule is not the obstacle most practices assume. Under 45 CFR § 164.506(c)(1), "a covered entity may use or disclose protected health information for its own treatment, payment, or health care operations", and § 164.501 defines payment to include "billing, claims management, collection activities, obtaining payment under a contract for reinsurance".

So the disclosure is permitted. What constrains it is everything around it:

- **Minimum necessary.** Section 164.502(b)(1) requires a covered entity to "make reasonable efforts to limit protected health information to the minimum necessary to accomplish the intended purpose of the use, disclosure, or request". An agency chasing a balance needs the balance, not the chart.
- **A business associate agreement.** Under § 164.502(e)(1)(i) you may disclose to a business associate if you obtain "satisfactory assurance" that they will appropriately safeguard the information, and § 164.502(e)(2) requires that to be documented in a written contract.
- **State law can be stricter.** HIPAA is a floor. Dental practice acts and state health-information statutes sit on top of it.

The second federal point is who the rules apply to. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act defines a debt collector at 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) as a person in a business whose principal purpose is collecting debts, or who regularly collects "debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another", and it excludes "any officer or employee of a creditor while, in the name of the creditor, collecting debts for such creditor".

> **Collecting your own bills in your own name keeps you outside the FDCPA, and inventing a name takes you back inside it.** The same definition "includes any creditor who, in the process of collecting his own debts, uses any name other than his own". A practice that sends letters as a made-up recovery office has made itself a debt collector.

## What never works, in either country

- **Do not withhold the records over an unpaid bill.** The right of access does not depend on the balance, and it has its own post: [when a patient asks for their dental records](/en/blog/patient-requests-their-dental-records/).
- **A credit file entry is not a dunning step.** Reporting a patient to a credit reference agency is its own regulated act with its own conditions, and it belongs with [GDPR in the dental clinic](/en/blog/gdpr-dental-clinic/) rather than in the reminder sequence.
- **Do not chase over WhatsApp.** The reasoning is in [sending patient data by email or WhatsApp](/en/blog/sending-patient-data-by-email-or-whatsapp/), and the answer there is not yes.

## The record that makes the claim stand up

Whichever route you take, the same five things decide whether the amount survives scrutiny: what was quoted, what was accepted, what was actually carried out and when, what was invoiced against it, and what was paid. Everything else is correspondence.

That is also the honest test for whether to proceed at all. If you cannot produce those five things for a given balance, the problem is not the patient's unwillingness to pay, and a court is not going to fix it.
