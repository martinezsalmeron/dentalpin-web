Asking a patient to recommend you carries no restriction anywhere. Paying them does, and the limit is lower than most referral schemes assume. In the United States, giving anything worth more than $15 per item or $75 a year to a patient covered by Medicare or Medicaid can expose the practice to civil monetary penalties, and cash or a gift card is outside the allowance at any value. In the United Kingdom the figure does not exist, because the rule is framed as a duty rather than a threshold.

What follows is where each of those sits in writing, the second problem that catches almost every scheme regardless of the reward, and what still works once you stop paying. This is not legal advice: it is a reading of the sources listed at the end, all consulted on 24 October 2026.

## The United States: the threshold is $15, and it is not about dentistry

The provision that decides this is not in dental law at all. Section 1128A(a)(5) of the Social Security Act, enacted as part of HIPAA in 1996, reaches anyone who offers a patient something designed to steer where they go for care.

The Office of Inspector General describes it this way: a person who offers or transfers to a Medicare or Medicaid beneficiary any remuneration that the person *"knows or should know is likely to influence the beneficiary's selection of a particular provider, practitioner, or supplier"* of payable items or services *"may be liable for civil monetary penalties (CMPs) of up to $10,000 for each wrongful act"*.

Two details decide whether a referral scheme is inside it.

- **"Remuneration" is broader than money.** The statute defines it to include *"waivers of copayments and deductible amounts (or any part thereof) and transfers of items or services for free or for other than fair market value"*. A free hygiene visit is a transfer of a service for free, so it counts.
- **There is a nominal-value allowance, and it is specific.** In a policy statement dated 7 December 2016, OIG raised its interpretation of nominal value to *"a retail value of no more than $15 per item or $75 in the aggregate per patient on an annual basis"*, adding that *"the items may not be cash or cash equivalents"* and that a gift at or below those thresholds *"need not fit into an exception"*.

> **A $25 gift card fails twice.** It is above the per-item figure and it is a cash equivalent, which the policy statement excludes at any value. The practices that get this wrong are almost never the ones handing out a toothbrush.

The scope limit matters as much as the threshold. The provision is about beneficiaries of Medicare and state health care programs, so a practice that takes no federal-program patients at all is not inside it. State dental boards and state law then set their own rules on patient inducements, and those differ enough that the only reliable answer is your own board's.

## The United Kingdom: a duty, not a number

The General Dental Council's Standards for the Dental Team does not name referral rewards. What it sets is a priority, in standard 1.7: *"You must put patients' interests before your own or those of any colleague, business or organisation"*.

Three numbered standards sit under it and are the ones to read against a scheme:

1. **1.7.1** requires that *"You must always put your patients' interests before any financial, personal or other gain"*.
2. **1.7.5** requires you to *"refuse any gifts, payment or hospitality if accepting them could affect"* your professional judgment, which is the mirror image of the question and covers what you accept rather than what you give.
3. **1.7.6** says that on a referral to another member of the dental team, *"you must make sure that the referral is made in the patients' best interests rather than for your own, or another team member's, financial gain or benefit"*.

Read together, those govern a dentist's own referrals rather than a patient's recommendation of the practice. So the honest position is that the GDC standards consulted do not prohibit rewarding a patient for a recommendation, and do prohibit letting any gain come before the patient's interest. A scheme that pays per new patient makes the practice's interest in volume visible to anyone reading it, which is the test that actually applies.

## The reward is not the only problem

Strip the payment out and most schemes still trip on the same step, which is the moment the patient hands over a friend's phone number and reception texts them an offer.

In the UK, regulation 22 of the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations 2003 says a sender may not send unsolicited direct marketing by electronic mail *"unless the recipient of the electronic mail has previously notified the sender that he consents"*. The soft opt-in in paragraph (3) does not help, because it requires that the sender obtained the details *"in the course of the sale or negotiations for the sale of a product or service to that recipient"*. The friend negotiated nothing with you, and the person who consented is not the person being messaged.

- **Consent is personal to the subscriber.** A patient saying "she won't mind" is the patient's permission, not the friend's.
- **Storing the number is already processing.** Writing a non-patient's name and mobile into your system is the processing of their personal data, and it needs a lawful basis under Article 6 UK GDPR before the first message is ever sent.
- **The compliant version is dull and works.** The patient passes your number to the friend, and the friend calls you. Nothing about a third party enters your system and nobody receives a message they did not ask for.

> **This is the step that converts a marketing idea into a data problem.** The reward is a question for the regulator of the profession. The contact details are a question for the data protection regulator, and the two are answerable separately.

## What to do instead, in order

Almost everything that actually produces recommendations survives the removal of the reward, because the reward was never the mechanism.

1. **Ask when the patient is pleased**, which is at the end of a treatment that went well, not during a payment call. One sentence, the same words, from the same person.
2. **Give them something to pass on**, a card with the number and the address. Someone willing to recommend you rarely has your details to hand at the moment it comes up.
3. **Make first contact easy**, with online booking and a phone somebody answers. Recommendations are lost between the intention and the call far more often than they are lost for want of a prize.
4. **Ask every new patient how they found you**, and record it on the chart at registration. It is a field, not a project.
5. **Close the loop with the person who recommended you** by telling them their friend came in. That is not a payment, and it is what makes them do it again.
6. **Look at the number quarterly.** If one new patient in five arrives by recommendation, you know how much of your schedule depends on how you treat the ones you already have.

## The part that is software

None of the above is solved by buying a program. Two parts of it are either in the software or they are a spreadsheet nobody updates: a referral-source field at registration that is mandatory and can be grouped afterwards, and a report that tells you how many of this quarter's registrations came from it.

Check both in the system you already have before building anything: if the field does not exist, or exists but cannot be grouped, step four is not measurable however diligently reception asks.
