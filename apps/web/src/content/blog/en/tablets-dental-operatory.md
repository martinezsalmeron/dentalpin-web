A tablet at the chair does three things well: it lets you read the clinical record without leaving the patient, it shows that patient their own odontogram or treatment plan on a screen you can turn towards them, and it collects a signature. As the only computer in the operatory it does badly, because long clinical notes and imaging software still want a keyboard and Windows.

Before you compare any models, one decision outranks all the others: how the device gets clean between one patient and the next. That question rules out more tablets than price does.

## What it is actually for

A tablet does not replace the workstation in the operatory. It is added for the moments when standing in front of a fixed screen gets in the way.

- **Reading while you work.** Allergies, medical alerts, the last note and what was done at the previous visit, without crossing the room or taking a glove off to move a mouse.
- **Showing the patient.** An odontogram or a treatment plan on a screen that turns towards the chair lands far better than the same plan described out loud.
- **Signatures.** Consent forms and quotes, with a finger or a stylus, without printing anything.
- **Short forms.** The medical history questionnaire at a first visit, filled in by the patient in the waiting room instead of by the front desk.
- **Quick clinical photography.** The camera is good enough to document a before and after, though it does not replace proper clinical photography with a DSLR.

## Hygiene decides before the model does

The CDC classifies as a clinical contact surface anything contaminated by spray or spatter during treatment, and it names computer equipment explicitly: "Clinical contact surfaces, such as light handles, bracket trays, switches on dental units, and computer equipment, are likely be contaminated by direct spray or spatter generated during dental procedures".

Its recommendation for that kind of surface has two branches, and both change how a tablet gets used:

1. **Protect it with a barrier**, ideally on surfaces that are hard to clean, and **change the barrier between each patient**. When you remove it, look at the surface: if it became soiled, clean and disinfect it before the next patient.
2. **If a barrier cannot be used**, clean and then disinfect with an EPA-registered low-level hospital disinfectant effective against HIV and HBV. Where there is visible contamination with blood or other potentially infectious material, the disinfectant has to be intermediate-level with a tuberculocidal claim.

A tablet is precisely a surface that is hard to clean: it has openings, speakers and a seam where the glass meets the frame. In practice, that makes the first branch the one that works at the chair.

> **The disposable barrier is not an accessory, it is the plan.** A tablet inside a sleeve you change with every patient is dealt with in two seconds and does not depend on any manufacturer authorising any product. Without a barrier, the tablet is tied to whatever its maker permits, which is a good deal less than what is in the clinic cupboard.

The CDC is the US guideline and the one that publishes the criteria in most detail, but what governs your operatory is your own country's infection control requirements and your practice protocol. This is not clinical or legal advice.

## What the manufacturer lets you clean it with

This is where a lot of tablets drop off the list, and the information is published. Apple's cleaning page sets out what may be applied to the hard, nonporous surfaces of its products: "Using a 70 percent isopropyl alcohol wipe, 75 percent ethyl alcohol wipe, or Clorox Disinfecting Wipes, you may gently wipe the hard, nonporous surfaces". And what may not: "Don't use products containing bleach or hydrogen peroxide".

The same page adds three limits that matter in an operatory: "Don't spray cleaners directly onto the item", "Avoid getting moisture in any opening" and "don't submerge your Apple product in any cleaning agents".

There is one more thing worth checking before you buy. The iPad Pro technical specifications Apple publishes list **no IP rating** for water or dust resistance, unlike the splash and water resistance Apple does publish for iPhone. A device with no declared resistance is not the one you want to wipe with a soaking cloth three times a day.

The general rule that follows works for any brand: before buying, find the cleaning page for that specific model and check that what it authorises matches what your disinfection protocol actually uses. If the manufacturer publishes nothing, the answer is the barrier.

| Way of working | Fits the operatory? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Disposable barrier sleeve, changed every patient | ✓ Yes | What the CDC recommends for surfaces that are hard to clean |
| Direct disinfection with a manufacturer-approved product | ~ Depends on the model | Only if the maker publishes it and it matches your protocol |
| A generic wipe from the cupboard, unchecked | ✗ No | Can strip the oleophobic coating and follows neither guideline |
| Bleach or hydrogen peroxide on the glass | ✗ No | Apple advises against it explicitly for its products |
| Spraying the product onto the tablet | ✗ No | Drives moisture into the openings and is not authorised |
| Tablet outside the treatment field, handled ungloved | ✓ Yes | Avoids the problem instead of managing it |

That last row is what most practices settle on: the tablet lives on an articulated arm outside the working field, whoever does not have their hands in the mouth operates it, and the barrier covers the rest.

## The between-patients protocol, in five steps

1. **Mount the tablet on a fixed support**, an articulated arm or the wall, so it never ends up resting on the bracket tray or the worktop.
2. **Put the barrier sleeve on** before the patient comes in, the same as you do with the light handle.
3. **Decide who touches it during treatment.** Ideally the assistant, and not whoever is treating.
4. **Remove the barrier afterwards and look at the surface.** If it became soiled, clean and disinfect with whatever your protocol authorises for that device.
5. **Put it on charge and lock it.** A short auto-lock is part of the protocol, not an annoyance.

## A lost tablet is a data breach

A front desk computer stays in the building. A tablet goes to the waiting room, to the office and sometimes into a bag, and inside it is an open session against the clinical records of your entire patient base.

The GDPR treats it as exactly that. Article 32(1) requires the controller and the processor to apply "appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk", and the first measure it names is "the pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data".

In practice that is four concrete device settings:

- **Storage encryption switched on**, checked in the device settings rather than assumed.
- **Auto-lock at thirty seconds or a minute**, not five.
- **One user account per person in the practice software**, never a shared session left permanently open. If the audit trail says "tablet", it says nothing.
- **Remote wipe configured and tested** before it is needed, with someone who knows how to run it.

> **Test the remote wipe the day you set the tablet up.** A feature nobody has ever run is not a security measure, it is a ticked box. The afternoon a device goes missing is not the moment to work out whose password you need.

## What a tablet will not fix

Worth knowing before you buy, because these are the four most common returns.

- **Long clinical notes.** Typing a full entry on glass is slower, and slower means less gets written down. The workstation keyboard is still needed.
- **Imaging software.** Radiology and intraoral scanners are usually Windows programs with their own hardware. A tablet views exported images, it does not drive the equipment.
- **Being the only station in the operatory.** When the tablet runs flat or updates itself, the room cannot be left with no access to the record.
- **Wi-Fi coverage.** If the operatory has a weak signal, the tablet will feel it before any wired machine does. Fix the access point first.

## How to choose, in order

1. **Start from your disinfection protocol** and from which barrier sleeves you can source for the screen size you want.
2. **Find the manufacturer's cleaning page** for the two or three candidate models and drop the ones that publish nothing.
3. **Check your practice software runs in the tablet's browser**, with a real test at the chair rather than a demo in the office.
4. **Decide the mount before the model.** A decent articulated arm changes the working day more than two extra inches of screen.
5. **Buy one and use it for a month** in a single operatory before you buy four.

Step three is the one everybody skips and the one that causes most returns. If your practice software is an installed Windows program, a tablet is only ever going to be a remote viewer, with the latency that drags along.
