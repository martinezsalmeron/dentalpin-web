If you employ anyone in a dental practice, you have to keep a record of the hours they work, and the two English-speaking markets this post covers set different rules. In the United States the FLSA requires hours worked each day and total hours each workweek for non-exempt staff, with payroll records kept at least three years. In the United Kingdom the Working Time Regulations require records adequate to show the limits were respected, kept two years. Worth saying up front: your practice management software does not do this.

> **The most common mistake is not failing to keep records, it is assuming the practice software already does.** Your PMS knows which chair was busy and who delivered which procedure. It does not know what time the hygienist walked in or what time she left, and that is exactly what the rules are about.

## The United States: hours each day, totals each week

The Fair Labor Standards Act does not prescribe a time clock. It prescribes content. The Department of Labor's own recordkeeping guidance lists what you must have for every non-exempt worker, and two of those items are the ones practices get wrong:

- **"Hours worked each day."** Day by day, per person. Not a weekly figure you reconstruct later.
- **"Total hours worked each workweek."** A separate item from the daily figure, not a byproduct of it.

On retention, the guidance sets two different clocks, and mixing them up is how practices end up with gaps:

| Record | Minimum retention |
|---|---|
| Payroll records | ✓ At least three years |
| Records on which wage computations are based, such as time cards | ~ Two years |
| The daily and weekly hours themselves | ✓ Three years, as part of payroll records |

Two years for the time cards is the shorter of the two, and it is the one that matters in a wage dispute, because the time card is the underlying evidence.

A second point that catches dental practices specifically: the exemption question. Whether a practice manager or an associate is exempt from overtime is not settled by job title or by paying a salary, and getting it wrong converts a recordkeeping problem into a back-pay problem.

## The United Kingdom: adequate records, two years

Regulation 9 of the Working Time Regulations 1998 is shorter and more flexible than most practices expect, and the flexibility is the part worth knowing.

> **The employer must "keep records which are adequate to show whether" the limits have been complied with, and "retain such records for two years from the date on which they were made".** Records "may be created, maintained and kept in such manner and format as the employer reasonably thinks fit".

And then the sentence that surprises people: "An employer need not record each worker's daily working hours in order to comply with paragraph (1)" if the employer "is able to demonstrate compliance without doing so".

That is a genuinely lighter duty than the American one. It is not an exemption from records, it is a different test: you must be able to show the weekly limit and the other limits were met. For a practice where everyone works a fixed rota well inside the limits, the rota plus exception records can be enough. For a practice with variable part-time hours, it will not be, because nothing there demonstrates compliance by itself.

## The dental practice is the awkward case

This is not generic HR. A practice has a harder time than an office on one fixed schedule, for concrete reasons.

1. **The part-time hygienist.** Variable hours are precisely the case where a rota proves nothing and an actual record proves everything.
2. **The receptionist on a split shift.** Two starts and two finishes in one day. A system that allows one clock-in and one clock-out per day does not fit this practice.
3. **The associate who may not be an employee at all.** No employment relationship, no working time record to keep. But the classification is not decided by what the agreement is called.
4. **The trainee or apprentice.** If they are employed, they count like anyone else, whatever the arrangement is named.
5. **The practice owner.** Records are about employees. A sole proprietor does not keep a working time record on themselves under these rules.

> **Do not try to settle employment classification here.** Whether an associate is an employee or an independent contractor changes the recordkeeping duty and a great deal else, and the answer turns on how the work is actually done rather than on the contract's title. It belongs with the [post on associate dentist pay](/en/blog/associate-dentist-pay-calculation/), and it is a question for an employment lawyer.

## Three ways to keep it

Neither regime mandates a particular tool. What matters is that the record is per person, contemporaneous, and survives the retention period.

| | Paper timesheet | Spreadsheet | Time clock app |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handles a split shift | ✓ Yes | ✓ Yes | ~ Depends on the system |
| Cost | ✓ None | ✓ None | ✗ Monthly fee |
| Worker can see their own hours | ✗ Only on request | ~ If you grant access | ✓ Usually yes |
| Survives three years | ~ If the folder is not lost | ✓ With backups | ✓ Yes |
| Can be filled in after the fact | ✗ Nothing prevents it | ✗ Nothing prevents it | ✓ Captures the real time |
| Works without internet | ✓ Yes | ✓ Yes | ~ Depends on the system |

Paper is lawful and free, and in a three-person practice it can serve for years. Its weakness is not the law, it is that it gets filled in on Friday from memory, and a timesheet written from memory is worth little when someone disputes their hours.

## How to set it up this week

1. **Pick one medium and write the decision down.** Paper, spreadsheet or app, the same one for everybody.
2. **Decide how many entries a day there are.** On a split shift it is four, not two. Settling it in advance closes the afternoon gap.
3. **Record the daily figure and the weekly total separately.** In the US these are two distinct requirements, and a weekly total alone cannot be unwound into days.
4. **Write down who you treat as exempt, and why.** The reasoning is what you will need later, not the conclusion.
5. **Keep three years, not two.** The longer of the two clocks is the safe default, and it costs nothing extra.
6. **Back it up somewhere other than the front desk computer.** If it lives on one machine, you do not have three years of anything.
