You spot it with one question on the first call, measure it with a validated five-item scale, record it as an alert the whole team can see, and turn it into a longer first appointment with no treatment in it. All four decisions happen before the patient sits in the chair, and together they are the difference between a record that moves forward and a list of failed appointments nobody can explain.

What practices usually lack is not sympathy for the patient who arrives frightened. It is the record: the information exists, whoever answered the phone has it, and it never reaches the surgery.

## Anxiety is not phobia, and avoidance is what separates them

Almost everyone arrives at a dental appointment with some apprehension, and that is not a diagnosis. What turns fear into a clinical problem is the patient stopping coming.

The UK has unusually good numbers on this, because the Modified Dental Anxiety Scale was included in the 2009 Adult Dental Health Survey. Of 13,509 adults invited, 11,382 took part, and 11.6% scored in the high dental anxiety band. The mean score was 10.65 out of 25, 9.33 for men and 11.82 for women, and it falls with age: around 11.7 at ages 16 to 34 against about 9.0 at 75 and over.

The German S3 guideline on dental treatment anxiety, produced by the DGZMK with twenty six societies and organisations, puts clinically significant dental anxiety at roughly 5 to 10% of the population, and adds the part that matters operationally: those people avoid treatment as a rule.

> **Avoidance is the clinical signal, not discomfort.** A patient who arrives tense and gets treated is a tense patient. A patient who has not been seen for four years and turns up in pain is something else, and the appointment book is where that difference shows up first.

## The question that belongs on the first call

You do not need a psychological protocol to collect this. The S3 guideline recommends that the question about fear of dental treatment already appear on the initial medical history form, and that a patient who answers yes be able to rate it on a visual analogue scale built into the same form.

Translated into a practice that works off a phone and a management system, the circuit looks like this:

1. **Ask directly when booking.** "Are you nervous about dental treatment, or have you had a bad experience before?" It fits in the call and obliges nobody to explain themselves.
2. **If yes, a 0 to 100 scale.** "Nought to a hundred, where are you?" The S3 guideline uses that threshold: above 50% of the scale, move to a fuller questionnaire.
3. **Above 50, the full questionnaire in the waiting room.** Five questions, a minute, before they come through.
4. **Ask how long it has been since their last visit.** This is the question that changes the picture, and it gets its own section below.
5. **Put it in the record, not on a sticky note.** With the score and the date, because in a year nobody will remember whether the 70 came from the first call or from a check-up.
6. **Adjust the appointment before you hang up.** More minutes, and if the diary allows it, no treatment on day one.

## Measuring it: the MDAS and its cut-off

The Modified Dental Anxiety Scale is the instrument that fits a general practice best: five questions, each scored 1 to 5, total 5 to 25. It asks how the patient would feel about treatment tomorrow, sitting in the waiting room, having a tooth drilled, having their teeth scaled and polished, and having a local anaesthetic injection in the gum above an upper back tooth.

The published cut-off is 19. In the UK norms paper the authors describe it as the point at which "possible additional assistance may be required for successful treatment completion". The scale is free to use, with no permission or fee, provided the original publication is cited, and its authors publish it in thirty one languages.

| | VAS | MDAS (1995) | HAF (1999) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Items | 1 | 5 | 11 |
| Range | 0 to 100 mm | 5 to 25 | 11 to 55 |
| High anxiety threshold | > 50 | ≥ 19 | > 38 |
| Separates phobia? | ✗ No | ✗ No | ✓ Yes, with avoidance over 2 years |
| Internal consistency | 0.968 | 0.83 | 0.936 |
| What it is for | Screening in seconds | A comparable measure | An indicative diagnosis |

The published MDAS bands are: under 11 not anxious, 11 to 14 moderately anxious, 15 to 18 highly anxious, 19 and above extremely anxious. The authors themselves caution that fixing a cut-off is contentious, because dental anxiety is a continuum, and that the measure is "an approximation and not a hard and fast decisional aid". Use it to organise the appointment and to open a conversation, not to label the patient.

## The second question, the one that changes the diagnosis

A high score on its own says the patient has a bad time. A high score plus avoidance is a different matter.

The S3 guideline records this as a recommendation carrying strong consensus: where high anxiety is found, ask in addition how long the patient has been avoiding appointments. High anxiety together with avoidance gives a working suspicion of clinically significant dental anxiety, and a medical specialist or a psychological psychotherapist should be brought in.

> **One in two patients with clinically significant dental anxiety has at least one other mental health condition.** That is the guideline's own reason for not keeping this inside the dental surgery. What gets referred on is not the fear of the drill, it is what tends to travel with it.

The guideline is explicit about the division of labour. The dentist has three jobs: screening, observing and referring. Diagnosing an anxiety disorder does not happen in the surgery.

## Where it gets recorded, and who sees it

A note that only its author ever reads is worth nothing. The hygienist going in to scale, the nurse setting up the surgery and whoever picks up the phone next week all need to see the same thing.

- **An alert on the record, not free text in a treatment note.** Free text gets read when somebody goes looking; an alert shows up by itself.
- **With the score and the date.** "Anxious" ages badly. "MDAS 21, 22/10/2026" can be compared with next year's.
- **Visible from the appointment book.** The decision that depends on this (how many minutes, what time of day) gets made when the appointment is booked, not when the record is opened.
- **With one line on what worked.** "Wants warning before each step", "cannot tolerate the suction": that is what saves the second bad experience.
- **Kept apart from medical alerts.** An allergy and a fear are not handled the same way, even though both are alerts. The strictly clinical ones belong in [medical alerts on the record](/en/blog/medical-alerts-dental-records/), and the opening question fits on the [medical history questionnaire](/en/blog/dental-medical-history-questionnaire/).

## The appointment they need

This is where the record turns into something the patient actually notices.

- **More minutes than the procedure needs.** Not to do more, but to avoid working in a hurry. Hurry is what an anxious patient reads as danger.
- **A first appointment with no treatment in it.** Talk, look, explain the plan, and stop. The S3 guideline notes that clinically significant anxiety is often associated with an experienced loss of control, frequently in childhood, and that what these patients ask for is pain-free treatment, information, empathy and a sense of control.
- **Early in the day where possible.** A long wait is time to anticipate.
- **No change of chair or of clinician in the early visits.** A familiar face is worth more than an earlier slot.
- **An agreed stop signal.** A raised hand, and a raised hand that is honoured. It is the cheapest way to hand control back.

> **The appointment is the intervention.** Before nitrous oxide and before any drug, what the S3 guideline asks of the dental team is adequate local anaesthesia and supporting the patient's sense of control by involving them in the steps of the treatment.

## What the evidence says works

The S3 guideline grades the treatments, and its conclusions are worth reading before building a protocol of your own:

- **Cognitive behavioural therapy with exposure: first-line treatment** for clinically significant dental anxiety, carrying the highest evidence and recommendation grades in the table.
- **EMDR: second line.**
- **An oral benzodiazepine (midazolam, for instance): first choice in an acute or emergency situation** where the first-line treatment is not available. Nitrous oxide is second in that scenario and general anaesthesia third, in limited exceptional cases only.
- **Relaxation techniques, music and information videos: a strong negative recommendation** in that guideline, both for immediate anxiety reduction and for treating the condition. Hypnosis and acupuncture receive a negative recommendation.

That last point is the one that clashes hardest with common practice. In France, the March 2023 white paper from the Ordre national des chirurgiens-dentistes and the Académie nationale de chirurgie dentaire does list hypnosis and acupuncture among the iatrosedation techniques usable in general practice. Two national authorities reading the same literature differently, and the honest thing is to say so rather than quote whichever suits.

What both share is the step before. The French document requires, ahead of any sedative approach, a physical assessment (medical questionnaire, examination and conversation) and a psychological one (observing the patient, scales and anxiety assessment questionnaires). Measure first, decide second.

And once treated, the follow-up: the S3 guideline recommends enrolling these patients in a regular dental recall, at least six-monthly, as relapse prophylaxis. The [recall system](/en/blog/dental-recall-system/) stops being a commercial tool and becomes part of the treatment.

## What not to do

- **Promising it will not hurt.** If it hurts once, you have spent both the trust and the argument.
- **Starting treatment on day one because they are in the chair anyway.** It is the fastest way to lose the patient who took longest to come.
- **Leaving the information in the head of whoever answered the phone.** That person takes holidays.
- **Commenting on the years they stayed away.** The patient already knows, and it is precisely what they were dreading hearing.
- **Using the score as a label.** It is a number for organising an appointment, not a diagnosis, and not something to discuss in front of the patient as though it were one.
