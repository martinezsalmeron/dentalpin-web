In the United States, who may administer sedation in a dental office is decided by your state dental board, not by a national rule. The American Dental Association's own guidelines say so plainly: "Dentists must comply with their state laws, rules and/or regulations when providing sedation and anesthesia." What the ADA does set is the clinical floor, and for minimal sedation that floor includes a second person trained in Basic Life Support, a positive-pressure oxygen system, a written sedative record and a documented discharge.

> **This is not legal or clinical advice, and it covers the United States only.** Everything below comes from the ADA's *Guidelines for the Use of Sedation and General Anesthesia by Dentists* in the version adopted by the House of Delegates in October 2025, with the link and the date consulted at the end. The United Kingdom has a separate framework under the General Dental Council and the Intercollegiate Advisory Committee for Sedation in Dentistry, and this post does not state what those documents require.

## Why the state board is the first call, not the last

The ADA guidelines are a professional standard, not a licence. They open by deferring twice: dentists must comply with state law, and office-based sedation "may be administered by a qualified dentist or another independently qualified anesthesia healthcare provider, in accordance with state laws, rules and/or regulations for the benefit of patient safety."

That matters because sedation permits in the US are issued per state and the categories do not line up across borders. A permit that covers you in one state is not portable, and the training a board accepts is set by that board.

One line in the guidelines removes a common misreading: "Level of sedation is entirely independent of the route of administration." Giving a drug by mouth does not make the sedation minimal.

## Where minimal sedation ends

This is the part worth reading twice, because it is where a practice drifts into a deeper category without meaning to.

**Minimal sedation** is defined as "a minimally depressed level of consciousness, produced by a pharmacological method, that retains the patient's normal response to verbal command." The patient keeps the ability to maintain an airway independently and continuously, and ventilatory and cardiovascular functions are unaffected.

Two rules then draw the boundary:

- **The maximum recommended dose.** Minimal sedation by the enteral route may use a drug "either singly or in divided doses" to reach the desired effect, "not to exceed the maximum recommended dose (MRD)", which is the maximum FDA-recommended dose in the labeling for unmonitored home use.
- **Exceeding it reclassifies the appointment.** Administering an enteral drug above that dose "during a single appointment is considered to be moderate sedation and the moderate sedation guidelines apply."

> **Nitrous oxide is not automatically minimal sedation.** The guidelines state that "Nitrous oxide/oxygen when used in combination with sedative agent(s) may produce minimal, moderate, deep sedation or general anesthesia." Nitrous oxide on its own and nitrous oxide on top of an oral sedative are not the same procedure, and the second one can land in a category your permit may not cover.

## What has to be in the room

For minimal sedation the guidelines separate personnel from equipment, and both are written as requirements rather than suggestions. The distinction between "must" and "should" in the table below is theirs, not ours.

| Requirement for minimal sedation | ADA guidelines |
|---|---|
| A second person trained in Basic Life Support, in addition to the dentist | ✓ Must be present |
| Positive-pressure oxygen delivery system suitable for the patient | ✓ Must be immediately available |
| Bag-valve-mask with multiple mask sizes | ✓ Must be available |
| Portable oxygen tank with regulator and key | ✓ Must be available |
| Oral pharyngeal airways | ✓ Must be available |
| Fail-safe system on inhalation equipment, checked and calibrated | ✓ Must, where inhalation equipment is used |
| A device prohibiting delivery of less than 30% oxygen, or a calibrated in-line oxygen analyzer with audible alarm | ✓ One of the two must be present |
| Scavenging system | ✓ Must be available if gases other than oxygen or air are used |
| Pre-procedural check of equipment, every time sedation is given | ✓ Must be performed |
| Documentation of manufacturers' recommended maintenance | ~ Should be maintained |

Before the appointment, the guidelines also require informed consent for the proposed sedation, a determination that the oxygen supply and positive-pressure equipment are adequate, preoperative verbal and written instructions, and baseline vital signs. The baseline list is specific: "body weight, height, BMI, blood pressure, pulse rate, and respiration rate", unless the nature of the patient, procedure or equipment makes them invalid.

## What gets monitored and what gets written down

Monitoring runs continuously, and the guidelines name who may do it: the qualified dentist, or at the dentist's direction "an appropriately trained individual", who must stay in the operatory during active treatment until the patient meets the discharge criteria.

Four things are monitored:

1. **Consciousness.** The level of sedation, for example responsiveness to verbal commands, "must be continually assessed".
2. **Oxygenation.** Oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry.
3. **Ventilation.** Someone must observe chest excursions and verify respirations.
4. **Circulation.** Blood pressure and heart rate "should be evaluated pre-operatively, post-operatively and intraoperatively as necessary".

The record itself has a defined content, and this is the sentence to measure your current chart against: "An appropriate sedative record must be maintained, including the names of all drugs administered, time administered and route of administration, including local anesthetics, dosages, and monitored physiological parameters."

> **Discharge is a documented decision, not a feeling.** The qualified dentist "must determine and document that level of consciousness, oxygenation, ventilation and circulation are satisfactory prior to discharge", and post-operative verbal and written instructions must be given to the patient, parent, escort, legal guardian or caregiver. If your chart has no field for that determination, it is being made and then lost.

## The emergency side, including the six-month drill

Three obligations here are easy to overlook because none of them involve buying anything.

- **Stopping.** "If a patient enters a deeper level of sedation than the dentist is qualified to provide, the dentist must stop the dental procedure until the patient returns to the intended level of sedation."
- **Written protocols.** "All providers and offices must have written emergency protocols."
- **Rehearsed drills.** Documented training sessions "including rehearsed emergency drills, must be developed and rehearsed, at minimum, every six months", and it is the qualified dentist's responsibility to ensure the training happens.

A drill every six months is a date that needs an owner and a record. It is the single requirement on this page most likely to be true in principle and missing in evidence.

## Five checks before you offer it

1. **Read your state board's sedation rules first** and match your permit to the deepest level you could plausibly reach, not the level you intend.
2. **Walk the equipment list physically**, with someone holding the list, rather than assuming the cabinet matches it. Check the oxygen analyzer or the 30% device specifically.
3. **Add the missing fields to your sedative record** so drug names, times, routes, dosages and monitored parameters all have a place.
4. **Create the discharge determination as a field**, not a habit, so consciousness, oxygenation, ventilation and circulation are recorded before the patient leaves.
5. **Put the six-month drill in the calendar** with a document to sign, because an undocumented drill is indistinguishable from no drill.
