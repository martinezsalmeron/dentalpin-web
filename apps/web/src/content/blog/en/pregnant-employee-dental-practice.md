In a UK dental practice, nothing in the maternity health and safety regime starts until the employee tells you **in writing**. Once she has, three things follow: your existing risk assessment must cover the risk to her and her baby, you must alter her working conditions or hours if that avoids the risk, and if it does not, you must suspend her from work on full pay. The written notification is the hinge, and it is the part practices most often get wrong in both directions.

> **Regulation 18(1) is explicit about the trigger.** Nothing in regulation 16(2) or (3) "shall require the employer to take any action in relation to an employee until she has notified the employer in writing that she is pregnant, has given birth within the previous six months, or is breastfeeding." A corridor conversation is not notification, and treating it as one leaves you with duties you cannot evidence.

## The assessment you already have has to cover one more thing

Regulation 16(1) of the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999 does not create a separate document. It extends the assessment you already do under regulation 3(1), and it does so for the workforce rather than for an individual: where the people working in an undertaking include **women of child-bearing age**, and the work could involve risk to a new or expectant mother or her baby, the regulation 3 assessment must also include an assessment of that risk.

That wording matters for a practice of six people. The duty does not wait for a pregnancy. It attaches as soon as you employ women of child-bearing age, which in dentistry is almost every practice in the country.

Regulation 16 then sets the ladder in paragraphs (2) and (3). If other action you are already required to take would not avoid the risk, you **"shall, if it is reasonable to do so, and would avoid such risks, alter her working conditions or hours of work"**. If that is not reasonable or would not avoid the risk, you **"shall, subject to section 67 of the 1996 Act, suspend the employee from work for so long as is necessary to avoid such risk."**

## The infection clause that saves most dental assessments

Regulation 16(4) contains a qualification that is widely missed and is directly useful in dentistry:

> **"In paragraphs (1) to (3) references to risk, in relation to risk from any infectious or contagious disease, are references to a level of risk at work which is in addition to the level to which a new or expectant mother may be expected to be exposed outside the workplace."**

The test is therefore not whether a pathogen exists in the practice. It is whether the work exposes her to more of it than ordinary life would. That is a defensible, proportionate standard, and it is the sentence that stops an infection-risk assessment from collapsing into "stop seeing patients".

## Radiation is governed separately, and it has a number

The Ionising Radiations Regulations 2017 run alongside, not instead of. Regulation 9(6)(a) requires an employer undertaking work with ionising radiation to ensure that, in relation to a pregnant employee, the conditions of exposure are such that, **after the employer has been notified of the pregnancy**, the equivalent dose to the foetus "is as low as is reasonably practicable and is unlikely to exceed 1 mSv during the remainder of the pregnancy".

Regulation 9(6)(b) adds that an employee who is breastfeeding must not be engaged in work involving a significant risk of intake of radionuclides or of bodily contamination, which in general dental practice is rarely the live question.

And regulation 9(7) repeats the written-notification trigger: no action is required until the employee has notified her employer in writing of the pregnancy or that she is breastfeeding, and the employer undertaking the work has been made aware, or should reasonably have been expected to be aware, of that notification.

## Suspension, and why it is not a last resort you can skip to

| | Alter conditions or hours | Offer suitable alternative work | Suspend on maternity grounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source | Reg. 16(2) MHSWR 1999 | s. 67 ERA 1996 | Reg. 16(3) MHSWR 1999, s. 68 ERA 1996 |
| When it applies | ✓ First, if reasonable and it avoids the risk | ✓ Whenever suitable work is available | ✗ Only when the first two do not avoid the risk |
| Pay | ✓ Unchanged | ~ Terms must not be substantially less favourable | ✓ Remuneration continues |
| Needs a doctor's certificate | ✓ No | ✓ No | ~ Only for the night-work route under reg. 17 |

Section 67(1) gives the employee a **right to be offered** suitable alternative work where the employer has it available, before being suspended. Section 67(2) defines suitable in two parts: the work must be "both suitable in relation to her and appropriate for her to do in the circumstances", and its terms and conditions must not be "substantially less favourable" than those of her normal work.

Section 68(1) then provides that an employee suspended on maternity grounds is entitled to be paid remuneration while suspended. The one exception is in section 68(2): no remuneration for a period where the employer offered suitable alternative work for the purposes of section 67 and the employee **unreasonably refused** to perform it.

Regulation 17 is the separate night-work route. Where a new or expectant mother works at night and a certificate from a registered medical practitioner or registered midwife shows it is necessary for her health or safety that she should not be at work for that period, the employer must suspend her for as long as is necessary.

## What you can ask for, and what happens if she does not provide it

Regulation 18(2) lets you stop maintaining the action in a narrow set of cases. The one that matters day to day: where she has notified you that she is pregnant and has failed, **within a reasonable time of being requested to do so in writing by her employer**, to produce for inspection a certificate from a registered medical practitioner or registered midwife showing that she is pregnant.

Note the shape of it. The request has to be in writing, the time allowed has to be reasonable, and the consequence is that you need not maintain the action, not that the notification is undone.

## The first week, in order

1. **Record the date** the written notification arrived, and keep the notification itself.
2. **Review the regulation 16 assessment** for her actual duties, not for the practice in general.
3. **Apply the regulation 16(4) test** to infection risk: what is the exposure here over and above ordinary life?
4. **Check the radiation side** against IRR 2017 regulation 9(6)(a) with your radiation protection adviser.
5. **Request the certificate in writing** if you want one, and allow a reasonable period.
6. **Alter conditions or hours first**, and record what you changed and why.
7. **Offer suitable alternative work** before considering suspension, and put the offer in writing.
