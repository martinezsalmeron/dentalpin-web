The piece of paper that comes back with the crown is the statement required by Annex XIII of Regulation (EU) 2017/745: the manufacturer draws it up, which in the usual prosthetic chain is the laboratory that made the work, it has to accompany the device, and it has to be made available to that one named patient. It is not a CE mark, because custom-made devices carry no CE marking. And if you mill in your own practice, you are the manufacturer of that work and you write the statement yourself.

> **This post is about the EU rulebook.** Regulation (EU) 2017/745 applies in the EU and the EEA. If you practise in Great Britain or the United States, the paperwork around a custom-made device is governed by your own national regime, which this post does not cover, so do not read the article numbers below as yours.

Three documents circulate around one prosthesis, and they are routinely confused with each other.

- **The lab prescription** is what leaves the practice: the design, the material, the instruction. That is [sending cases to the lab](/en/blog/dental-lab-case-workflow/).
- **The lot number** belongs to what was implanted, so a recall can find the patient. That is [implant lot tracking](/en/blog/implant-lot-tracking/).
- **The Annex XIII statement** is what comes back with the finished work, and it is the one most practices assume is the laboratory's problem alone.

## What it has to say, item by item

Annex XIII, Section 1 sets out what the statement must contain. It is not free text and it is not a stamp: it is eight items, and the list is closed.

- **The name and address of the manufacturer** and of all manufacturing sites. Plural, because if part of the work happens elsewhere, that site appears too.
- **The name and address of the authorised representative**, if applicable.
- **Data allowing identification of the device** in question.
- **A statement that the device is intended for exclusive use by a particular patient or user**, identified by name, an acronym or a numerical code.
- **The name of the person who made out the prescription**, authorised by national law by virtue of their professional qualifications, and where applicable the name of the health institution.
- **The specific characteristics of the product** as indicated by the prescription.
- **A statement that the device conforms to the general safety and performance requirements in Annex I**, and where applicable which ones are not fully met, with the grounds.
- **Where applicable, an indication that it contains a medicinal substance**, a human blood or plasma derivative, or tissues or cells of human or animal origin.

That fourth item is what makes the document what it is. The statement is not valid for a type of crown. It is valid for one crown, for one patient, on one date.

> **The regulation does require it to reach the patient.** Article 21(2) says custom-made devices "shall be accompanied by the statement referred to in Section 1 of Annex XIII, which shall be made available to the particular patient or user identified by name, an acronym or a numerical code". Accompanying the device and being made available to the patient are two things, and both sit in the same sentence.

## Do not call it a CE mark

This is the mistake that makes everything else on the page look unreliable. Article 21(1) closes by saying that the devices it covers "shall not bear the CE marking", and Article 20(1) reserves the marking for devices other than custom-made and investigational ones.

The regulation is consistent about it. Article 10(6) asks manufacturers of devices other than custom-made ones for an EU declaration of conformity and the CE marking, while Article 10(5) sends manufacturers of custom-made devices to Section 2 of Annex XIII instead. They get no UDI and no Eudamed device registration either, because Articles 27 and 29 exclude custom-made devices by name.

What the device does carry, under Annex I, Chapter III, Section 23.2, is the words "custom-made device" on its label.

## Who keeps what, and for how long

This is where periods that answer different questions get mixed together. Annex XIII, Section 4 is clear about the manufacturer's: the statement "shall be kept for a period of at least 10 years after the device has been placed on the market", and at least 15 years for implantable devices.

That clock belongs to the manufacturer, not to you. Your copy lives in the patient's clinical record, and its retention is the clinical record rule, which is a different rule and a different count. If that is the part you need, it is in [how long to keep dental records](/en/blog/how-long-to-keep-dental-records/).

## Mill in-house and the regulation is looking at you

A practice with a chairside mill or its own lab bench is manufacturing custom-made devices, and the duties follow the activity rather than the business you thought you were in. At EU level that means Article 52(8), which tells manufacturers of custom-made devices to follow Annex XIII and draw up the Section 1 statement before placing the device on the market, and Article 10(5), which requires documentation under Section 2 of that Annex to be drawn up, kept up to date and kept available for competent authorities.

Two things sit on top of that and vary by country. Article 21(2) lets member states require the manufacturer of a custom-made device to submit a list of such devices made available on their territory, and national law decides whether manufacturing custom-made devices needs a licence or a register entry before you start. Check both with your own competent authority before the mill arrives, not after.

> **One more thing Article 15(1) quietly allows.** Manufacturers of custom-made devices may demonstrate the expertise required of the person responsible for regulatory compliance "by having at least two years of professional experience within a relevant field of manufacturing", without prejudice to national rules on professional qualifications. It is the one place in the regulation where the bar is set for a small manufacturer rather than a large one.

## What to ask the lab for, in order

1. **One statement per piece of work, not one per supplier.** An annual certificate on the laboratory's letterhead is not what Annex XIII describes: it has no patient and no device in it.
2. **Have it arrive with the work.** Article 21(2) says "accompanied". Chasing it three weeks later works badly, and it shows the moment somebody asks about one specific item.
3. **Check the prescriber's name is on it.** That is you, or whoever in the practice signed the instruction, and it is one of the eight items.
4. **Check every manufacturing site is listed.** If the lab subcontracts milling or sintering, Annex XIII wants those addresses too.
5. **File it with the patient, not in a folder per laboratory.** When it is needed, the search is by patient or by item, never by supplier.
