Measure your real rate before changing anything, send a reminder the patient can confirm with a one-word reply, put the concrete consequence of an empty chair in that message, and know who to call the same day. Published trials put text message reminders at roughly ten percentage points of extra attendance, and past that the result is decided by the small group of patients who miss appointments repeatedly.

What follows is how to work out that rate, what the trials say about each channel, how the message should be written, and what to do on the day the gap is already in front of you.

## Measure first: what counts as a no-show, and what your rate is

A practice that says "a lot of people miss" is not measuring, it is remembering the bad days. The number that helps is a division: appointments the patient did not attend over appointments scheduled, across a period longer than one stray week.

Start by separating four situations that get lumped together and do not have the same fix.

| Situation | How to count it | What it leaves you |
|---|---|---|
| Does not arrive, no warning | ✗ No-show | Nothing, the whole slot is lost |
| Cancels with under 24 hours | ~ Late cancellation | Little, unless you have someone to call |
| Cancels two days or more ahead | ✓ Notified cancellation | Time to refill the slot normally |
| Rebooks on the same call | ✓ Rescheduled | Not a loss, just a move |

Count the first two rows separately and you will see within a minute which of the two problems you have. Lots of late cancellations means you need a call list; lots of silent no-shows means you need to change the reminder.

> **Published rates are not your rate.** A Finnish register of 2,513,376 public oral health appointments in Helsinki measured 7.4% no-shows, and a university dental service in Riyadh found 9.14% across 15,193 appointments. Those are different populations under different payment systems, so they tell you the problem is real and universal, not what your target should be.

Convert the count into chair time rather than into revenue at list price. Hours lost per month is the figure the whole team understands, and the only one that compares fairly between operatories.

## What reminders actually achieve, according to the trials

The Cochrane review of mobile phone messaging reminders for healthcare appointments pools seven studies and 5,841 participants. Attendance went from 67.8% with no reminder to 78.6% with a text message, a risk ratio of 1.14 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.26), on evidence the authors graded as moderate quality.

The second finding is the one that settles a spending decision. Against a phone call, text messaging came out at a risk ratio of 0.99 (95% CI 0.95 to 1.02) across three studies and 2,509 participants. No detectable difference between ringing people and texting them.

| Reminder | What the Cochrane review measured | What it costs to keep running |
|---|---|---|
| None | ✗ 67.8% attendance | Nothing |
| Text message | ✓ 78.6% attendance, RR 1.14 (1.03-1.26) | ✓ Low, and automatable |
| Phone call | ✓ 80.3% attendance, no difference against texting | ✗ Front desk time on every appointment |

That review covers healthcare appointments in general rather than dentistry alone, and that is its honest limit. What does carry across is the comparison between channels, which is where a small practice saves half a morning of phone work a week without losing attendance.

## How the message is written matters as much as when it is sent

Two randomised controlled trials published in PLoS ONE in 2015 tested exactly this on NHS hospital appointments. Changing only the wording of the SMS, the message stating the specific cost of a missed appointment cut the did-not-attend rate from 11.1% to 8.4% (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.89) across 10,111 patients.

The second trial, with 9,848 patients, pinned down why: stating the cost in specific terms gave 8.2%, while talking about cost in general terms only reached 9.9%. Vagueness does not work.

- **Make the consequence concrete.** "If you cannot come, tell us and we will give your slot to another patient" says something checkable. "Your appointment is important" says nothing.
- **Ask for one small action**, a one-word reply to confirm. Confirmation is what turns a notice into information you can use today.
- **Send two, not five.** One at booking, with the date, and one between 24 and 48 hours before. Beyond that people stop reading them.
- **Make it repliable.** A number that does not accept replies turns a notified cancellation into a no-show, because the patient tried and could not.
- **Name, day, time and place on the first line.** The message is read on a lock screen, and few words fit there.

> **Do not run service notices down the same pipe as marketing.** A reminder for an appointment the patient already booked is not a promotion, and mixing the two in one channel is the fastest way to get both blocked. The consent rules differ by country, so confirm yours before you set the sending up.

## No-shows concentrate in a few patients

The same Finnish register carries the finding that changes how you work: 5% of patients accounted for 21.8% of all no-shows. Missed appointments are not spread evenly across your list, they are concentrated.

That study followed children and adolescents in a public service, so the magnitude does not transfer untouched to a private adult practice. The shape of the problem does, and it takes minutes to check in your own software: sort patients by missed appointments over the last year and see how few of them do half the damage.

Once that group is identified, the measures stop being generic and start working:

- **Book them at shorter notice.** An appointment three weeks out gets forgotten; five days out, much less.
- **Require an active confirmation** and do not treat the slot as safe until you have it.
- **Offer them the first slot of the morning** or the last of the day, the ones that hurt least when they fall through.
- **Ask the reason once.** Transport, work hours and childcare explain a large share, and some of it is solved by moving the day.
- **Record it on the patient's file**, not in the memory of whoever is on reception that week.

## Filling the gap the same day

This is where a practice recovers real money, because the no-show has already happened and the only variable left is how long the hole stays open.

1. **Keep a live list of who wants an earlier appointment.** Not a notebook: a field on the record you can filter in ten seconds.
2. **Ask at the time of booking.** "Shall I call you if something opens up sooner?" is a three-second question that fills slots for months.
3. **Prioritise by treatment, not by queue order.** A 40-minute gap goes to someone with a 40-minute treatment pending, not to whoever is first on the list.
4. **Message several people at once and give it to the first to reply**, saying so in the message so you never promise the same slot twice.
5. **Have an internal plan B for the slot**: the team's own overdue check-ups, follow-up calls on unaccepted quotes, radiographs waiting to be reviewed. A gap spent on something useful hurts less.
6. **Record whether it was filled and with what.** Without that, you will never know if the call list is working.

## Deposits and cancellation policies: what is known and what is not

There is no published figure here that I can point at, so what follows is operational judgement rather than evidence, and it should be treated that way.

A deposit reduces no-shows because it moves part of the cost onto the patient, and that is precisely its problem: it also turns away patients who would have attended, and it adds taking and refunding money to the front desk's workload. On long, expensive treatments it usually pays off. On a twenty-minute check-up it rarely does.

If you are going to set a policy, meet these three conditions or skip it:

- **Written down and stated at booking**, not produced after the event.
- **Actually enforceable.** A rule reception does not dare apply is worse than no rule, because it teaches everyone that the practice's rules are decorative.
- **With a planned exception.** Illness, emergency and a first miss from someone who never misses get waived, and that is decided in advance too, not at the desk.

## Where to start

1. **Pull the rate for the last twelve months**, keeping no-shows and late cancellations apart.
2. **Turn it into chair hours per month.** That number is what justifies everything else.
3. **Rewrite your reminder** to include the concrete consequence and the request to confirm.
4. **Settle on two sends**, one at booking and one 24 to 48 hours before.
5. **List the patients with three or more misses in the last year** and give them short notice plus mandatory confirmation.
6. **Build the call list** and start asking about it at every booking.
7. **Measure again after three months.** Any sooner and the noise is bigger than the effect.
