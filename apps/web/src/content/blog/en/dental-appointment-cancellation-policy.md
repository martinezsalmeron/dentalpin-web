In the UK the answer splits cleanly in two. For NHS dental appointments a practice should not charge a fee for a missed appointment at all, though repeated non-attendance has other consequences. For private treatment there is no cap on what you charge, but the cancellation term is a consumer contract term, which means it has to be transparent and fair or it simply does not bind the patient.

Getting that split wrong is the most common problem here, because a single policy document applied to both sides of the practice will be wrong on one of them.

> **This page has two halves and only one is legal.** The first is what you may charge in the UK, with the source and the date consulted. The second is operational and is just as useful to a practice that charges nothing: where the policy text lives, how notice is given and recorded, and what the schedule does when a slot is released.

## In thirty seconds

**NHS side: no fee.** The NHS states that practices "should not charge you a fee for missing NHS dental appointments". What repeated non-attendance can do is end the course of treatment early.

**Private side: a fee is possible, a flat penalty is risky.** Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 a term is unfair if, contrary to good faith, it causes a significant imbalance to the patient's detriment, and an unfair term is not binding.

**Either way you need a written policy.** For NHS work it is a contractual requirement, not a nice-to-have.

## The NHS half

For NHS treatment the position is published by the NHS itself. On what should not happen at an NHS dental appointment, practices "should not charge you a fee for missing NHS dental appointments". Patients are told that if they cannot attend they should "let the dental surgery know as soon as possible so your appointment can be given to someone else".

That does not make non-attendance free of consequence. The same page warns that if a patient misses an appointment for ongoing treatment without telling the surgery, "you may need to pay again for a new course of treatment".

The mechanism behind that is Failed To Return. The NHS Business Services Authority explains that a course of treatment can be claimed as FTR when "a patient repeatedly fails to attend for planned care or cancels at short notice", and that "a practice must follow its contract with the Commissioner where it specifies how many missed appointments are allowed before a Course of Treatment (CoT) can be recorded as 'Incomplete Treatment'".

> **The written policy is a requirement on the NHS side, not an option.** The NHSBSA states that "the practice must have an agreed 'Fail to Attend' and 'Short Notice Cancellation' policy" so that missed or last-minute cancelled appointments are handled "in a consistent, fair, and transparent way", and that "all members of the practice must be familiar with the policy and always follow it".

## The private half

For private treatment the General Dental Council does not set prices: "there are no set limits on what practices can charge for private dental treatment and prices will vary from practice to practice". What the GDC does require is visible pricing, a "simple price list ... clearly displayed in the reception or waiting area of the practice", covering a consultation, a single-surface filling, an extraction, radiographs and treatment provided by the hygienist.

The GDC publishes nothing on missed-appointment charges, so the constraint comes from consumer law instead. Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015:

- **Fairness, section 62.** "A term is unfair if, contrary to the requirement of good faith, it causes a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations under the contract to the detriment of the consumer." The same section provides that "an unfair term of a consumer contract is not binding on the consumer".
- **Transparency, section 68.** A trader "must ensure that a written term of a consumer contract, or a consumer notice in writing, is transparent", meaning expressed in plain and intelligible language and, for a written term, legible.

Read together, those two sections are what a cancellation charge has to survive. A charge the patient was told about before booking, proportionate to the time actually lost, and applied only where the slot could not be refilled is a very different thing from a flat percentage buried in a form.

This is not legal advice. Have the wording checked before you publish it, and check your own NHS contract for the numbers it specifies.

## Where the policy text has to live

Where it is written decides whether the patient was genuinely told, and that is the requirement most policies fail.

- **In the new patient form**, signed, alongside the medical history. It is the only place that evidences the patient actually saw it.
- **In the appointment confirmation**, one line, with the actual notice period. Someone who registered four years ago does not remember the form.
- **On the practice website**, reachable before booking rather than buried in terms and conditions.
- **Said out loud for long appointments.** An hour of surgical time is booked in conversation, not by text message.

A notice taped to the reception desk is not enough. It does not evidence that this patient knew before this appointment.

## What to record when the appointment is booked

Four fields, and they fit on the appointment itself.

| Field | Why you keep it | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Notice period the patient accepted | ✓ Evidences prior notice | New patient form and appointment |
| Time reserved and chair | ✓ Shows what was actually held | Schedule |
| Channel and date of the reminder | ✓ Evidences the reminder went out | Communications history |
| Patients offered the released slot | ✓ Evidences the attempt to refill | Note on the freed appointment |
| NHS or private | ✓ Decides which rules apply | Appointment |

The last two are the ones practices skip. Without the fourth you cannot say the slot was lost, and without the fifth a single policy gets applied to an NHS patient who should never have been charged.

## What the schedule does when a slot is released

1. **Mark the slot released, with the time the patient called.** That is what later separates a cancellation two days out from a call twenty minutes before.
2. **Record the reason in the patient's own words.** Illness or a family emergency changes the decision, and on the NHS side it also changes how the non-attendance is counted.
3. **Offer the slot in order**, not to whoever answers first: pain first, then whoever has waited longest, then whoever lives close enough to come now.
4. **Log who was offered it and who declined.** This is the evidence that the time could not be recovered.
5. **Decide about charging at the end of the day**, with the outcome and the reason in front of you, and invoice it separately from clinical treatment.

Step 4 is the one almost nobody does and the one that matters most. It is the difference between a charge you can explain and a charge you cannot.

## Five mistakes that keep recurring

- **One policy for NHS and private.** They have different answers and a shared document will be wrong on one side.
- **A flat percentage of the treatment.** Easy to calculate, and the first thing anyone will test against section 62.
- **Charging without trying to refill.** No record of the attempt means no loss you can point at.
- **Applying it to a free consultation.** There was no consideration and the reputational cost is high.
- **Putting the charge on the next treatment invoice.** It is not a clinical act and belongs on its own document with its own description.

## If you charge nothing at all

That is a defensible choice and plenty of practices make it. The policy does not disappear, it just says something different: a clear notice period, a reminder that lands in time, and a procedure for the freed slot. On NHS work you still need the written Fail to Attend and Short Notice Cancellation policy regardless.

## What the software has to do

Nearly all of the above is record-keeping, which makes it more dependent on the system than it looks: the accepted notice period belongs on the record, the reminder has to leave a trace with date and channel, and the freed slot has to hold a note of who was offered it.
