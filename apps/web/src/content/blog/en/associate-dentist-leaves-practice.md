Three answers, in the order they matter the morning after. Patients can follow the associate, because they choose their dentist and nobody owns a patient list. The clinical records stay with the practice, which still holds them and still has to give the patient a copy on request. And the non-compete is worth much less than most practice owners assume: in the UK a clause in restraint of trade starts out unenforceable, and the employer has to justify it.

That last point surprises people who have signed one. A non-compete is not a rule you can simply invoke. It is a restriction you have to defend as reasonable, clause by clause, if it is ever tested.

## In the UK, the starting point is that the clause does not bind

There is no statute setting the length of a non-compete in the UK. The Department for Business and Trade's own working paper on reforming these clauses puts the current position plainly: "The starting point under current law is that a non-compete clause, as with any clause in restraint of trade, is unenforceable." The employer can then show it is reasonable, which is where every argument actually happens.

It is worth knowing how close this came to changing, because practice owners and associates both quote the three-month figure as if it were law. The same paper records that the previous government announced on 10 May 2023 that it "would introduce a statutory limit on the length of non-compete clauses of 3 months", and then states: "However, no action was taken."

> **The three-month cap is not law and never was.** It was an announcement in 2023 that was never legislated, and the 2025 working paper lists a statutory limit as one option among several. A contract written around it is written around something that does not exist.

So the clause is judged on what it actually restricts. A restriction tied to a named practice, a modest radius and a few months reads very differently from one that bars a dentist from practising across a county for two years.

## In the US, the nationwide ban is not in force

If the practice is in the United States, the position is a state-law question, and the federal rule people remember did not survive. The FTC's own page on the Non-Compete Clause Rule says: "The Noncompete Rule is not in effect and it is not enforceable."

The sequence matters because it is still widely misreported. A district court order stopped the FTC from enforcing the rule on 20 August 2024, the FTC appealed on 18 October 2024, and on 5 September 2025 "the FTC took steps to dismiss its appeal in the Fifth Circuit". A Federal Register notice of 12 February 2026 removes the rule to conform it to the court decisions.

## Patients can follow; the records do not

Read what a non-compete restricts and the patient question answers itself. It restricts the departing dentist's activity. It creates no right over patients, who are not party to the contract and who pick whoever they want to see.

The records are a separate matter, and the law here is specific. Under Article 15(1) of the UK GDPR the patient has "the right to obtain from the controller confirmation as to whether or not personal data" concerning them is being processed, and Article 15(3) requires that the controller "shall provide a copy of the personal data undergoing processing". The controller is the practice, and it stays the controller after the associate has gone.

That is the clean route for a patient who wants to continue treatment elsewhere. They do not need anyone's permission and they do not need the departing dentist to take anything: they ask the practice for a copy.

> **Taking a copy of the patient list is not a bargaining position.** It is new processing by someone who is no longer part of the controller, and it is argued on far worse ground than the non-compete. What the patient takes with them, the patient asks for.

**One thing this post deliberately does not state:** the US federal position on a patient's right of access. We could not reach the official text of 45 CFR 164.524 or the HHS guidance on it while writing this, and quoting a provision second hand is how wrong numbers spread. US readers should take that question to the Office for Civil Rights at HHS, which is the body that answers it.

## The booked appointments: who calls, and what they say

No statute covers this part, and it is the part that decides how the practice comes out of it. There are patients booked three weeks out with someone who will not be there.

1. **Fix one date on which the diary changes hands**, and use it for everything: last clinical day, reassignment, and access being switched off.
2. **Go through the appointments one at a time**, separating check-ups from treatment in progress. A half-finished root canal is not reassigned the way a hygiene visit is.
3. **Reassign before you call.** Ringing a patient to say their dentist is leaving without being able to say who is seeing them is how you lose the appointment during that call.
4. **Agree one sentence, word for word, for "where have they gone?"** Neutral, short, and the same from everyone on reception. What damages a handover is not the question, it is five different improvised answers.
5. **Do not make a records request difficult.** It is the patient's right, and handling it well is what brings that patient back later, which happens more often than owners expect.
6. **Note the change of treating clinician in each record**, with the date. In two years that note is the only way to tell who did what.
7. **Switch off access on the day**, not the following week. Email, shared diary and the practice software.
