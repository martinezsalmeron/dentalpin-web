Patient debt is controlled by three things, and none of them is software: the outstanding amount is recorded the day the treatment is invoiced rather than whenever someone remembers, every instalment arrangement is signed before treatment starts with its dates and amounts on it, and one named person works an aged debt list on a fixed day each month.

Without that fixed day in the calendar there is no debt control. There is a figure that turns up at the year end, by which point very little of it can still be recovered.

## An instalment that is not due yet is not debt

Most practices that describe a bad debt problem actually have a classification problem. Overdue invoices, instalments that are not yet due and finished work that has never been billed all sit in the same pile.

They are three different things, and only the first is debt:

- **Due and unpaid.** It is invoiced, the due date has passed and the money has not arrived. This is the only thing that should count as debt.
- **On a plan and in date.** There is a signed arrangement and the next instalment falls in the future. That is expected income, not debt, and putting it in the same number inflates the alarm until nobody believes it.
- **Done and unbilled.** The work is delivered and the invoice never went out. That is not the patient's debt yet, it is a practice task, and it is usually the easiest part to fix.

> **Mixing the three is what turns debt into a number nobody looks at.** If the figure climbs because three orthodontic cases were signed onto plans this month, the figure is not measuring anything.

The separation has to live in the system rather than in the head of whoever runs the front desk. Four invoice states are enough: issued, paid, part paid and overdue.

## The payment plan is written before treatment starts

An instalment arrangement agreed verbally in the chair is the single biggest source of uncollectable debt, because four months later the practice and the patient do not remember the same thing.

The document does not need to be complicated. It needs six facts:

1. **The total cost of the treatment** and what it covers, with the accepted quote as the reference.
2. **The deposit**, how much and when. It is the most commonly skipped field and the one that shows real commitment.
3. **The number of instalments, the amount of each and its exact date.** Not "over six months", but the six specific dates.
4. **How each instalment is collected**: direct debit, a card held on file, bank transfer or payment at the desk.
5. **What happens if one payment fails**, including who contacts whom and how quickly.
6. **What happens if treatment stops part way**, which is the clause nobody thinks about until the worst possible conversation.

A worked example: a 30% deposit on the day treatment starts, then six equal monthly payments on the fifth of each month. Written down it takes three lines and saves six phone calls.

> **A late fee that was not written into the plan the patient signed is an argument the practice loses.** If you are going to charge anything for a missed payment, it belongs on the paper from the start and said out loud.

## The aged debt list is the tool, not the invoice list

A list of unpaid invoices sorted by number tells you nothing. Sorted by age it becomes a task list, because each band calls for a different action and the right action changes as time passes.

| Age | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 30 days | ✓ Normal | Nothing. This is the ordinary payment window |
| 31 to 60 days | ~ It slipped their mind | Written reminder with the amount and what it was for |
| 61 to 90 days | ~ There is a reason nobody has said | Phone call, not a message. Something usually surfaces |
| Over 90 days | ✗ A decision, not an oversight | A new written arrangement, or close the case |

The reason to call after sixty days rather than send a fourth reminder is that the cause is rarely the obvious one. Some of that balance is people who abandoned treatment half way, some is invoices that went to the wrong address, and some is people having a hard year who would welcome a new plan instead of another automated nudge.

## How to chase, in this order

1. **Written reminder seven days after the due date**, with the amount, what it covers and how to pay. Neutral, no reproach, invoice attached.
2. **Second notice at thirty days**, referring to the date of the first. It should be visible that a record exists.
3. **Phone call at sixty days**, made by someone with the authority to offer a new arrangement. A call that can only repeat the figure is wasted.
4. **A written proposal** if the call surfaces a real reason. A smaller plan paid in full beats a larger balance never paid at all.
5. **Formal letter** if nothing above got a response, sent only once you have decided what follows it.
6. **An explicit decision**: pursue it or write it off. What must not happen is that it sits on the list for another three years while nobody decides.

Every one of those steps needs to be logged against the patient record with a date. Without that log, step five is impossible to justify and step three gets repeated three times without anyone noticing.

## How long a bill stays claimable

There is a time limit, and it is worth knowing before letting a balance go quiet. In England and Wales, section 5 of the Limitation Act 1980 provides that "an action founded on simple contract shall not be brought after the expiration of six years from the date on which the cause of action accrued".

Six years is the outer edge, not a plan. Scotland runs a different regime under its own prescription legislation, and a practice in Ireland, Australia or Canada is under different rules again, so check the limit that applies where you are.

> **This is not legal advice.** The useful part of knowing the limit is not counting the years left. It is that a balance which is still unresolved after a year has already failed every step above, and the answer is a decision rather than more time.

## The numbers to watch each month

![Practice reporting screen with the figures for the period](/screenshots/reports.png)

*Period reporting on one screen. Four numbers are enough to know whether debt is under control.*

| Number | How it is calculated | What decision it triggers |
|---|---|---|
| Overdue balance | Invoiced and unpaid, excluding future instalments | Money the practice already spent on materials and chair time |
| Share over 90 days | Debt older than 90 days over the total overdue balance | Set a threshold. Two months of rising means chasing has slipped |
| Average days to collect | Mean days from invoice to payment in full | If it grows, find which step of the sequence is being skipped |
| Plans with a signed document | Instalment cases with a signed plan over all instalment cases | It should be 100%. Anything missing is debt with nothing behind it |

The second number changes team behaviour fastest, because it turns a feeling into a percentage. The fourth is the one that stops the problem coming back.

## Debt that is never going to be collected

Part of the balance will never arrive, and keeping it on the list has a cost. It pollutes the four numbers above until the team stops looking at them.

Writing it off is an accounting decision with tax consequences, so it is taken with your accountant rather than at Monday's meeting. What the practice owes that conversation is the evidence: what was chased, when, by which channel and with what response.

That is another reason to log every contact on the record. The history is not bureaucracy, it is what turns a list of names into a file that can be closed.

## Where to start this week

1. **Pull the outstanding balance with future instalments separated out.** It is usually a good deal smaller than it felt.
2. **Sort it by age** into the four bands above rather than by invoice number.
3. **Bill everything that is finished and unbilled** before touching anything else. That part fixes itself.
4. **Pick the day of the month** the list gets worked, and who works it, by name.
5. **Call the ten oldest cases past sixty days** before writing any template. Ten calls will tell you what is really happening.
6. **Require a signed plan** on every new instalment arrangement from tomorrow, with the six lines above.
7. **Close what has been sitting for years**, deciding case by case whether to pursue it or let it go.
