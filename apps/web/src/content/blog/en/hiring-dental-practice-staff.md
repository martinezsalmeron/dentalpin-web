When a dental assistant advert gets no replies, the problem is usually not the market: the role was never defined, the advert does not answer what a candidate needs to know, and the first two weeks are not ready. Trained people exist, but the pool has changed shape, and practices that hire well do it by looking somewhere else and by keeping the people who arrive.

The order matters. Defining the role, writing the advert and planning the induction are three separate jobs, and publishing before the first one is done is the most common reason nobody comes.

## The workforce did not shrink, it went part time

The most detailed published figures on dental assistants in Europe are German, and they come from a parliamentary document: the federal government's answer to a Bundestag question, published on 18 December 2025, with Federal Employment Agency data as at November 2025.

Between 31 May 2015 and 31 May 2025 the number of dental assistants in insured employment in Germany rose from 194,324 to 222,110, up 14.3 per cent. The useful figure sits underneath that total.

> **Headcount rose 14.3 per cent while full time fell 5.2 per cent.** Full-time dental assistants went from 130,695 down to 123,836, and part-time from 63,629 up to 98,274, a rise of 54.4 per cent. More people in the profession, fewer available hours per person.

The second split says the same thing from another angle. Dental assistants with German citizenship grew 2.6 per cent over that decade; those with foreign citizenship grew 188.6 per cent, from 12,211 to 35,236. All of the growth came from part-time work and from abroad.

There is no reason to think this is only German. In France the shortage reached the National Assembly: written question 16750 of the 17th legislature, published in the Journal officiel of 7 July 2026, records that for dental assistants "les candidatures sont souvent peu nombreuses et les délais de recrutement peuvent être particulièrement longs", that applications are few and hiring times can be very long. No ministerial reply had been published when we checked.

For a practice that is hiring, the reading is direct. Advertising one full-time post for an experienced candidate means competing for the part of the market that is shrinking, and that is why that particular advert gets no answer.

## Define the role before writing the advert

"We need a nurse" is not one role, it is four, and in a small practice they blur together. Separating them changes who you are allowed to hire, because only one of the four turns on a qualification.

| Role | What decides the hire | Trainable in the practice? |
|---|---|---|
| Front desk and schedule | Telephone manner and order, not dental experience | ✓ Yes, in weeks |
| Chairside support | Method and tolerance for the clinical setting | ~ Partly, with a named mentor |
| Hygiene and prevention | Regulated qualification, experience does not substitute | ✗ No |
| Administration and collections | Judgement with numbers and consistency | ✓ Yes, if procedures are written down |

Which roles are closed by a registrable qualification is a national question and it varies more than people expect. Check it with your regulator or registration body before you advertise, because publishing a post you then cannot lawfully fill burns weeks.

The other roles can be opened to people with no dental background at all, and there the market is far wider. Someone orderly who has worked with the public learns a dental front desk in weeks. What is not learned in weeks is handling a patient who arrives in pain and frightened.

## Where the candidates are

- **Qualified people already working part time.** This is the group that grew 54.4 per cent in the figures above. Many would take more hours from a practice that commits to the rota in advance, and that is the argument, not the pay.
- **Your local training route.** Dental nursing and hygiene courses place students every year. An arrangement with the college puts you in front of candidates a year before they reach the market, with no competition.
- **People who already worked with you.** Former trainees, parental leave that never came back, people who left for another sector. The shortest list to draw up and the one with the highest return.
- **Your own team.** A receptionist with two years in the practice is usually a better coordinator than an external hire, and the vacancy that then opens is the easy one to fill.
- **Public-facing staff from other sectors.** Pharmacy, optics, local hospitality, call centres. They are looking for a stable rota, which is exactly what a dental practice can offer.

## The advert that gets replies

A dental advert competes with adverts that state the pay. If yours does not, the candidate who hesitates goes to the other one, and adjectives will not fix it.

1. **State the exact hours**, days and times. It is the first filter for anyone already working part time, and the single most decisive line for that group.
2. **Give a real pay range** that you will honour. A closed figure is not needed, a range is.
3. **Say whether dental experience is required**, in those words. If it is not, put it in the headline: it multiplies applications.
4. **Describe a day, not a person specification.** How many chairs, how big the team, how much is chairside and how much is front desk.
5. **Name who will train them** and what they will cover in the first fortnight. A named mentor beats "training provided".
6. **Give a reply deadline and keep it.** In a tight market the practice that answers within 48 hours hires; the one that takes a fortnight advertises again.

## The first two weeks decide whether they stay

Hiring and losing someone after two months costs more than not hiring, because the gap comes back with a more tired team. Induction is not paperwork, it is the first ten working days.

1. **Name one mentor from the team**, one, by name, and take part of their own diary away for those two weeks. Without this, none of the rest happens.
2. **Set up access before day one**: their own login, the permissions for their role, keys, phone. Starting on a colleague's account is the source of most of the later audit problems.
3. **First week, shadow and own one thing.** One complete task in their hands from day two, not five half tasks.
4. **Write down the five procedures used every day**, not the whole manual: opening, closing, booking, taking payment, sterilising. One page each.
5. **Fifteen minutes at the end of each day** in the first week, then weekly for the first month. That is where the questions come out that nobody asks in front of a patient.
6. **At a fortnight, a conversation with two questions**: what about the work was unexpected, and what is still missing to work unsupervised. The answers correct the role, not the person.
7. **Close the clinical induction in writing at one month**, listing what they now do unsupervised and what they do not.

> **The commonest expensive mistake is hiring for the title instead of the hours.** Count the hours you actually need to cover and split them by role first. Most practices find that two thirds of the gap is front desk, schedule and recalls, which can be recruited on an open market in weeks, and that filling it frees the qualified person already on the payroll for the chair.
