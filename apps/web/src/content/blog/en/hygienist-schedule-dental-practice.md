The hygienist needs a column of their own in the diary, not a slot borrowed from the dentist. That single decision changes everything downstream: while hygiene is booked as a gap between treatments, it is the first thing dropped when the day goes wrong, and the recall cycle goes with it. A column of its own, with its own appointment lengths, its own clinical record and its own close-out, turns hygiene into a loop that feeds itself.

In the UK there is a second reason, and it is bigger than the first. A hygienist can see patients without a dentist seeing them first, so that column is not downstream of anyone. It can be a front door.

> **Chair occupancy and recall are two different problems.** A chair either fills or it does not, and that is solved with opening hours. A recall cycle either holds or collapses, and that is solved by what the hygienist writes down before the patient stands up.

## What direct access actually changes

The General Dental Council's direct access guidance, consulted on 29 September 2026, is explicit: "Dental hygienists and dental therapists can carry out their full scope of practice (except tooth whitening) without referral from a dentist."

Two conditions come with it. They "should only diagnose and provide care within their scope of practice", and they "should refer to a dentist (or other relevant healthcare practitioner) when they identify areas of concern, or when the treatment required is out of their scope of practice".

That turns the hygiene column into something a diary has to model properly, because appointments can now arrive from outside as well as from inside.

- **New patients can book straight in.** The column needs its own availability that the public booking route can see, not a private resource hidden inside someone else's diary.
- **Referral out is a real event.** When the hygienist spots something, the record has to carry it to a dentist, with a date, rather than as a note nobody reads.
- **Whitening is the carve-out.** The GDC states that for tooth whitening "the first cycle of use must be provided by a dentist or by a dental hygienist, dental therapist... under the direct supervision of a dentist", so it is not an ordinary hygiene slot.
- **Scope is per registrant, not per role.** Two hygienists in the same practice can have different training, and the diary should reflect who can take what.

This is not legal advice. Outside the UK the rules are set by a different regulator, and this section does not describe them.

## Why a column and not a gap

A gap is negotiable. A column is not. When hygiene lives inside the dentist's diary, it competes with an emergency every morning, and it loses every time.

A separate column does three things at once. It separates the hygienist's availability from the dentist's, it allows different lengths for different appointment types, and it makes the empty slot visible early enough to fill.

- **Invisible double-booking stops.** If the hygienist only exists as a resource inside someone else's appointment, two people can take them at once with no warning.
- **There is more than one appointment length.** Periodontal maintenance is not a new-patient hygiene visit, and forcing both into one template guarantees one of them runs late every time.
- **Utilisation can be measured on its own.** If hygiene and treatment share a column, there is no way to tell which one is underperforming.

## What every appointment has to record

A hygiene appointment that records only "scale and polish done" is useless for the next recall, because it contains none of the things that decide when to come back.

1. **Why they came, and who sent them.** Direct access booking, internal referral, or a recall that fell due.
2. **The indices measured.** Plaque and bleeding, dated, because those are what make the next visit comparable.
3. **The periodontal chart when it applies.** Where there are pockets, the comparison between charts is what justifies the interval.
4. **What was actually done, by sextant.** A full debridement and a partial one are not the same appointment, and in six months nobody remembers.
5. **The home care advice given.** Technique, interdental, fluoride. Without it the next visit starts from zero.
6. **The proposed interval and the reason for it.** Not a loose date, but an interval with the clinical reason beside it.
7. **Anything referred out.** Recorded as a finding with a date, and tracked until a dentist has seen it.

## Three appointment types, three templates

Putting all three on one length is the most expensive mistake in this column, because whichever one runs short drags the rest of the day with it.

| | New patient hygiene | Routine maintenance | Periodontal maintenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| What triggers it | Direct access or new registration | Interval fell due | Discharge from active therapy |
| Minimum record | Indices and habit history | Indices, compared | Periodontal chart, compared |

## How the interval is actually set

NICE clinical guideline CG19, consulted on 29 September 2026, puts the decision on risk rather than on a fixed period: "The recommended interval between oral health reviews should be determined specifically for each patient and tailored to meet his or her needs, on the basis of an assessment of disease levels and risk of or from dental disease."

The guideline then sets the outer edges. The shortest interval between oral health reviews for all patients "should be 3 months". The longest is "12 months" for patients younger than 18 and "24 months" for patients aged 18 and older, and it notes that intervals beyond 24 months "are undesirable".

> **An interval is a clinical decision recorded at the chair, not an administrative default.** A practice that books everyone at six months has not assessed anyone, and it will be wrong in both directions: too often for the low-risk patient, and far too rarely for the one who needed three months.

## Closing the loop

1. **Set the interval while closing the appointment.** The hygienist has just looked in the mouth, and it is the only moment the information is fresh.
2. **Store the interval on the patient, not in a separate list.** A parallel spreadsheet survives about three months.
3. **Let the system surface the patient when the interval falls due.** Otherwise recall is a task competing with a full front desk.
4. **Send the prompt with enough lead time to book.** A 48-hour reminder confirms an appointment, it does not create one.
5. **Push uncovered slots to a cancellation list.** A hygiene cancellation is the easiest gap in the whole diary to fill, because it does not need the dentist.
6. **Measure what stays empty.** A weekly percentage of unfilled hygiene time says more about the loop than any impression does.

## The mistakes that repeat

- **Booking against the chair instead of the clinician.** When the hygienist moves surgery, the diary stops adding up and nobody can say what their utilisation was.
- **Recording hygiene as a sale rather than as clinical care.** If it only exists on the invoice, the clinical detail that sets the next interval is gone.
- **Leaving recall in one person's head.** It works until they take annual leave, and the drop shows up six months later, which is too late to fix.

None of this needs a particular product. It needs a diary with one column per clinician, an interval field on the patient record, and the discipline of closing the appointment before the patient stands up.
