Record what you can see, and keep the verdict on whoever did it out of the notes. The finding goes in the record on the day you make it, in descriptive language, with the radiograph or photograph that supports it. What you tell the patient is the state of their mouth now and the options they have, which is a different conversation from an opinion about the practitioner who treated them. If you genuinely believe patients are at risk, the GDC standards do not ask you to stay quiet: they require you to raise a concern, and that duty overrides professional loyalty.

This post is about the UK, because the rules that answer it are the General Dental Council's. It is not legal advice, and the sources are listed at the end with the date consulted.

## The record is the whole defence

Standard 4.1 of *Standards for the Dental Team* is the one this situation turns on. It requires you to "make and keep contemporaneous, complete and accurate patient records", and the guidance under it is unusually specific about what counts.

Standard 4.1.1 says records include "radiographs, consent forms, photographs, models, audio or visual recordings of consultations, laboratory prescriptions, statements of conformity and referral letters" wherever those exist. A first appointment with a patient who has been treated elsewhere is exactly when most of that list gets created.

Two more that decide how a record reads two years later. Standard 4.1.4 requires that documentation is "clear, legible, accurate, and can be readily understood by others", and that you "record the name or initials of the treating clinician". Standard 4.1.5 requires that any amendment to a record is "clearly marked up and dated".

> **An amendment that is not marked up is worse than no amendment.** A note tidied up after a complaint arrives looks exactly like a note tidied up after a complaint arrives. Marked and dated changes are the only version of that edit anyone will believe.

## A finding is not a verdict

The useful distinction is not about being polite. A finding is something another clinician could verify by looking at the same mouth and the same images. A verdict assigns blame, and you cannot assign blame without knowing what happened in that surgery, what the patient was told, or what they chose.

| Record this | Not this |
|---|---|
| ✓ Crown at UR6 with visible marginal discrepancy mesially on today's periapical | ✗ Badly fitted crown |
| ✓ LL6 absent; patient reports extraction in 2024 at another practice | ✗ They took out a tooth that could have been saved |
| ✓ Root filling at UR1 ending 4 mm short of the radiographic apex, currently asymptomatic | ✗ Incomplete root treatment, poor technique |
| ✓ Patient states they did not receive a written estimate | ✗ Nobody explained anything to them, as usual |
| ✓ Options discussed and written estimate provided, dated today | ~ Explained that all of this needs redoing |

The last row is the one people argue about. That a restoration needs replacing is a legitimate clinical conclusion and belongs in the notes. What does not belong is writing it as a reproach rather than as an indication, without the findings that support it.

## The first appointment, step by step

This appointment gets documented more heavily than a routine check-up, because it establishes the baseline. If a complaint arrives two years later, the question will be what state the patient was in when they walked in.

1. **A full charting, tooth by tooth.** What is present, what is missing, what is restored, before anything is touched.
2. **Images taken today.** Radiographs justified on their own clinical merits, and intraoral photographs where the case warrants them. A claim about a marginal discrepancy with no image showing it is an opinion.
3. **What the patient says, attributed to them.** Dates as they recall them, what they were told, what hurt. Recorded as the patient's account, never as a fact the practice has verified.
4. **What you explained, and the evidence they understood it.** Standard 4.1.2 asks you to record "as much detail as possible" about these discussions, including evidence that valid consent was obtained.
5. **The treatment plan and the estimate, dated.** Plus the consent for whatever you are about to do, which is specific to the procedure.
6. **What you decided not to do, and why.** If you are leaving someone else's crown alone for now, write that down. A record that only lists what was done does not explain the decisions.
7. **If this is an emergency attendance, the limit of what you did.** What was resolved today and what remains to be assessed.

## Getting the previous records

Through the patient. Standard 4.4 requires you to "ensure that patients can have access to their records", so the previous practice owes their patient that access, and the request is the patient's to make. A subject access request under UK data protection law is the same route by another name.

Until those records arrive, the only documented account of the previous state is the one you create today. That is the practical reason for the order of operations: examine and document first, request second.

If the case needs someone else, [what travels with a dental referral and what should come back](/en/blog/dental-patient-referrals/) is a separate workflow, and standard 4.1.6 requires that the referral itself is recorded accurately in the patient's notes.

## When raising a concern becomes a duty

Principle 8 is where the professional obligation flips. Standard 8.1.1 says you must raise any concern that patients might be at risk due to, among other things, "the health, behaviour or professional performance of a colleague". The same guidance adds that your duty to raise concerns "overrides any personal and professional loyalties".

Standard 8.2.2 removes the usual excuse for not doing it: "you should not have to prove your concern for it to be investigated", and raising a justified concern should not be held against you. It also warns that failing to raise one can put your own registration at risk.

The route matters. Standard 8.2.3 says to raise concerns with your employer or manager where possible, and 8.2.4 covers what to do when that is not appropriate or they fail to act: escalate to the local commissioner of health or to the relevant regulator, which the guidance lists as the Care Quality Commission, Healthcare Inspectorate Wales, the Regulation and Quality Improvement Authority or Healthcare Improvement Scotland.

> **None of those routes is the patient.** Telling a patient that their previous dentist is not fit to practise protects nobody and starts nothing. Standard 6.1.2 still applies while you do it: you must treat colleagues "fairly and with respect, in all situations and all forms of interaction and communication".

## Where software helps and where it does not

No system decides what you may say about a colleague. Standard 8.1.1 and your own judgement decide that.

What software settles is the evidence problem behind it. Standard 4.1.4 wants the treating clinician named on the entry, 4.1.5 wants amendments marked and dated, and 4.1.1 wants the radiograph filed as part of the record rather than in a folder somewhere. All three are properties of the system, not of how careful you were on the day. If you also need to know who opened a record and when, [an audit trail on dental records](/en/blog/audit-trail-dental-records/) is the part that answers it.
