A relative is not automatically entitled to a dead patient's chart. In the US the person who steps into the patient's shoes is whoever has authority under state law to act for the estate, and a family member involved in care before the death can get only the information relevant to that involvement. In England, Wales and Scotland a separate 1990 statute applies, it names two kinds of applicant, and it caps what a claimant can see at what is relevant to the claim.

> **Death does not end the obligation, and under HIPAA it puts a number on it.** 45 CFR 164.502(f) requires a covered entity to protect a deceased individual's health information "for a period of 50 years following the death of the individual". The chart of a patient who died last month is still protected health information.

This is not any of the three cases that already have their own article. When the patient asks, that is [their right of access](/en/blog/patient-requests-their-dental-records/). When a court, an insurer or an employer asks, that is [a third party request](/en/blog/third-party-requests-dental-records/). How long the file has to exist at all is [a retention question](/en/blog/how-long-to-keep-dental-records/).

Here the person who held the right has died and the caller is not the patient. It is the one version of this request that the front desk has no script for.

## In the US, "personal representative" is a legal role, not a relationship

HIPAA does not give the family a right of access. It gives it to a personal representative, and 45 CFR 164.502(g)(4) defines who that is after a death:

> **"If under applicable law an executor, administrator, or other person has authority to act on behalf of a deceased individual or of the individual's estate, a covered entity must treat such person as a personal representative under this subchapter, with respect to protected health information relevant to such personal representation."**

Two things follow from that sentence, and both change what you do at the desk.

- **The authority comes from state law, not from HIPAA.** Whether the person calling has it is a question about the estate, which means the practice has to establish authority rather than assume it from the relationship.
- **The access is scoped.** "Relevant to such personal representation" is a limit. A personal representative handling the estate is not automatically entitled to every clinical note ever written.

There is a second, narrower route that does not involve the estate at all. Under 45 CFR 164.510(b)(5), where the individual is deceased you may disclose to a family member or other person who was involved in the individual's care or payment for care before the death the information "that is relevant to such person's involvement", and the regulation closes with the condition that decides most of these calls: "unless doing so is inconsistent with any prior expressed preference of the individual that is known to the covered entity".

## In the UK, a 1990 Act takes over

The UK GDPR does not apply to a dead person, so the right of access route closes and the Access to Health Records Act 1990 opens. Section 3(1) names who may apply once the patient has died:

> **"where the patient has died, the patient's personal representative and any person who may have a claim arising out of the patient's death".**

Section 3(1)(g), inserted in September 2024, adds a medical examiner exercising functions under the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 in relation to the death.

The same Act sets out the mechanics, and they are more specific than most practices expect:

1. **Inspection or copy, the applicant's choice.** Section 3(2) requires the holder to allow the applicant to inspect the record, and, "if the applicant so requires", to supply a copy.
2. **No fee.** Section 3(4) says plainly that "No fee shall be required for giving access under subsection (2) above".
3. **21 days, or 40.** Section 3(5) gives 21 days where every part of the record applied for was made within the 40 days before the application, and 40 days in any other case. For a dental record going back years, it is 40.
4. **Unintelligible terms get an explanation.** Section 3(3) requires an explanation of terms that cannot be understood without one, supplied with the record.
5. **The clock can be reset once.** Under section 3(6), if you ask within 14 days for the further information you reasonably need to identify the patient or satisfy yourself the applicant is entitled, the period runs from when that information arrives.

## What you hold back, in both countries

| | US, personal representative | US, family involved in care | UK, claim arising out of the death |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source of the right | 45 CFR 164.502(g)(4) | 45 CFR 164.510(b)(5) | s. 3(1)(f), 1990 Act |
| Scope | ~ Relevant to the representation | ~ Relevant to their involvement | ~ Relevant to the claim, s. 5(4) |
| Patient's known contrary wish | ~ Depends on state law and the estate | ✗ Blocks the disclosure | ✗ Blocks it, s. 5(3)(a) |
| Fee allowed | ~ Within the access rules | ~ Not an access request | ✗ No fee, s. 3(4) |
| Deadline | ✓ 30 days, access rules | ~ No fixed period | ✓ 21 or 40 days, s. 3(5) |

The UK exclusions in section 5 are worth reading before you copy anything. Access is not given to a part of the record that, in the holder's opinion, would disclose "information provided by the patient in the expectation that it would not be disclosed to the applicant", or information obtained from an examination the patient consented to on the same expectation. Section 5(1)(a)(ii) protects information relating to or provided by a third party who could be identified from it, and section 5(4) limits a claim applicant to what is relevant to the claim.

> **The family asking is not a reason to skip the identity check.** A disputed estate is the most common reason this request lands at all, and the sibling on the phone may be exactly the person the other sibling does not want anything released to. Identity and entitlement get established the same way they would for a stranger.

## How to handle the call

1. **Write it down, even if it came by phone.** Who is asking, in what capacity, for what, and why.
2. **Establish the capacity, not the relationship.** Executor or administrator in the US, personal representative or claimant in the UK. Ask what you reasonably need to satisfy yourself, which in the UK is a step the Act expressly contemplates in section 3(6).
3. **Look for a known contrary preference.** Both the US family route and the UK Act turn on it. If it lives only in the memory of whoever had that conversation, you do not actually know it.
4. **Decide the scope before you print.** Relevant to the representation, relevant to their involvement, or relevant to the claim. Those are three different extracts from one chart.
5. **Separate what has to stay in.** Third party information, and anything the patient gave on the understanding it would not reach this applicant.
6. **Log the disclosure.** Date, recipient, scope and the basis you relied on.
7. **Put a refusal in writing.** A reasoned refusal is an answer. Silence is not, and in the UK the 21 or 40 day period is running either way.
