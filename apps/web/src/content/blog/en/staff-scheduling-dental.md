A dental rota is built from the operatories outwards, not from what the team would prefer: decide which chairs you want open in each block, then who covers them, and only then who prefers what. Publish it ahead of time, name a substitute for every role rather than a general one, and keep a record of the hours actually worked, because both the UK and the US require records of some kind.

The difference between a practice that absorbs a sick day and one that cancels the whole schedule is not size. It is whether somebody wrote down who covers what before it was needed.

## An open operatory is not one person, it is three

The mistake that causes all the others is rostering only the dentists. A chair produces when several people coincide, and one missing person is enough to turn a covered day into an uncovered one.

- **The dentist or hygienist**, who owns the schedule column and is the one role almost everybody remembers to roster.
- **The nurse or assistant**, who sets the actual pace. With an assistant you can run patients back to back; without one the same clinician takes close to twice as long per patient.
- **The front desk**, which holds the phone, the waiting room and the payments. When the front desk is short, the gap does not show in the schedule, it shows three days later in the treatment plans nobody followed up.
- **Whoever owns decontamination that shift.** It does not have to be a dedicated person, but it has to be a named one, because instruments do not turn themselves around between patients.

> **Roster roles, then names.** Write down how many people each block needs and in what role, and hand out names afterwards. Start with names and you get a rota that only works when nobody is missing, which is exactly the day you did not need one.

## Build the rota in this order

1. **Set the opening hours of each operatory**, not of the practice. One chair open 9 to 2 and another 12 to 8 are two separate decisions and they are rostered separately.
2. **Assign the clinical seat for each block** and, next to it, the assistant who belongs to it. If there is no assistant for that block, that block is not open, however available the dentist is.
3. **Cover the front desk from the first appointment to the last**, including the overlap at open and close. It is the most assumed role and the most expensive one to leave uncovered.
4. **Mark who is absent in the same rota**, with the reason in one word: leave, training, rest day, sick. An unlabelled gap reads as availability and somebody will book into it.
5. **Name a substitute per role, not per practice.** "If the morning nurse is out, Ana comes in and we drop operatory 2" is a plan. "We will sort it out" is not.

## What falls over when each role is missing

This is the table worth deciding calmly, because on the day of the absence it gets decided in a hurry and usually badly.

| Who is missing | Can you keep treating? | What goes first |
|---|---|---|
| Front desk | ~ Partly | The phone, the payments and treatment plan follow-up |
| Nurse or assistant | ✗ Not normally | Chair pace and instrument turnaround between patients |
| Hygienist | ~ Their column only | The day's hygiene visits, the easiest appointments to move |
| Dentist | ✗ No | Everything clinical in that column, long treatments included |
| Decontamination lead for the shift | ~ Depends on the load | Available instruments from mid-morning onwards |

The hygienist row is the good news in that table. Those appointments are short, repeatable and have no open course of treatment behind them, which is why that is where you sacrifice capacity rather than in a chair with work in progress.

## The first hour of an absence

Most of the damage from a sick call happens in the first sixty minutes, and most of it is avoidable with a fixed order.

1. **Work out which blocks are now uncovered** and which patients sit inside them, before calling anyone.
2. **Separate what can stand from what cannot.** A check-up or a hygiene visit survives almost any reshuffle; a long appointment under local anaesthetic does not.
3. **Call the affected patients yourself, and early.** A call first thing is accepted. The same call thirty minutes before the appointment is remembered for months.
4. **Offer a specific slot when you rebook**, never "we will call you back". An appointment that falls without a new date is a lost appointment about half the time.
5. **Put the change in the rota, not just in the schedule.** If the absence runs three days, Wednesday's rota is already wrong and nobody has looked at it.

## The records the law expects, and they are not the rota

Two jurisdictions, two quite different answers, and it is worth knowing which one you are in.

**In the UK**, regulation 9 of the Working Time Regulations 1998 requires an employer to "keep records which are adequate to show whether the employer has complied with the limits" in the regulations listed there, and to "retain such records for two years from the date on which they were made". Since 1 January 2024 the same regulation adds two things that matter to a small practice: the records "may be created, maintained and kept in such manner and format as the employer reasonably thinks fit", and an employer "need not record each worker's daily working hours in order to comply with paragraph (1) if the employer is able to demonstrate compliance without doing so".

**In the US**, the recordkeeping rule under the Fair Labor Standards Act is more specific about hours. 29 CFR 516.2 requires every covered employer to maintain and preserve records including "Hours worked each workday and total hours worked each workweek", along with the time of day and day of the week on which the employee's workweek begins.

> **The rota is a plan, the record is history.** The rota says what was meant to happen and the record says what did. Producing one when you are asked for the other is the most common documentation failure in a small practice, and it costs nothing to fix.

If you also employ people in Spain, France, Germany, Italy, Poland or Portugal, do not carry this section across the border. The duty to record daily start and finish times is far stricter in several of those countries than the UK position above.

## Annual leave, planned once a year

Handing out holiday in June is how half of a practice's staffing arguments start. The statutory floor gives you an anchor to plan against.

Under regulation 13 of the Working Time Regulations 1998 a worker in the UK is "entitled to four weeks' annual leave in each leave year", and regulation 13A adds a period of additional leave on top of that. The US position is the opposite: the Department of Labor states that "the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) does not require payment for time not worked, such as vacations, sick leave or federal or other holidays", which are a matter of agreement between employer and employee. For US staff, your own policy is the whole answer.

In practice, whichever applies to you:

- **Close the year's leave calendar in January**, not in May. Notice periods are a floor, not a target.
- **Decide first which weeks the practice itself closes**, if any, and allocate the rest around them.
- **Block approved leave in the schedule the day it is approved**, so nobody books over it.
- **Check that the front desk and decontamination are never at zero at the same time.** August is usually where this breaks.

## What makes a rota fall apart

- **It does not exist in writing.** It lives in the head of the person who makes it, who is also the person who takes leave.
- **It changes over WhatsApp.** If the change never gets back into the rota, the rota stopped being true on day one.
- **It has no named substitutes.** A plan that starts with "we will ring around" is not a plan.
- **Nobody checks the schedule against it.** Booking a 7:30pm appointment with one clinician who started at 8am is easy when the two documents live in different places.
- **It does not separate planned from actual.** Without that split you have neither a usable working time record nor any idea how many hours are really being worked.

## Where software actually helps

A rota in a spreadsheet works perfectly well in a single-chair practice, and there is no reason to replace it for its own sake. It starts to fail when the rota and the appointment book are two separate documents, because then the front desk books from one while the team works from the other.
