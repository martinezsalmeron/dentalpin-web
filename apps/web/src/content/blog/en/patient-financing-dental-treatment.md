When a £3,000 treatment plan cannot be paid in one go there are three routes, and they are not interchangeable. The practice waits for its own money, a lender pays the practice and collects from the patient, or the practice sells the invoice to a third party. All three get the patient treated, but each one changes who holds the cash, who chases it, and who the problem comes back to when the treatment plan changes halfway through.

The operational question is always the same one: after the paperwork is signed, who owes what to whom, and does the software know? If the answer lives in the head of whoever was on reception that day, the practice has a cash-flow problem waiting to happen.

This is not legal or financial advice. Every claim below is sourced, with the date consulted.

## Three instruments, three different risks

| | Practice's own plan | Third-party lender | Factoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who puts up the money | The practice, by waiting | A lender, to the patient | A factor, buying the receivable |
| When the practice is paid | ✗ In instalments, as the patient pays | ✓ Up front | ✓ Up front |
| Who chases the patient | The practice | The lender | The factor |
| Who carries the default | ✗ The practice, in full | ✓ The lender | ~ Depends on recourse or non-recourse |
| Patient data leaving the practice | ✓ None | Identity and amount | Identity, amount and what for |
| If treatment is not completed | The practice reworks the plan | ~ The patient can claim against the lender | ~ The factor can come back to the practice |
| Practice becomes a credit broker | ✓ No | ✗ Yes, if it presents or offers the agreement | ✓ No |

That last row is the one practices discover late. Handing a lender's form across the desk is not an administrative courtesy in the United Kingdom, it is a named regulated activity, and it is better to know that before ordering the leaflets.

## Introducing a patient to a lender is a regulated activity

The Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Regulated Activities) Order 2001 defines credit broking at article 36A, and the list is wider than most people assume. It covers "effecting an introduction" of someone who wishes to enter into a credit agreement to a prospective lender, but also "presenting or offering an agreement which would (if entered into) be a regulated credit agreement" and "assisting an individual or relevant recipient of credit by undertaking preparatory work" with a view to that person entering into one.

Presenting, offering and helping with the form are therefore each enough on their own. The exclusion at article 36B is narrow: it only covers activities carried on by an individual canvassing off trade premises, which is not what happens at a reception desk.

> **Section 19 of FSMA 2000 is blunt about the consequence.** "No person may carry on a regulated activity in the United Kingdom, or purport to do so, unless he is an authorised person or an exempt person." Whether a given practice needs authorisation, or is covered by an exemption, is a question for the FCA and the practice's own advisers, not for the lender's sales representative.

The practical version is short. Check whether the practice itself is on the Financial Services Register for credit broking, and if it is not, work out which of two models applies: the lender's own authorised representative does the selling, or the patient is pointed to the lender and completes everything without the practice presenting or preparing anything.

## Why an unfinished treatment comes back to the practice

This is the risk almost nobody writes down, and in the United Kingdom it is unusually explicit.

Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 says that where the debtor under a debtor-creditor-supplier agreement "has, in relation to a transaction financed by the agreement, any claim against the supplier in respect of a misrepresentation or breach of contract, he shall have a like claim against the creditor, who, with the supplier, shall accordingly be jointly and severally liable to the debtor."

Then subsection (2) closes the loop back onto the practice. "Subject to any agreement between them, the creditor shall be entitled to be indemnified by the supplier for loss suffered by the creditor in satisfying his liability under subsection (1), including costs reasonably incurred by him in defending proceedings instituted by the debtor."

Two limits are worth knowing. Subsection (3)(b) takes the section out of play where the cash price of a single item does not exceed £100 or is more than £30,000, which leaves most of dentistry squarely inside it.

> **The patient stops paying the day the treatment stops, and the lender looks at the practice.** That is why the date each stage was actually delivered is not a clinical detail. It is the evidence that the financed work was done.

## Readers in the EU: the date to put in the diary

Practices outside the United Kingdom work to a different instrument, and it changes shortly. Directive (EU) 2023/2225 on consumer credit agreements replaces the 2008 directive, and Article 48(1) requires Member States to "apply those measures from 20 November 2026". Article 47 repeals Directive 2008/48/EC with effect from the same date.

Two of its provisions decide most dental cases:

- **Article 2(2)(h) draws the line between deferring and lending.** A deferred payment is outside the directive where no third party offers credit, the price is paid "free of interest and without any other charges" apart from limited late-payment charges, and "the payment is to be entirely executed within 50 days of the delivery of the good or service". The directive's own recitals give the example of "deferred payment of medical bills where hospitals give time to consumers to pay for medical expenses".
- **Article 27(2) is the EU counterpart of section 75.** Where the financed services "are not supplied or provided, or are supplied or provided only in part, or are not in conformity with the contract", the consumer may pursue remedies against the creditor, having first pursued the supplier without satisfaction.

Article 3(13) also explains what creates that link, and it is the marketing rather than the money: a commercial unit is deemed to exist where the creditor "uses the services of the supplier of goods or the provider of services in connection with the marketing, conclusion or preparation of the credit agreement", or where the specific service is explicitly specified in the credit agreement.

## When the plan changes halfway, the finance does not change itself

A phased plan gets revised more often than anyone admits, and that is the moment the finance quietly breaks.

1. **Freeze the financed amount** and set it against what is genuinely left to do.
2. **Write down which stages have been delivered**, on what date and by whom.
3. **Decide whether the new plan fits inside the financed amount.** If it is smaller, there is money in the practice that is not the practice's.
4. **Tell the lender about the change** through the agreed channel, and keep the acknowledgement. This is the step that gets skipped.
5. **Record the patient's agreement** to the revised plan and to what happens to the finance.
6. **Re-invoice what was delivered**, not the original quote.
7. **Leave the patient balance at zero or explained.** An unexplained live balance is the opening line of a complaint.

## What leaves the practice is health data

Sending a lender a patient's name, an amount and a treatment description is not a neutral administrative step. Article 9(1) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 prohibits the processing of "data concerning health", and Article 9(2)(a) lifts that prohibition where the data subject "has given explicit consent to the processing of those personal data for one or more specified purposes". Readers in the United Kingdom should read the corresponding provision of the UK GDPR, which is the instrument that applies there.

- **Ask before anything is sent**, in writing, naming the lender and the purpose.
- **Send the minimum that makes the credit work.** An amount and a quote reference will usually do it. The charting will not.
- **A refusal must not change the treatment offered.** If it does, the consent was not freely given.

## The eight fields worth recording

- **The quote it attaches to**, with its amount and the date it was accepted.
- **The instrument**: own plan, lender or factoring. Three different circuits, and mixing them is where the mess starts.
- **The provider's full name** and the reference number it uses for the case.
- **The financed amount and the patient's own share.** They rarely add up to the quote total.
- **The date the money actually reaches the practice**, which is the one that matters for the day's takings, not the signature date.
- **The commission retained**, because it is a practice cost and it comes out of the margin.
- **Which stages the finance covers**, and what happens to it if the plan changes.
- **The consent to share data**, with its date and scope.

## The mistakes that cost money

- **Treating the case as paid on the day of signature.** The money arrives when it arrives, and the commission has already gone.
- **Arranging finance before the quote is accepted.** It leaves the practice without the document that says what it agreed to do.
- **Not recording the commission.** Case profitability then gets read off the gross figure and looks better than it is.
- **Confusing finance with a deposit.** A deposit is money the practice owes back, covered in [patient deposits and credits](/en/blog/dental-patient-deposits-and-credits/).
- **Confusing it with patient debt.** Once a third party has lent, the debt is not the practice's. Running your own is a separate job, in [tracking patient payment plans](/en/blog/patient-payment-plans-tracking/).
- **Closing the case without telling the lender the plan changed.** The patient stops paying, the lender comes back under section 75, and the practice has no paperwork.

## What the system has to be able to show you

Three views: which cases have live third-party finance and what stage they are at, what has been received from each provider and when, and how much of each financed quote is still undelivered. The third one is what prevents the nasty surprise, and it is exactly the one nobody has.

Dentalpin keeps the finance attached to the quote that justifies it, separates money received from a third party from what the patient still owes, and records each delivered stage with its date on the same patient record, which is what you need on the day someone asks whether the financed work was actually done.
