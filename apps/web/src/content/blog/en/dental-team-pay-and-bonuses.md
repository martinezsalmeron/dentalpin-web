Pay for a dental assistant, a hygienist or a front desk coordinator is built in three layers, in this order: the statutory wage floor, which in the UK is £12.71 an hour for workers aged 21 and over and in the US is $7.25 an hour federally before any higher state rate; whatever the employment contract and any applicable agreement already commit you to; and on top of that, the variable you design. Only the third layer is yours to set. Most pay disputes in a small practice come from designing the third before writing down the second.

The floor is the easy part. The part that goes wrong is the bonus nobody wrote down.

## The floor, and which floor applies to you

The two markets this post is read in work differently, and the difference matters before you quote anyone a number.

In the UK the National Minimum Wage and National Living Wage are set centrally and change on 1 April every year. From April 2026 the rates are £12.71 for workers aged 21 and over, £10.85 for 18 to 20 year olds, £8 for under 18s and £8 for apprentices, with the apprentice rate applying to apprentices aged under 19, or aged 19 or over and in the first year of their apprenticeship.

In the US the federal floor under the Fair Labor Standards Act is "$7.25 per hour effective July 24, 2009", and the Department of Labor is explicit that it is a floor and not a ceiling: "Some state laws provide greater employee protections; employers must comply with both."

| | Who sets it | Your room to move |
|---|---|---|
| UK National Minimum and Living Wage | Central government, revised each April | ✗ None downward |
| US federal minimum under the FLSA | Federal statute | ✗ None downward |
| US state minimum wage | The state, where it is higher | ✗ None downward |
| Contractual pay and premiums | The practice and the employee | ✓ Upward, in writing |
| The variable on top | The practice | ✓ Wide |

> **A US practice may have two floors, not one.** Where a state sets a higher rate, the Department of Labor's position is that both apply and the employer complies with both, which in practice means the state figure is the operative one. Check the rate for the state the chair is in, not the state the owner lives in.

## Write the pay down, because you have to anyway

In the UK this is not optional. Section 1 of the Employment Rights Act 1996 requires the written statement of particulars to include "the scale or rate of remuneration or the method of calculating remuneration" and "the intervals at which remuneration is paid (that is, weekly, monthly or other specified intervals)".

Read that as a drafting instruction rather than a compliance chore. "The method of calculating remuneration" means a bonus you cannot describe as a method is a bonus you have not actually documented, whatever the practice has been paying.

1. **State the base rate and the hours it assumes.** An hourly rate and a monthly salary behave differently the first time someone works a bank holiday.
2. **State each premium separately** and say what triggers it, rather than folding three things into one round number.
3. **Describe the bonus as a formula**, with the metric, the period and who it belongs to.
4. **Say whether it is discretionary**, and mean it. A bonus paid on the same terms for three years running is hard to describe as discretionary afterwards.
5. **Put a review date in the document.** "In force until December, reviewed in January" removes the entire argument about why it changed.
6. **Pay it on money collected, not money billed.** A signed treatment plan is not revenue and neither is an issued invoice.

> **An individual bonus you cannot attribute from data is a bonus you cannot defend.** If the only record of who did what is somebody's memory of the month, the first disagreement about the figure has no way of ending. Decide the attribution before deciding the percentage.

## What not to tie a bonus to

- **Treatment plan acceptance.** This is the one with no good fix. Paying someone more for getting more yeses puts a financial interest inside a clinical conversation, and a patient who senses it does not come back and does not say why.
- **Total practice revenue.** An assistant does not set the schedule, the fee guide or how many emergencies walk in during August.
- **Individual targets on shared work.** A hygiene appointment is prepared by one person, delivered by another and collected by a third. Splitting that into three individual targets produces arguments, not production.
- **A number you can change mid-year.** A bonus that gets adjusted when it turns out expensive stops working as an incentive on the day it is adjusted.
- **Sickness absence.** Rewarding people for not calling in sick pushes someone to come in with a fever to a place where people sit with their mouths open.

## What to tie it to instead

- **Recall actually closed**, not calls made. It is objective, it is genuinely front desk work, and it counts itself.
- **Short notice gaps filled** from the waiting list, which is the metric that tracks the month's takings most closely.
- **Day end reconciliation done and balanced**, which sounds minor and is the task whose failure takes longest to notice.
- **Surgery level team measures**: stock logged, sterilisation documented, notes closed the same day. Everyone is paid the same and nobody is set against anyone.
- **Training completed**, with the certificate as the condition. It is the only bonus that leaves the person better off even if they leave.

## What the software has to give you

- **Attribution per treatment and per clinician**, not just per patient, because without it no individual bonus can be justified.
- **Production for a closed period** that does not move when somebody edits an old treatment plan halfway through the month.
- **Collected against billed**, kept separate, so the variable settles on what arrived.
- **Recall with an outcome**, distinguishing the patient who was contacted from the patient who ended up in the chair.
- **Export to a spreadsheet**, because payroll is run somewhere else and needs the figure in a format it can read.
