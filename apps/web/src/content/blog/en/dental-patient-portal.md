A patient portal should let people do five things without picking up the phone: see and cancel their appointments, fill in their details and health history before they arrive, read and sign treatment plans and consent forms, see what they owe and pay it, and get a copy of their clinical record. Everything else is optional, and most of the rest adds risk without taking work off the front desk.

What follows is why a portal nobody uses is almost never a software problem, what patients can already demand of their record whether you have a portal or not, and where the line sits on what to show.

## A portal nobody uses is a front desk problem

It is the most expensive failure and the easiest to fix, because you do not fix it by buying something else.

The best public data on this comes from *Individuals' Access and Use of Patient Portals and Smartphone Health Apps*, published by ASTP in July 2025 using the 2024 HINTS survey. It covers US healthcare rather than dentistry specifically, but it measures something that does not depend on the specialty: what makes a patient log in.

In 2024, 77 percent of individuals were offered online access to their health information and 65 percent logged in at least once, up from 73 and 57 percent in 2022.

The interesting part comes next. Of those offered access, 89 percent said their provider encouraged them to use the portal. That one detail changes the whole outcome.

| | Encouraged by their provider | Not encouraged |
|---|---|---|
| Accessed the portal at least once | ✓ 87% | ✗ 57% |
| Viewed test results | ✓ 92% | ~ 78% |
| Viewed clinical notes | ✓ 82% | ✗ 57% |

> **Thirty points of adoption come from one sentence at the front desk.** No portal feature moves the number as much as somebody in the practice mentioning it out loud on the way out. If you are buying a portal and not changing what gets said at checkout, save the money.

One more figure from the same brief is worth keeping in mind: 59 percent of people already had more than one portal or online record, and fewer than one in ten used an app to pull them together. Your patient is not waiting for your portal, they are juggling several.

## The five things they should be able to do alone

The question is not how many features it has. It is which of them replace an entire phone call. A feature that handles half a task and leaves the other half on the phone has moved the work, not removed it.

| Feature | In the portal? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| See upcoming appointments and cancel them | ✓ Yes | The most common call and the simplest to automate |
| Update contact details and medical history | ✓ Yes | It arrives filled in and nobody retypes it |
| Read and sign treatment plans and consent forms | ✓ Yes | Clears the clipboard and timestamps the signature |
| See the balance and pay it | ✓ Yes | Collecting without a conversation is what the front desk notices |
| Download a copy of their clinical record | ✓ Yes | You already owe it, and this is the cheapest way to deliver it |
| View their radiographs | ~ With care | An image with no report generates a call instead of preventing one |
| Read verbatim clinical notes | ~ It depends | They are written for the clinician, not the patient |
| Book an emergency slot | ✗ No | Somebody has to decide whether it waits until morning |
| Edit their own clinical record | ✗ No | The entry belongs to the clinician who signed it |

- **Start with appointments and the health history form.** Both show a result in the first week, and neither forces you to decide anything about clinical data.
- **Signed treatment plans pay for the portal fastest.** A plan read at home, unhurried and with nobody standing there, gets accepted differently than one explained at the desk.
- **Online payment earns its keep on the phone, not the bank balance.** The win is the "how much do I still owe" calls that nobody has to answer.
- **Record download is not a marketing feature.** It is what stops every records request turning into half an hour of admin.

## What patients can demand whether you have a portal or not

This is where a portal stops being a convenience.

In the United States, the HIPAA right of access at 45 CFR 164.524 gives individuals the right to see and get a copy of their health information held in a designated record set. A practice must act on the request within 30 calendar days, with a single 30-day extension available, and where the information is held electronically the individual can ask for an electronic copy. HHS guidance treats a patient portal as a legitimate way of providing that access, not as something separate from it.

In the UK, Ireland and the EU, the equivalent is Article 15 of the GDPR, which gives a right to a copy of the personal data being processed, with Article 12(3) setting a one-month deadline to respond. Australia and New Zealand run their own equivalents under their privacy legislation.

> **The GDPR names the portal as a way of complying.** Recital 63 says that "where possible, the controller should be able to provide remote access to a secure system which would provide the data subject with direct access to his or her personal data". A properly built portal is not an extra on top of the access right, it is the cheapest way to satisfy it.

The practical consequence is the same on both sides of the Atlantic. Every records request you answer by hand costs staff time you have already agreed to spend; a portal turns that cost into a one-off setup.

## Proxy access is the most requested and the most delicate

In the same US survey, access by a caregiver or representative to somebody else's record went from 24 percent in 2020 to 51 percent in 2024. It doubled in four years, and in a dental practice it is the request that reaches the desk most often: parents with children, adult children with ageing parents.

- **Delegated access, never a shared login.** A parent signing in as themselves and seeing their child's chart leaves a record of who looked at what. Two people sharing one password leaves nothing.
- **Give every delegation an expiry date.** A child has a birthday, and the delegation should lapse on its own rather than when somebody remembers.
- **Decide the teenager rule before you need it.** It is the awkward conversation, and it goes better written down than improvised at the desk.
- **Log every delegated access.** If a family disagreement ever arrives, the log is the only thing that settles it.

## What to leave switched off

- **Notes written for clinicians.** If your software cannot separate a clinical shorthand entry from what the patient sees, that is a question for the vendor before you sign.
- **Findings you have not explained yet.** Publishing an image or a report the patient reads before you talk them through it turns a consultation into a late-night phone call.
- **An open message channel with no hours.** If you open a channel, somebody has to read it; a message about pain that nobody sees for three days is worse than no channel at all.
- **Another family member's data**, absent an explicit delegation, even when they share a phone number, a surname and a payment card.
- **Any editable field over the clinical record.** Patients update their contact details; the clinical entry belongs to whoever signed it.

## How to turn one on without creating work

1. **Count a week of phone calls** and sort them into five piles: appointments, details, treatment plans, payments and record copies. The tallest pile is the first feature you switch on.
2. **Switch on one feature** and leave it a month. A portal with five features on day one produces five kinds of confusion at once.
3. **Write the checkout sentence.** One sentence, the same for the whole team, said as the patient leaves. That is the piece worth thirty points of adoption.
4. **Decide what of the record is visible** and check it on a test patient, in the portal itself, before a real one does.
5. **Define delegated access**: who can request it, how it is verified, when it expires and where it is logged.
6. **Sign the data processing agreement** with the vendor if the portal is a third party's, and establish where the data is processed and stored.
7. **Name the portal in your privacy notice**, including the company that operates it.
8. **Measure at three months**: the share of active patients who have logged in at least once, and the size of the call pile you picked. If adoption is under 20 percent, the problem is step 3, not the software.

Always keep a route that is not the portal. The seventy-eight-year-old who has been coming for fifteen years is not installing anything, and has exactly the same rights as everyone else.
