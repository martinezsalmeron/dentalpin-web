The price of a dental practice is two numbers added together: the current value of the equipment and fit-out, and goodwill, meaning the part of the patient base and reputation that keeps working once the current owner has gone. Goodwill is derived from sustainable future profit, after deducting a notional salary for the owner's own clinical work, over a short horizon of two to five years.

There is no official multiplier and no formula nobody can argue with. What follows is how the number is actually built, what published data exists, and what a buyer will ask for once a lender wants the price justified.

## The two numbers

**Tangible value** is the sum of the practice's assets at today's value: chairs, imaging, cabinetry, IT. The asset register is the starting point, but it usually understates the position, because tax depreciation periods are shorter than the real working life of an operatory.

**Goodwill** is everything else: active patients, location, team, lease terms, reputation. It is not estimated by feel. It is derived from the practice's ability to keep producing profit once the current owner is no longer behind the chair.

> **What a buyer pays for is the profit that survives the handover.** The share of the patient base that came for the departing dentist is not transferable, and it is the first thing a buyer deducts.

## The one dental dataset that is actually published

Most of the percentages quoted in practice sales come from brokers and consolidators, who are party to the transaction. That is not a source.

Germany is the exception. Since 1984 the Institute of German Dentists has published the *InvestMonitor Zahnarztpraxis* jointly with apoBank, built from real financing records of dentists setting up in practice. The 2024 edition, published on 24 October 2025, covers 306 single-practice acquisitions.

| Component | 2023 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Goodwill | EUR 171,000 | EUR 153,000 |
| Tangible value | EUR 76,000 | EUR 73,000 |
| Purchase price | EUR 247,000 | EUR 226,000 |
| Total capital required | EUR 463,000 | EUR 450,000 |

Three things in that table travel to any market. Goodwill was 68 per cent of the purchase price and tangible assets 32 per cent, a split that repeats year after year. The purchase price was only half the money the buyer actually needed, because refurbishment, new equipment and working capital come after it. And the spread is wide: one practice in five sold for under EUR 100,000, and 73 per cent for under EUR 300,000.

Location is priced in, measurably. In large cities the average purchase price was EUR 243,000, which is 31 per cent above the EUR 186,000 paid in rural areas.

> **It is a sample, and it is German.** The report says so itself: 538 financing cases from a single bank, against 1,122 dentists who set up in Germany that year, which it states allows only limited general conclusions. Use it for the structure of a deal, not as a price list.

## Three methods, and only one survives scrutiny

| Method | How it works | Stands up to challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage of revenue | A percentage of average revenue over recent years | ✗ Two practices with identical revenue and different costs come out the same |
| Professional body formula | Average profit minus a notional owner's salary, times a fixed factor | ~ Gives a reference point; its own publishers say it is not a valuation |
| Modified earnings value method | Normalised future profit, less owner's salary and tax, discounted over two to five years, plus tangible value | ✓ The method expert witnesses use |

**Percentage of revenue** is popular because it takes thirty seconds. It also answers the wrong question: revenue says nothing about the profit a buyer has to service debt from.

**The professional body formula**, in the German chambers' 2008 version, at least starts from profit rather than revenue, deducts a notional practitioner's salary and multiplies. It is still a fixed rule applied to a specific practice.

**The modified earnings value method** is what gets used when real money moves. Recent accounts are normalised to strip out one-off items, the expected surplus is projected forward, the owner's salary and tax are deducted, and the remainder is discounted at a rate that reflects risk. The horizon is deliberately short, two to five years, because inherited goodwill either decays or becomes the buyer's own in that time.

## Why the revenue percentage falls over

The objection is arithmetic before it is legal. A practice billing GBP 400,000 at a 25 per cent margin and one billing the same at 8 per cent are not worth the same money, and a percentage of revenue cannot tell them apart.

German courts, where this has been litigated hardest, reached the same conclusion. In its judgment of 9 February 2011 (case XII ZR 40/09) the Federal Court of Justice held that the goodwill of a professional practice is an intangible asset that must in principle be counted, that an owner's salary measured against that owner's individual circumstances must be deducted when valuing it, and that valuation on revenue alone is ruled out because revenue permits no safe conclusions about expected profit.

Read it for what it is: German law, decided in a matrimonial finance case, not a rule in any English-speaking jurisdiction. The reasoning is what transfers.

The authors of the formula method qualify it themselves. Presenting the revised guidance in the Deutsches Ärzteblatt, the lawyer from the joint legal department of the German Medical Association and the National Association of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians wrote that the guidance can "only give reference points and does not constitute a basis for a conclusive valuation in an individual case".

## What moves the number

- **How much of production depends on one person.** If the owner produces 70 per cent and leaves, the buyer is purchasing rooms with chairs in them. Several clinicians and a spread patient base are worth more at the same revenue.
- **The lease.** Years remaining, rent, whether it can be assigned. Ten secured years are part of the price; fourteen months remaining discounts it without anyone arguing.
- **The age of the equipment.** Not the book value, but what the buyer has to spend in year one. In the German data that follow-on spend is as large as the purchase price itself.
- **The private versus third-party mix.** Predictable, repeatable work with margin outweighs revenue that depends on one contract or one insurer's fee schedule.
- **The schedule for the next few months.** Booked appointments and treatment in progress are near-certain profit, and they get looked at one by one.
- **A recall system that works.** A patient base under active recall generates demand without advertising. A list nobody has contacted in three years is a list, not a patient base.
- **Outstanding patient debt.** It enters the negotiation, almost always discounted and broken down by age.

## The figures a buyer will ask for

A valuation is defended with reports, not impressions. This is what a lender-backed buyer ends up requesting, and it should come out of the practice software without anyone building a spreadsheet by hand.

1. **Active patients**, with the definition written down: seen in the last twelve months, or twenty-four, but the same definition in every figure.
2. **New patients per month** over three years, to show whether the base is growing or being consumed.
3. **Recall compliance**: how many patients invited for review actually attend, and how many are overdue.
4. **Production per chair and per clinical hour**, which is the real measure of whether installed capacity is used.
5. **Treatment mix** and its share of revenue, separating recurring work from one-off cases.
6. **Treatment plan acceptance rate** and the average value accepted.
7. **Outstanding patient debt** by age, including open payment plans.
8. **Failure-to-attend and cancellation rates** against total appointments.
9. **Three years of accounts**, with non-recurring costs identified so they can be normalised out.

## What does not transfer with the practice

Clinical records are not an asset of the sale. Handing them to the buyer is not settled by a clause in the contract, and the seller remains responsible for keeping them while the retention period runs. [What happens to patient records when a practice is sold](/en/blog/dental-practice-sale-patient-records/) sets out the mechanics, and it belongs before the price is agreed, not after.

Money already taken for work not yet done does not transfer either. Deposits against unfinished treatment are an obligation someone has to discharge, and they appear in the negotiation as a liability rather than as cash.

## Before anything is signed

A practice value is not created on valuation day. It is the result of two or three years of being able to evidence what the practice does. A seller three years out has room to improve the number; a seller going from March to June sells what is there.
