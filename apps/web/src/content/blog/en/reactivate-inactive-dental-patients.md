Reactivation is not one message to your whole database. It is four separate lists, worked in order, starting with the smallest and warmest: patients who accepted a treatment plan and never finished it. Before anything goes out, two checks matter. Is this person still a patient of the practice, and are you allowed to use their contact details for this, which is not the same question as whether you are required to keep their record.

What sinks these campaigns is rarely the wording. It is one generic message to a thousand contacts, of whom two hundred already have an appointment booked, thirty asked never to be contacted, and a handful left over something that was never resolved.

## Inactive is two of their own intervals, not twelve months

A patient on a twenty-four month recall interval is not inactive after a year. A periodontal patient reviewed every three months has been gone a long while by the time a year passes. One fixed cut-off for everyone produces a list full of people who are fine and missing the ones who are not.

NICE guideline CG19 is the most explicit published reference on this, and it is unambiguous: the interval "should be determined specifically for each patient and tailored to meet his or her needs, on the basis of an assessment of disease levels and risk of or from dental disease". The shortest interval is three months, the longest twenty-four for adults and twelve for under-18s.

That gives a definition you can actually query: **inactive means more than two of that patient's own intervals with no visit and no future appointment**. The twenty-four month patient qualifies at four years, the three-month patient at six months, which is the right answer in both cases.

If the interval is not stored per patient, that query cannot be run. The practice falls back on "hasn't been in for over a year", and that number mixes people who are perfectly on track with people who have been gone for three years.

## Before you send: what the marketing rules actually require

This is where win-back campaigns get practices into trouble. Keeping a clinical record is a legal obligation. Using that person's phone number or email to invite them back is a separate activity with its own rules, and the retention duty says nothing about it.

In the UK, the relevant rule is regulation 22 of the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations 2003. Unsolicited direct marketing by electronic mail needs prior consent, with a narrow exception for existing customers: the details were obtained during a sale or negotiations for a sale, the marketing covers only your own similar products and services, and a simple means of refusal was offered when the details were collected and in every message since.

In the United States, CAN-SPAM applies to commercial email. The FTC requires accurate headers and subject lines, a valid physical postal address in the message, a clear explanation of how to opt out, and opt-out requests honoured within ten business days. Transactional and relationship messages, such as confirming an appointment the patient already booked, are treated differently from commercial ones.

> **A duty to retain records is not permission to market to the person in them.** They are two different processing activities, decided by two different sets of rules, and conflating them is the single most common mistake in a reactivation campaign.

Three practical consequences, all of which also make the campaign work better:

- **Put the opt-out in every message.** Both regimes require it, and a visible opt-out costs you far less than a complaint.
- **Objections to direct marketing are absolute.** Under Article 21(2) of the UK GDPR and the GDPR, a person can object at any time and there is no balancing test to apply. You stop, and you record that you stopped.
- **Stay inside what they came to you for.** The existing-customer exception is about similar services. An overdue check-up or an unfinished treatment fits comfortably; a cosmetic dentistry promotion sent to someone who came once for an emergency does not.

This is not legal advice, and the rules differ by country. If the campaign is large, or you are adding a channel you have not used before, run it past whoever advises the practice on data protection.

## Four lists, four different conversations

The expensive mistake is treating someone who left a root canal unfinished the same as someone who came once, four years ago. The first list is worth many times more per contact and needs a completely different message.

| Segment | What they have in common | Campaign? |
|---|---|---|
| Accepted plan, unfinished | They already said yes and something clinical is open | ✓ First, and by phone |
| Accepted quote, never started | They decided and never made the first appointment | ✓ Yes, referencing the quote |
| Recall overdue by two intervals | Nothing open, just elapsed time | ✓ Yes, short message |
| Left after a complaint | A grievance, a refund, a visit that went badly | ✗ Never in a campaign |
| Deceased, opted out, bounced | Should not be contacted at all | ✗ Off every list |

1. **Accepted plan, unfinished.** Work this one by phone, person by person. It returns more booked chair time per call than anything else you will do, and the reason they stopped is usually specific: money, fear, or a schedule that changed.
2. **Accepted quote, never started.** A written message works here, but it has to name the treatment that was discussed. A generic nudge throws away the one advantage you have, which is knowing what they wanted.
3. **Recall overdue.** The big list and the lowest response rate per message. Worth a short message once or twice a year, and no more than that.
4. **Left after a complaint.** These never go in a batch. If someone calls them, it is to talk about what happened, and it is someone who can actually fix it.

> **An unfinished treatment is a clinical problem before it is a commercial one.** If a patient has had a root canal sitting without its crown for two years, that is the reason to call, and the message should sound like it.

## Cleaning the list is half the work

A dirty list does not just perform badly. It generates opt-outs, complaints and at least one deeply awkward phone call. Half an hour of cleaning before each batch prevents all three.

1. **Remove anyone who already has an appointment** booked. This filter alone prevents the most irritating message you can send.
2. **Remove anyone who asked not to be contacted**, and check that the flag lives in the record rather than in someone's memory.
3. **Check for deaths and departures** with the front desk team before anything goes out.
4. **Merge duplicates**, or the same person gets two messages addressed to two slightly different names.
5. **Drop contacts that bounced last time**, and record that they bounced.
6. **Pull out anyone with an open complaint** so they can never fall into a batch by accident.
7. **Cap the first batch** at fifty to a hundred, so the practice can actually answer what comes back.

Step seven is the one almost nobody does. A thousand messages on a Monday morning ties up reception for three days, and half the people who reply never get through.

## What the message says, and what the evidence really supports

There is evidence on channel, and it needs reading carefully. A 2013 Cochrane review of eight randomised trials and 6,615 participants compared SMS reminders with no reminder and with phone calls.

Text messages improved attendance over no reminder, with a risk ratio of 1.14 and attendance rising from 67.8% to 78.6%. Against phone calls they were equivalent, at a risk ratio of 0.99, and cost between 55% and 65% less. The review rates the evidence low to moderate quality.

> **That is evidence about reminding someone of an appointment they already have, not about persuading them to book one.** Reactivation is the harder case, and nothing of comparable quality supports it. What does carry over is the comparison between channels.

Given that, what the message needs:

- **The practice name in the first line.** Someone who does not have you saved has no idea who is writing, and an unidentified message gets deleted.
- **The specific fact that justifies the contact.** "The crown on your lower right molar is still outstanding" works. "We haven't seen you in a while" is noise.
- **One action, not two.** Reply to this message, a link, or a number someone actually answers. Offering two routes reduces the response to both.
- **No invented urgency.** Discounts that expire on Friday perform badly in healthcare and leave a bad impression that lasts.
- **A visible way out.** How to stop hearing from you. Losing a contact is cheaper than earning a complaint.

Send once, then once more ten to fourteen days later to anyone who did not reply. Then stop until next year.

## Four numbers, and one nobody looks at

| Metric | How it is calculated | The decision it drives |
|---|---|---|
| Reachability | Valid contacts as a share of the list | Below 70%, your problem is data quality, not copy |
| Response rate | Replies per message delivered | Compares segments and templates, batch by batch |
| Appointments gained | Booked and attended, per batch | The only number that finally counts |
| Opt-outs generated | Requests to stop contact, per batch | Rising means poor segmentation or too much repetition |

The last one is the one nobody looks at. A batch that produces fifteen appointments and forty opt-outs was expensive: those forty contacts are now unavailable for ordinary recall, which is what keeps the schedule full for the rest of the year.

Measure one more thing over time: how many reactivated patients are still attending twelve months later. Bringing someone back for a single visit is not bringing them back.

## Where to start this week

1. **Count how many active patients have a recall interval stored.** That number decides whether you can segment properly or are stuck with "over a year".
2. **Pull only the accepted-but-unfinished plans first.** The list is usually surprisingly short and surprisingly valuable.
3. **Clean it** with the seven steps above.
4. **Call the first ten yourself** before writing to anyone. Ten calls will tell you why people left, and that rewrites the message.
5. **Write two templates**, one for accepted quotes and one for overdue recalls.
6. **Send a batch of fifty** and record reachability, replies, appointments and opt-outs.
7. **Change one thing** in the next batch, so you know which change moved the number.
